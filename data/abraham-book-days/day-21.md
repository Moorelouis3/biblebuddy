Twenty five years.

That is how long it has been since a seventy five year old man walked out of Haran with a promise and no map.

Twenty five years of altars and famines and lies and wars and shortcuts and a covenant cut in the dark.

Twenty five years of a tent with no baby in it.

Yesterday Abraham was on his knees praying that another man’s household could have children.

Today the promise finally lands in his own.

A ninety year old woman goes into labor, and a hundred year old man holds his son.

If this were a movie, that is where the credits would roll. But the Bible does not stop at the happy ending.

It keeps the camera running until the baby is a toddler, the household is fighting again, and Abraham has to do the hardest thing a father can do.

Send one son away so the other one can grow up in peace.

Then the camera leaves Abraham entirely and follows a mother and her teenage boy into the desert with one skin of water.

**Today God keeps His word, and it still costs something.**

# The Birth of Isaac

> “And Sarah said, God hath made me to laugh, so that all that hear will laugh with me.” (Genesis 21:6)

Yesterday ended with Abraham praying for Abimelech’s house to have babies. Here is the very next thing the Bible says:

> “And the LORD visited Sarah as he had said, and the LORD did unto Sarah as he had spoken. For Sarah conceived, and bare Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him.” (Genesis 21:1–2)

Read those two verses slowly, because the Bible is being deliberate. *As he had said*. *As he had spoken*.

*At the set time of which God had spoken*. Three times in two verses, the point is the same.

God did exactly what He said He would do, exactly when He said He would do it.

**Visited** here means God came through for her. He showed up and did the thing.

Think about everything that stood between the promise and this verse. A famine. A lie in Egypt. A split with Lot. A war.

A shortcut named Hagar. Thirteen years with no word. A laugh behind a tent door. A second lie in Gerar.

And through all of it, the promise did not move. Sarah conceived. Sarah bore a son. The tent is not quiet anymore.

> “And Abraham called the name of his son that was born unto him, whom Sarah bare to him, Isaac. And Abraham circumcised his son Isaac being eight days old, as God had commanded him. And Abraham was an hundred years old, when his son Isaac was born unto him.” (Genesis 21:3–5)

Three quiet acts of obedience in three verses. God said name him Isaac back on Day 14, and Abraham names him Isaac.

God said circumcise every boy on the eighth day, and Abraham does it on the eighth day.

No arguing, no improving on the instructions. And then the number. A hundred years old.

He was ninety nine when God gave the date. The date came.

Do the math with me one more time, because it matters. Seventy five when he left Haran. A hundred when he holds Isaac.

Twenty five years of waiting. God did not lie, and God did not hurry.

## God Hath Made Me to Laugh

> “And Sarah said, God hath made me to laugh, so that all that hear will laugh with me. And she said, Who would have said unto Abraham, that Sarah should have given children suck? for I have born him a son in his old age.” (Genesis 21:6–7)

Now Sarah laughs again. And put this laugh next to the one on Day 15.

Behind the tent door, she laughed because the whole idea was ridiculous. A ninety year old woman with a baby.

Now she is holding the baby, and she laughs again, but it is a different sound.

This is the laugh of a woman who cannot believe her own life. God, You are good. Look what You did.

And she says everybody who hears about it is going to laugh right along with her, because the news is that wonderful and that unbelievable.

That is why the boy’s name is Isaac. We learned on Day 14 that **Isaac** means he laughs. His name carries both laughs.

The one in the tent door that did not believe it, and the one in the nursery that could not get over it.

And catch the second thing she says. *Given children suck* means breastfed.

Who would ever have told Abraham that Sarah would be nursing a baby? Not just having one.

Feeding one, at her breast, at ninety. Picture her saying it with the child in her arms.

The thing she had given up on so completely that she handed her servant to her husband is now latched onto her and feeding.

One small note, since you are reading the King James.

Some of the words in these verses are printed in italics, like *so that* in verse 6. That is not emphasis.

The translators put words in italics when they added an English word to make the sentence read smoothly.

It is their way of being honest about what is in the Hebrew and what they supplied. You will see it all over your Bible.

## A Great Feast

> “And the child grew, and was weaned: and Abraham made a great feast the same day that Isaac was weaned.” (Genesis 21:8)

Now time jumps. **Weaned** means the child stopped nursing and moved to regular food.

In that world, that happened late, usually somewhere around the age of three.

So picture Isaac as a little boy now, walking, talking, running around the camp. Years have passed since the birth.

Do not read this chapter like everything happened in one week.

And why a great feast for that? Because in that world, a lot of babies did not make it to three.

Getting a child past nursing and onto solid food meant he had survived the most dangerous years of his life.

For any family, that was worth celebrating.

For a hundred and three year old man and his ninety three year old wife, who waited twenty five years for this boy, it was worth throwing the biggest party the camp had ever seen.

And at that party, Sarah sees something:

> “And Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, which she had born unto Abraham, mocking. Wherefore she said unto Abraham, Cast out this bondwoman and her son: for the son of this bondwoman shall not be heir with my son, even with Isaac.” (Genesis 21:9–10)

The son of Hagar the Egyptian is Ishmael. And do the math on him too.

Abraham was eighty six when Ishmael was born and a hundred when Isaac was born. Fourteen years apart.

If Isaac is about three, Ishmael is about sixteen or seventeen. This is not two toddlers in a sandbox.

This is a teenager and a little boy.

And Sarah sees him **mocking**. Different Bibles translate that word differently. If you read the ESV, it says laughing.

The King James says mocking.

The idea is that Ishmael was laughing at Isaac, making fun of him, ridiculing the little boy at his own party.

Feel the twist in that. Two verses ago, Sarah was laughing with joy over Isaac. Now a teenager is laughing at him.

Same kind of word, completely different sound.

And Sarah snaps. Look at how she talks. She does not say Hagar. She does not say Ishmael. *This bondwoman and her son*.

Go back to Day 13. This is the same household fight, fifteen years later. Sarah handed Hagar to Abraham.

Hagar got pregnant and looked down on Sarah. Sarah treated her harshly and Hagar ran.

The angel sent her back, and the problem never went away. It just waited.

Now the covenant son is here, and Sarah says it out loud. That boy is not going to split the inheritance with mine.

Get them out.

## Because of His Son

> “And the thing was very grievous in Abraham’s sight because of his son.” (Genesis 21:11)

Stop on this verse, because this is the heart of the day. **Grievous** means deeply painful. Distressing.

This tore Abraham up. And read why. *Because of his son*. Not because Sarah was angry. Not because the party got ruined.

Because Ishmael is his boy.

Remember what we said on Day 14. For thirteen years, Ishmael was Abraham’s only son.

He taught him to ride, to herd, to fight.

When God said Sarah would have a son, the first words out of Abraham’s mouth were, O that Ishmael might live before thee.

He loves this kid. He has loved him for sixteen or seventeen years.

And now his wife is telling him to put that kid out of the house.

> “And God said unto Abraham, Let it not be grievous in thy sight because of the lad, and because of thy bondwoman; in all that Sarah hath said unto thee, hearken unto her voice; for in Isaac shall thy seed be called. And also of the son of the bondwoman will I make a nation, because he is thy seed.” (Genesis 21:12–13)

God steps in, and listen to what He does and does not say. He says do what Sarah asked, in this matter.

**Hearken unto her voice** means listen to her and do it.

That is not God saying Sarah has been right about everything since Day 13.

It is God saying, in this specific thing, let them go.

Because the covenant line, the line that runs through Day 12 and Day 14 and all the way to Jesus, runs through Isaac.

*In Isaac shall thy seed be called*. Isaac is the one the promise goes through.

But then God says something about Ishmael that He wants Abraham to hear. *Because he is thy seed*. He is your son too.

I have not forgotten that. I am going to make a nation out of him. Hear what God is really telling a grieving father.

I will take care of him. You can let him go, because I am not letting him go.

That is what makes this obedience possible, and it is still painful.

God did not promise Abraham that Hagar and Ishmael would have an easy road. He promised them a future.

Abraham has to send his son into the desert trusting that the God who made the promise will keep it out there, where Abraham cannot see.

## Early in the Morning

> “And Abraham rose up early in the morning, and took bread, and a bottle of water, and gave it unto Hagar, putting it on her shoulder, and the child, and sent her away: and she departed, and wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba.” (Genesis 21:14)

Rose up early in the morning. Remember that phrase, because you are going to see it again tomorrow and the day after.

When God tells Abraham to do something hard, he does not sleep on it. He gets up at first light and does it.

Hold on to that about him. It does not mean it did not hurt. It means he obeyed while it hurt.

He packs provisions. **Bread** here means food for the road, and a **bottle** is not glass or plastic.

It is a water skin, an animal hide sewn up to carry water. He puts it on Hagar’s shoulder, and he sends them away.

Picture that morning. A father handing his sixteen year old son food and water and watching him walk out of the camp.

The Bible does not give us one word Abraham said. Just that he did it.

And Hagar wanders in the wilderness of **Beersheba**.

That is the dry southern edge of the land, open scrub country with hardly any water.

Keep the name in your head. Tomorrow we find out where it came from.

The Bible is using the name people knew later to tell us where she was.

Put this next to Day 13.

The first time Hagar left this camp, she was pregnant, running on her own, on the road toward Egypt.

This time she is not running. She has been sent. And she is not carrying a baby in her belly.

She is walking beside a teenage son.

## The Voice of the Lad

> “And the water was spent in the bottle, and she cast the child under one of the shrubs. And she went, and sat her down over against him a good way off, as it were a bowshot: for she said, Let me not see the death of the child. And she sat over against him, and lift up her voice, and wept.” (Genesis 21:15–16)

Slow down here. This is the scene where the music gets quiet.

The water runs out. In that country, that is a death sentence. There is nothing in sight.

Her son, a teenager, is too weak to keep walking.

She gets him into the little bit of shade under a desert shrub and leaves him there.

*Cast* does not mean she threw a baby down.

It means she set her collapsing son under the bush because she could not carry him any farther.

Then she walks away. A **bowshot**, as far as an arrow flies, far enough that she cannot see his face.

And she tells you why. *Let me not see the death of the child*. She cannot watch her son die.

So she sits down in the dirt, facing him from a distance, and she *lift up her voice, and wept*.

That is not a quiet tear. That is a mother wailing out loud in an empty desert.

Here is the tension of the scene. You know the promise.

God told Abraham that Ishmael would become a nation, which means Ishmael cannot die under that bush.

Hagar does not know that conversation happened. From where she is sitting, her son is dying and nobody is coming.

> “And God heard the voice of the lad; and the angel of God called to Hagar out of heaven, and said unto her, What aileth thee, Hagar? fear not; for God hath heard the voice of the lad where he is.” (Genesis 21:17)

Hagar is the one weeping out loud. But look at whose voice God says He heard. *The voice of the lad*. The boy.

And remember the boy’s name. **Ishmael** means God hears.

The angel gave Hagar that name at the well on Day 13, because the LORD had heard her suffering.

Now the boy named God Hears is dying under a bush, and God hears him. His name is coming true in the dirt.

And God calls Hagar by name again. Just like the first time. *What aileth thee* means what is wrong, Hagar? Fear not.

God heard him, *where he is*. Not back in Abraham’s camp. Not in the covenant line.

Under that bush, in that desert, outside the family. God’s care went out there with them.

You will notice this messenger is called *the angel of God* here, and on Day 13 it was *the angel of the LORD*.

Same family, same kind of messenger, same God sending His word and His rescue into the desert.

The Bible does not give this messenger a personal name, and we do not need one.

What matters is that God keeps showing up for Hagar.

## A Well of Water

> “Arise, lift up the lad, and hold him in thine hand; for I will make him a great nation. And God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water; and she went, and filled the bottle with water, and gave the lad drink.” (Genesis 21:18–19)

Get up. Go to your son.

*Lift up the lad, and hold him in thine hand* means get him up and support him, get under his arm, hold him steady.

And then the promise, said straight to her this time. I will make him a great nation.

She heard that at the well sixteen years ago. Now she hears it again, with the boy dying in front of her.

Then God opens her eyes, and she sees a well. Hear what the Bible is saying. The well was there.

She just could not see it, because grief and fear had closed her eyes to everything but the shrub and the dying boy.

God did not drop a well out of the sky. He opened her eyes to what He had already put there.

And she fills the same skin Abraham gave her and gives her son a drink.

Here is something to carry with you from this scene. Look at the two kinds of water in the story.

Abraham gave Hagar a water skin. It was a real gift, from a father who cared, and it got them part of the way.

But what a man carries runs out. Then God showed her a well. A well does not run out.

You can fill the same skin from it again and again. Man’s provision lasts a little while. God’s provision is a source.

The skin is not worthless. It just is not the well.

> “And God was with the lad; and he grew, and dwelt in the wilderness, and became an archer. And he dwelt in the wilderness of Paran: and his mother took him a wife out of the land of Egypt.” (Genesis 21:20–21)

*God was with the lad*. Not for one drink. As he grew.

God did not pull Ishmael out from under a bush and then forget about him. He stayed with him.

The boy grows up in the wilderness and becomes an **archer**, a man skilled with a bow, which is exactly what you would need to feed yourself in open desert country.

He makes a life in **Paran**, the desert region south of the land, down toward Sinai.

And notice the last line, because it is not where Day 13 left her. Back then, the angel sent Hagar back to Sarah.

This time, nobody sends her back.

Ishmael grows up in the wilderness, and his mother finds him a wife from Egypt, her own homeland.

The Egyptian servant girl finally gets to go back toward Egypt, in a way, through her son’s marriage.

That line raises something worth thinking about.

An Egyptian wife means an Egyptian household, with Egypt’s gods and Egypt’s ways in the tent.

It is fair to wonder what that did to Ishmael’s children and grandchildren over time.

That is a real question about how a spouse and a family culture shape what a household worships.

But do not let it cancel what the verse before it just said. God was with the lad.

The Bible wants you to leave Ishmael with that.

And do not say Abraham never saw him again.

At the very end of this book, when Abraham dies, Isaac and Ishmael bury him together.

The desert was not the end of their story. It was the end of Ishmael living in his father’s tent.

## Letting Go of Ishmael

So step back and look at the whole day. God kept His word.

A hundred year old man and a ninety year old woman got the son He promised, at the set time, and Sarah laughed the best laugh of her life.

And then God asked that same man to let go of his other son. Abraham had loved Ishmael for sixteen years.

He got up early and did it anyway, trusting that God would be in the desert with the boy. And God was.

Hold on to this, because it is going to matter in two days.

Before God ever asks Abraham to put Isaac on an altar, Abraham has already had to open his hands and let a son walk away.

Letting go of Ishmael was practice for something harder.

God was teaching this man, one painful morning at a time, that his sons were never really his to keep.

They were his to love and trust to God.

Tomorrow, the king from Day 20 shows up at Abraham’s camp with his army commander, and he has noticed something about Abraham that he cannot explain.

But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 21

The first two verses say the same thing twice. As He had said. As He had spoken. Let that repetition land.

This is the whole book coming true.

Sarah laughs again in this chapter. Compare it to the laugh behind the tent door on Day 15.

Watch what happens to the water in verse 15, and what happens to Hagar’s eyes in verse 19.

**As you read, notice:**

* 👴 How old Abraham is when Isaac is born
* 👀 What Sarah sees at the feast, and what she demands
* 💔 How the Bible describes Abraham’s feelings in verse 11
* 👂 Whose voice God hears in the wilderness

And think about this question: **How does a father send his own son into the desert?**

## Reflection Questions

1. **What did God say He would do that you have quietly stopped expecting?** Sarah waited twenty five years and then laughed at how good He is. Name the promise, and remember the set time is His, not yours.
2. **What are you holding on to that God is asking you to open your hands and release to Him?** Abraham loved Ishmael for sixteen years and still got up early to let him go. Letting go is not the same as not loving.
3. **Where has your water skin run out, and where might God be trying to open your eyes to a well that was already there?** Hagar could not see past the shrub. Ask God to show you what He has already provided.

## Today's One Thing

**Open Your Hands**

Abraham had to release a son he loved and trust God with him in the desert. Practice that today with something smaller.

1. Write down one person or one outcome you have been gripping, trying to control, because you are afraid of what happens if you let go.
2. Pray over it out loud, with your hands open in front of you, and say the words: God, You will be with them where they are.
3. Take one concrete step back from controlling it today. Do not send the message. Do not fix it for them. Let God have the desert.

One grip named, one prayer with open hands, one step back. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You did for Sarah as You had spoken, at the set time. Twenty five years, and You did not forget one word.

Thank You that Your promises do not expire while I wait on them.

Make me laugh the way Sarah laughed.

Not the laugh that does not believe You, but the laugh that cannot get over how good You are.

**And teach me to open my hands, Lord, even when it is grievous.**

There are people I am holding too tight. Outcomes I am trying to control because I am afraid of the desert.

Abraham got up early and let his son go because You said You would make him a nation.

Help me trust You with the people I cannot follow into the wilderness.

And when my water runs out, open my eyes. You have already dug wells I cannot see because I am staring at the shrub.

Show me the source, not just the skin.

Thank You for being the God who hears the boy under the bush. You heard Ishmael where he was. You hear me where I am.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
