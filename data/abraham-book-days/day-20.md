We are leaving the cave today and climbing back up the hill to Abraham. And I have to be honest with you, I expected better from him.

This is the man who ran to feed three strangers. The man who stood before God and argued for a city. The man God let in on His plans before He judged Sodom. He has a promise with a date on it. This time next year, Sarah holds a son. And he is still living in the land God said was his.

Then he packs up the camp, moves south, and does the exact thing he did in Egypt almost twenty five years ago. He tells a king that his wife is his sister. And the king takes her.

Same lie. Same fear. Same wife put in danger. Except this time the baby God promised is supposed to come through that wife within the year.

That is what makes today so important. Abraham is not a beginner anymore. He has walked with God for a quarter of a century. And the old fear is still in him, waiting for the right moment to run the old play. If that is true of Abraham, it is true of you and me. Today we find out what God does about it.

**Today the old fear comes back, and God protects the promise anyway.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 20

Keep Day 9 in your head. The Egypt story is about to happen again, and the Bible wants you to feel the repeat.

Watch who God talks to in this chapter. It is not Abraham.

Verse 7 uses a word we have not seen yet in the Bible. Mark it when you get there.

**As you read, notice:**

* How Abimelech defends himself to God, and what God says back
* The reason Abraham gives for the lie in verse 11
* The family detail Abraham reveals in verse 12
* What was wrong in Abimelech’s household, and what fixed it

And think about this question: **Why does God still call this man a prophet?**

> “Now therefore restore the man his wife; for he is a prophet, and he shall pray for thee, and thou shalt live.” (Genesis 20:7)

# Abraham and Abimelech

Yesterday ended in a cave in the hills with Lot. Today the Bible leaves him there for good and goes back to Abraham, who is on the move:

> “And Abraham journeyed from thence toward the south country, and dwelled between Kadesh and Shur, and sojourned in Gerar.” (Genesis 20:1)

Get your map out. Abraham has been camped at Mamre, by Hebron, in the hill country. Now he heads for the **south country**, which is the Negev, the dry stretch of southern Canaan that runs down toward Egypt. **Kadesh** is an oasis area at the far southern edge of the land. **Shur** is the desert frontier on the way to Egypt. We have heard that name before. It is the road Hagar was running down when God found her at the well. And **Gerar** is a town and the territory around it in the southwest, down past where Gaza is.

So he is still inside the land God promised him. He has not left. He has gone to the southern edge of it. And the word **sojourned** means he is living there as a temporary resident, an outsider under somebody else’s rule. That somebody is a king named Abimelech. Remember from Day 11 that Canaan was not one country with one king. It was a patchwork of little kingdoms and city states. Abimelech is king of Gerar, not king of the world.

Why did he move? He had a home at Mamre. Think about what Abraham is responsible for. Hundreds of people. Thousands of animals. All of them need grass and water every single day, and after a while a place gets eaten down. A household that size has to keep moving to keep eating. The valley to the east just burned to ash, so it is not hard to see him turning south toward fresh pasture. That is the practical reason. Hold on to it, because the practical reason is about to run straight into an old problem.

> “And Abraham said of Sarah his wife, She is my sister: and Abimelech king of Gerar sent, and took Sarah.” (Genesis 20:2)

There it is. One verse. No warning, no buildup. Abraham tells the people of Gerar that Sarah is his sister, and the king sends for her and takes her into his house.

If you did Day 9, you are already feeling it. This is Egypt all over again. Same lie, same result, a king takes Sarah. Twenty five years ago Abraham did this to a seventy five year old wife. Now he does it to a ninety year old wife who is supposed to have the promised baby within the year. The clock God set on Day 14 is ticking, and Abraham just put Sarah in another man’s house.

Now look at who God goes to:

> “But God came to Abimelech in a dream by night, and said to him, Behold, thou art but a dead man, for the woman which thou hast taken; for she is a man’s wife.” (Genesis 20:3)

God does not speak to Abraham in this chapter. Not once. He goes to the pagan king in a dream and tells him, you are a dead man. The woman you took is married. Picture Abimelech waking up from that one.

> “But Abimelech had not come near her: and he said, Lord, wilt thou slay also a righteous nation? Said he not unto me, She is my sister? and she, even she herself said, He is my brother: in the integrity of my heart and innocency of my hands have I done this.” (Genesis 20:4–5)

First, the detail that matters most. *Abimelech had not come near her*. He has not touched Sarah. Keep that in your pocket, because the whole promise about Isaac depends on it.

Then listen to the king defend himself. Lord, will you kill innocent people? He told me she was his sister. And look at this. *She, even she herself said, He is my brother*. Sarah said it too. Both of them. The king is not making that up. **Integrity** means a clean heart, no bad intent.

**Innocency of my hands** means I did nothing wrong with what I did. He acted on what two people told him.

Stop on Sarah for a second, because this is where I have to say what I see. Abraham did not do this alone. Sarah backed the story. When the king asked, she said he is my brother. That is two people running the same play together. Call it what it looks like. A couple working a con on a king. Not for money. For safety. Abraham was afraid he would be killed, and the two of them had an arrangement to protect him, and Sarah was the one who paid for it. We are going to find out in a few verses just how long this arrangement had been running.

> “And God said unto him in a dream, Yea, I know that thou didst this in the integrity of thy heart; for I also withheld thee from sinning against me: therefore suffered I thee not to touch her.” (Genesis 20:6)

God agrees with him. Yes, I know you did this with a clean heart. And then God tells him something he did not know. *I also withheld thee*. I am the one who kept you from touching her. Abimelech thought he just had not gotten around to it. God says, no, that was Me. I held you back.

Hear what is really going on. Abraham lied. Sarah went along with it. A king took her. And God Himself stepped into that king’s bedroom and stood between him and Sarah, because the son God promised was going to come through this woman and nobody was going to put that in doubt. Abraham created the danger. God protected the promise. That is the whole chapter in one verse.

> “Now therefore restore the man his wife; for he is a prophet, and he shall pray for thee, and thou shalt live: and if thou restore her not, know thou that thou shalt surely die, thou, and all that are thine.” (Genesis 20:7)

Here is the word to mark. **Prophet**. This is the first time that word shows up in the whole Bible, and the first person it is ever used for is Abraham. A prophet is a person God picks to speak for Him and to stand between Him and people. We usually think of a prophet as somebody who predicts the future, but look at what God says this prophet will do. *He shall pray for thee*. The job here is intercession, which we learned on Day 16. Praying for somebody else.

Now feel how strange that is. The man God calls His prophet is the man who just lied to this king and put his wife in the king’s house. God does not say, he used to be My prophet until he did this. God says he is a prophet, and the king needs his prayers to live. Being called by God did not make what Abraham did right. And what Abraham did did not cancel his calling. Both things are true at the same time, and if you have ever wondered whether God can still use you after you did the same dumb thing again, read this verse twice.

But notice the warning is still real. Innocent heart or not, if you do not give her back, you die, you and everyone who is yours. Knowing the truth now, Abimelech has to act on it.

> “Therefore Abimelech rose early in the morning, and called all his servants, and told all these things in their ears: and the men were sore afraid.” (Genesis 20:8)

**Sore afraid** means very frightened. The pagan king wakes up early, calls in his whole staff, and tells them what God said. And the whole room is scared of God. Hold on to that picture, because Abraham is about to tell us what he assumed about these people.

> “Then Abimelech called Abraham, and said unto him, What hast thou done unto us? and what have I offended thee, that thou hast brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin? thou hast done deeds unto me that ought not to be done. And Abimelech said unto Abraham, What sawest thou, that thou hast done this thing?” (Genesis 20:9–10)

Now the king confronts the prophet, and the king is in the right. What did you do to us? What did I ever do to you? You nearly brought a great sin down on me and my whole kingdom. *Deeds that ought not to be done*. Things one person does not do to another. And then the question that cuts. *What sawest thou*? What did you see here that made you do this?

Sit in that for a second. A pagan king is lecturing the friend of God about right and wrong, and he has a point. Abraham exposed a whole kingdom to God’s judgment to cover his own fear. Abimelech wants to know what he saw in Gerar that justified that.

> “And Abraham said, Because I thought, Surely the fear of God is not in this place; and they will slay me for my wife’s sake.” (Genesis 20:11)

Here is Abraham’s answer. *Because I thought*. Not because I saw. Because I thought. He assumed nobody in Gerar feared God, so he assumed they would kill him to get his wife, so he lied before anybody did anything.

And he was wrong. Go back two verses. The king and his whole staff were *sore afraid* of God. There was fear of God in that place. Abraham decided ahead of time what these people were, and he treated his fear like it was a fact. That is what fear does. It writes the ending before the story starts, and then it makes you act on the ending you wrote.

Where did the fear come from? It goes all the way back. On Day 9, before they ever got to Egypt, Abram said the Egyptians would see Sarai, say this is his wife, and kill him. That was before he had met a single Egyptian. Kings in that world did have power to take what they wanted, and the Egyptians themselves later told stories about a Pharaoh who took a man’s wife and had the man killed, so the fear was not crazy. But a possible threat and an actual threat from this actual king are two different things, and Abraham never waited to find out which one he was facing. He just ran the play.

> “And yet indeed she is my sister; she is the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother; and she became my wife.” (Genesis 20:12)

Now a piece of family information we have never been told. Sarah really is Abraham’s sister. Half sister. Same father, Terah, different mother. Back on Day 6, Genesis 11 just said Abram took Sarai as his wife and never explained how they were related. Here it comes out. In that world, marrying a half sister was not unheard of, and it keeps the family line tight.

But do not let Abraham off the hook with that. Technically true is still a lie when the point is to deceive. He told the truth that would get him what he wanted and left out the one word that mattered. Wife. Everybody in Gerar heard sister and understood available. That was the whole point of saying it.

> “And it came to pass, when God caused me to wander from my father’s house, that I said unto her, This is thy kindness which thou shalt shew unto me; at every place whither we shall come, say of me, He is my brother.” (Genesis 20:13)

And here is the sentence that answers the question I raised at the start. How long has this been going on? Since the beginning. When God first called him out of Haran, Abraham sat Sarah down and said, here is how you can be kind to me. Everywhere we go, tell them I am your brother.

Everywhere. Not just Egypt. Not just Gerar. This was the standing plan for twenty five years. Every town they rolled into, Sarah was his sister. So the couple working the con was not a one time panic. It was a policy. And it was built on the wife taking the risk so the husband could feel safe. Sarah agreed to it, and she went along with it both times we know about, so she is not a bystander. But she is also the one who got taken, twice, while the plan protected him.

Now, give Abraham this much, because it is fair. Moving a household like his was not a road trip. He was responsible for hundreds of people, every animal, every supply, water, pasture, and safety, through country full of strangers and thieves and wild animals. The man carrying all of that would feel the weight of what happens to everybody if he dies. That makes the fear understandable. It does not make the plan right. Understanding why a man is afraid and excusing what he did with the fear are two different things.

> “And Abimelech took sheep, and oxen, and menservants, and womenservants, and gave them unto Abraham, and restored him Sarah his wife. And Abimelech said, Behold, my land is before thee: dwell where it pleaseth thee.” (Genesis 20:14–15)

Now the part that bothers people. Abraham lied, and he walks away richer. Sheep, oxen, servants, and his wife back. And on top of that, permission to live anywhere in the kingdom he wants. Compare that to Egypt, where Pharaoh gave him gifts and then threw him out of the country. Here the king says, stay. My land is before you.

So why the gifts? Go back to verse 7. God told this king, give the man his wife back, he is a prophet, and he will pray for you so you can live. Abimelech is a frightened man who just heard that his life depends on this prophet’s prayer. Of course he wants peace with him. Of course he sends gifts. He is making things right with the man whose God just spoke to him in the night. The gifts are the king trying to repair what happened and get on God’s good side, not God paying Abraham for lying. God never says well done in this chapter. The king says please.

The servants, by the way, are enslaved household workers, men and women who would do the labor of a camp that size. That is what a king gave a wealthy man in that world. We will see this word again, and it is never pretty.

> “And unto Sarah he said, Behold, I have given thy brother a thousand pieces of silver: behold, he is to thee a covering of the eyes, unto all that are with thee, and with all other: thus she was reproved.” (Genesis 20:16)

This verse is hard in the old English, so take it slow. First, the money. *A thousand pieces of silver*. The Hebrew says a thousand of silver, and that means a thousand shekels, a unit of weight, not a thousand coins. It comes to around twenty five pounds of silver.

How much is that? Here is a comparison from inside this very book. A few days from now, Abraham is going to buy a field with a cave in it to bury Sarah, and he pays four hundred shekels for the whole property. Abimelech just handed over two and a half times that. If you imagine that field and cave as a two hundred thousand dollar property today, the king just paid Sarah’s husband five hundred thousand dollars. That is a picture of the scale, not a real exchange rate, but it tells you this was not a tip. It was a very public, very large payment.

Second, *thy brother*. Hear the edge in that. The king knows Abraham is her husband now. He calls him her brother anyway, and I think he means it to sting. Your brother, Sarah. The one you told me about.

Third, *a covering of the eyes*. This is a saying, and it means the payment covers Sarah in front of everybody who is looking. Think about her situation. A woman gets taken into a king’s house and then sent back out. People talk. Did he sleep with her? Did he reject her? Is she damaged goods now? A giant public payment like this says to the whole town, nothing happened to this woman, her honor is intact, and here is twenty five pounds of silver to prove I mean it. The king is protecting Sarah’s name in other people’s eyes. The Bible already told us the truth in verse 6. God never let him touch her. The payment makes that truth public.

And last, *thus she was reproved*. **Reproved** means corrected. Some Bibles translate this last phrase as vindicated, cleared, because the Hebrew is hard and both ideas are in the scene. Sarah is cleared of any shame about what happened in the king’s house. And Sarah is also corrected, because she said he is my brother too, and that lie put a kingdom in danger.

Cleared and corrected are not the same thing, and both are happening to her in the same sentence.

> “So Abraham prayed unto God: and God healed Abimelech, and his wife, and his maidservants; and they bare children. For the LORD had fast closed up all the wombs of the house of Abimelech, because of Sarah Abraham’s wife.” (Genesis 20:17–18)

Now the last piece falls into place, and verse 18 explains what had been going on the whole time. **Fast closed up** does not mean closed quickly. It means shut tight. Completely. While Sarah was in that house, God had shut the wombs of every woman in Abimelech’s household. Nobody was conceiving, nobody was giving birth. And Abimelech himself needed healing too. The text does not tell us what was wrong with him, just that God healed him.

Feel the weight of that for a king. Children are the whole future of a house. The family line, the inheritance, and for a king, the throne. If this had gone on, Abimelech’s house would have had no next generation at all. No people lasts without new babies. God had reached into the one thing that keeps a royal household alive and shut it down, because of Sarah.

Then look at the order of events. Sarah is returned. Abraham prays. God heals. Not, Abimelech pays and God heals. The silver did not buy anything from God. The prayer God told the king about in verse 7 is what opened the wombs again. And do not miss who is praying. The man whose lie caused the problem is the man God uses to fix it. Abraham stands before God for a household he endangered, and God answers him.

This is the Egypt pattern finishing the way it did before. Abraham lies, a ruler takes Sarah, God afflicts the ruler’s house, Sarah comes back. In Egypt the Bible just called it great plagues. Here it tells us exactly what it was. Same God protecting the same woman from the same fear in the same husband, twenty five years apart.

And here is a connection I cannot stop thinking about. Abraham is praying for another man’s household to have children. While he prays, his own wife, ninety years old, is still waiting on the son God promised within the year. The last thing Abraham does in this chapter is ask God to open wombs. The first thing that happens in the next chapter is that God opens Sarah’s. That is not Abraham earning Isaac with a prayer. Isaac was promised long before this. But it is a beautiful thing to see, a man asking for other people’s children right before God gives him his own.

So step back and look at the whole day. Abraham, twenty five years into walking with God, with a promise and a date in his hand, ran the oldest play he had, and his wife ran it with him. The fear he brought out of Haran was still in him. And God did not throw him away for it. God protected the promise, warned the king, called the liar a prophet, and used his prayer to heal the house he had hurt.

That is good news, and it is also a warning. The good news is that your old fear does not cancel God’s calling on your life. He can still use you the day after you fell back into the same thing. The warning is that twenty five years of walking with God did not make Abraham’s fear go away on its own. The fear you do not deal with will be waiting for you in the next town. It will put the people you love in danger to keep you safe. And a pagan king might end up being the one to ask you, what did you see that made you do this?

Tomorrow, after twenty five years of waiting, a baby cries in Sarah’s tent. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **What is your old play, the thing you fall back on when you are afraid, even after years of walking with God?** Abraham ran the same lie twenty five years apart. Name yours plainly. The fear you do not deal with will be waiting in the next town.
2. **Where have you decided ahead of time what people are, and treated your fear like a fact?** Abraham thought there was no fear of God in Gerar. He was wrong, and a whole kingdom paid for his assumption. Who have you written a story about before you met them?
3. **Who pays the price when you protect yourself?** Sarah got taken twice so Abraham could feel safe. Look honestly at who carries the cost of your self protection.

## Today's One Thing

**Pray for the House You Hurt**

The man whose lie caused the damage was the man God used to pray for the healing. Do what Abraham did at the end of the chapter.

1. Write down one person or household your fear has cost something. A lie you told to stay safe. A risk you handed to somebody else.
2. Pray for them by name today, specifically, for the thing your choice took from them. Then ask God to deal with the fear underneath it.
3. If there is a conversation you owe them, write the first sentence of it tonight. Just the first sentence.

One name, one prayer, one sentence. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You protected Sarah in a king’s house when her own husband would not. You stood between her and danger that he created. Thank You for protecting Your promises even from the people You made them to.

I have an old play too. A thing I reach for when I am afraid, that I thought I left behind years ago. You know what it is. You have watched me run it in more than one town.

**Deal with the fear underneath, Lord, not just the lie on top of it.**

Forgive me for deciding ahead of time what people are. For treating my fear like a fact and acting on an ending I wrote myself. Open my eyes the way You opened Abraham’s, even if it takes a stranger to ask me what I saw.

And forgive me for the people who paid for my safety. Show me who they are. Give me the courage to pray for them by name, and to say the first sentence of what I owe them.

Thank You that You still called Abraham a prophet the morning after his lie. Thank You that You used his prayer to heal a house he hurt. Do that with me. Do not throw me away for the old fear. Use me anyway, and heal what I broke.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
