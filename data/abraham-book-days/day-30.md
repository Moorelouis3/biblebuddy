Thirty days ago we met a man in a city full of idols, with a wife who could not have children and a father who got comfortable halfway to the promise. His name was Abram. He did not know God. God knew him.

We have walked beside him for a hundred years since then. We watched him leave Haran, lie in Egypt, split with Lot, ride to war, laugh at a promise, take a shortcut, get a new name, feed three strangers, argue for a city, lie again, hold his son, let his other son go, lift a knife, bury his wife, and send a servant to find a bride.

Today he dies.

But look at how the Bible tells it. Not as a tragedy. As a man who got everything God said he would get, lived a full life, and was buried by both of his sons, side by side, in the one piece of the promised land he ever owned. And then, in the very last verse, God turns to the next generation and keeps going.

**Today the father of the promise comes home, and the promise does not stop.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 25

The chapter opens with a wife you have never heard of and a list of sons. Do not get lost in the names. Watch what Abraham does with them.

Notice who is standing at the grave in verse 9. Both of them.

The last verse is about Isaac, not Abraham. That is on purpose.

**As you read, notice:**

* Who gets everything, and who gets gifts
* How old Abraham is when he dies
* Where he is buried, and who he is buried next to
* Where Isaac is living when the chapter ends

And think about this question: **What does it mean to die full?**

> “Then Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people.” (Genesis 25:8)

# The End of the Road

Yesterday ended with Isaac loving his wife in his mother’s tent. Today opens with his father, and a surprise:

> “Then again Abraham took a wife, and her name was Keturah. And she bare him Zimran, and Jokshan, and Medan, and Midian, and Ishbak, and Shuah.” (Genesis 25:1–2)

Keturah. Another wife. Six more sons. I know. The first time I read this I had to stop and read it again.

Here is what the Bible tells us and what it does not. Her name is Keturah. In the book of Chronicles she is called Abraham’s concubine, which means she was a recognized partner in the household but never held the place Sarah held. We are not told where she came from, who her family was, or exactly when Abraham married her. Genesis puts her here, after Sarah, but it is also gathering up the rest of Abraham’s family history in one place before it tells us he died, so do not assume every one of these boys was born after Isaac’s wedding. What we know is that Abraham had more children, and the Bible wants them on the record.

And yes, you are noticing the question. Abraham made his servant swear an oath to get Isaac a wife from his own family, and then we have no idea where Keturah came from. The difference is that Isaac was the covenant son. The promise ran through him, so his marriage had to protect the line. That rule was for Isaac. It was never written over every marriage in Abraham’s tent.

> “And Jokshan begat Sheba, and Dedan. And the sons of Dedan were Asshurim, and Letushim, and Leummim. And the sons of Midian; Ephah, and Epher, and Hanoch, and Abidah, and Eldaah. All these were the children of Keturah.” (Genesis 25:3–4)

Now grandsons and great grandsons. Do not try to hold all of these names. Just notice two things. First, not all of them are Abraham’s sons.

Sheba and Dedan are Jokshan’s boys, Abraham’s grandsons. The Asshurim and the rest are a generation further down. Second, one name in this list is going to matter a lot later. Midian. The Midianites come from this son. When Moses runs from Egypt, he runs to Midian and marries a Midianite. When Israel is in the wilderness, Midian is both family and trouble. All of that starts here, with a son of Abraham and Keturah.

> “And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts, and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he yet lived, eastward, unto the east country.” (Genesis 25:5–6)

Here is what Abraham does with his family before he dies, and it is not equal. *All that he had* goes to Isaac. Everything. The herds, the silver, the gold, the servants, the field with the cave, and the promise. Isaac is the covenant son, and the covenant son gets the whole inheritance.

The other sons get **gifts**. Real gifts, from a very rich man, so not nothing. And then they are sent away, *eastward, unto the east country*, while Abraham is still alive. He settles his estate with his own hands, so there is no fight over it after he is gone. He sends the other boys east with something to start on, and he leaves Isaac standing alone in the land God promised.

Now, be honest about how that feels. If you were one of Keturah’s sons, watching your half brother get everything while you got a gift and directions out of town, how would you feel? The Bible does not tell us. It does not record any resentment or any fight. I just want you to sit with the question, because Abraham is making a hard, clear choice. The promise goes one way. He is not going to split it six ways to keep everybody happy. He is going to protect it, even if it costs him sons walking east.

And remember, this is the third time. Ishmael went into the desert on Day 21. Now Keturah’s sons go east. Abraham has spent his old age letting go of sons so that the one son could carry the one promise. Nobody in this book has had to open his hands more.

> “And these are the days of the years of Abraham’s life which he lived, an hundred threescore and fifteen years.” (Genesis 25:7)

A **score** is twenty. Three score is sixty. A hundred, plus sixty, plus fifteen. A hundred and seventy five years old.

Do the math one last time, because we have been doing it the whole book. He was a hundred when Isaac was born. He was a hundred and forty when Isaac married. So Abraham lived thirty five more years after Day 29. Long enough to see grandsons. Long enough for the quiet decades the Bible skips over. He lost Sarah at a hundred and thirty seven and lived thirty eight more years without her. This chapter is covering a lot of time in a few verses, and it is all happening after the last big scene we saw.

> “Then Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people.” (Genesis 25:8)

Four ways of saying one thing, and each one adds something.

*Gave up the ghost* is old English for breathed his last. Ghost here just means spirit, breath. He let go of his last breath and died.

*In a good old age*. Go back to Day 12. Standing in the dark beside the cut animals, God told a frightened man, *thou shalt be buried in a good old age*. Here it is. God said he would die old and at peace, and he did. Not by a king’s sword in Egypt. Not on a battlefield chasing four kings. Old, in his own tent, with his affairs settled.

*Full of years*. Not just old. Full. A life that had everything in it. That is how I want you to think about Abraham at the end. Not a man who ran out of time. A man who was filled up. He got the son. He got the land, one field of it anyway, and the promise of all the rest. He got the covenant, sworn on God’s own name. He got to see his son married and loved. He was full.

*Gathered to his people*. This is more than being buried in the family cave, because his people, his fathers, were not buried at Machpelah. Terah is in Haran. The ancestors are back in Ur. Gathered to his people means he joined them, on the other side of death. The Bible does not explain how that works, and I am not going to pretend to. It just says that when Abraham died, he was not alone. He went to be with his own.

> “And his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him in the cave of Machpelah, in the field of Ephron the son of Zohar the Hittite, which is before Mamre; The field which Abraham purchased of the sons of Heth: there was Abraham buried, and Sarah his wife.” (Genesis 25:9–10)

Read the first four words slowly. *His sons Isaac and Ishmael*. Both of them. Standing at the same grave.

Think about what that means. On Day 21, Abraham sent Ishmael and his mother into the desert with a skin of water, and I told you not to say he never saw him again. Here is why. Ishmael came to bury his father. The son who was sent away and the son who got everything stand together at the cave and lay the old man down. The Bible does not tell us what happened in between, whether there were visits, whether there were letters, whether they spoke at the grave. It just tells us they were both there. God said He would be with the lad in the desert, and the lad grew up and came back to put his father in the ground.

And look where they put him. The cave of Machpelah. Ephron’s field. The one Abraham bought at the city gate for four hundred shekels of silver on Day 24, weighed out in front of the whole town. The Bible repeats every detail of that purchase here on purpose. Ephron, son of Zohar, the Hittite, before Mamre, bought from the sons of Heth. It is making sure you know that Abraham was buried as an owner, in land that was legally his, in the country God promised him. The only piece of the promise he ever held in his hands is the piece he is lying in.

*There was Abraham buried, and Sarah his wife*. He bought that cave on the worst day of his life to put her in it. Thirty eight years later, his sons put him in beside her. The man who left Ur with her is lying next to her in the land God showed them. That is the end of their road.

> “And it came to pass after the death of Abraham, that God blessed his son Isaac; and Isaac dwelt by the well Lahairoi.” (Genesis 25:11)

And then the Bible does the thing it has been doing since Day 1. It keeps going. After the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac. The man is gone. The promise is not. God does not bless Abraham and then retire. He turns to the son and keeps blessing. The covenant He swore by His own name on the mountain did not die in the cave. It just moved to the next tent.

And where is that tent? *By the well Lahairoi*. Hagar’s well. The well of the Living One who sees me. The last word in Abraham’s story is Isaac living beside the place where God found the servant girl nobody else was looking for. God’s mercy to the outsider and God’s promise to the heir end up at the same water. That is who this God is. He saw Hagar. He saw Abraham. He sees Isaac. He sees you.

So step back now and look at the whole road, from Ur to the cave.

This book is called The Obedience of Abraham, and I hope you have seen by now what that does not mean. It does not mean Abraham was perfect. He lied about his wife twice. He took a shortcut with Hagar and let it wreck his household. He laughed in God’s face. He got comfortable and had to be dragged forward. He was afraid more than he was brave. If obedience meant never failing, this book would be three pages long.

Here is what it does mean. He kept going. Every time he fell, he got up and walked the direction God pointed. He left when God said leave. He built an altar every place he camped. He rode for his nephew. He argued for a city. He got up early on the hardest mornings of his life and did the thing. He opened his hands and let sons go. He lifted a knife over the promise itself because God asked, and he trusted that God would provide. And God did, every single time.

That is obedience. Not a perfect record. A consistent direction. Falling and getting back up and walking toward God for a hundred years until you are full of years and your sons lay you down in a field you bought with your own silver, in a land God swore was yours.

You are not going to be perfect either. You already know that. So stop beating yourself up for the Egypt moments and the Hagar moments and the laughing behind the door. Abraham had all of those. The question God is asking you is the same one He asked him. Will you keep walking? Will you get up early tomorrow and do the next thing I said? Will you trust Me with the thing you love most?

If the answer is yes, then you are in Abraham’s family, whatever your last name is. Because the Bible says the blessing was always going to reach all the nations of the earth through his seed. It reached you. You are reading this because an old man in Ur said yes to a God he did not know, and kept saying it for a hundred years.

Go and do likewise. Not perfectly. Faithfully.

This is the last day of our walk with Abraham, but his family is just getting started. Isaac and Rebekah are about to have twin boys who fight before they are born, and that is a whole book of its own. For now, finish this one well. Take the reflection questions, do today’s action, and end your day, and this study, in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **Abraham died full of years. If your life ended today, what would it be full of?** Not long. Full. Write down what you want in it that is not there yet, and what God has already filled it with.
2. **Isaac and Ishmael stood at the same grave. Who in your family have you written off as gone for good that God might still bring back to the same cave?** God was with the lad in the desert, and the lad came home for the funeral. Do not close a door God has not closed.
3. **After thirty days with Abraham, what is the one thing you are going to keep doing when this book is closed?** His obedience was a direction, not a perfect record. Pick your direction and name the next step.

## Today's One Thing

**Keep Walking**

Abraham obeyed for a hundred years by getting up and doing the next thing. Set up your next thing before you close this book.

1. Go back through your thirty actions. Write down the three that changed something, and the one you skipped that you know you should not have.
2. Do the one you skipped today. Not tomorrow. Rise up early, like Abraham, and finish it.
3. Write down what you will read next, and when. Put the first day on your calendar. The promise did not stop at Abraham’s grave, and your walk does not stop at page thirty.

Three kept, one finished, one next thing scheduled. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You told a frightened man in the dark that he would die in a good old age, and he did. Full of years. Buried by both of his sons in a field he owned in a land You promised. You keep every word You say, all the way to the grave and past it.

Thank You for Abraham. For a man who was not perfect, who lied and laughed and took shortcuts and got scared, and who kept walking anyway. You did not need him to be flawless. You needed him to keep going. That gives me more hope than I can say.

**Make me faithful, Lord, not perfect. Keep me walking in Your direction for the rest of my life.**

Forgive me for the Egypt days and the Hagar days and the days I laughed behind the door. Then get me up early tomorrow and show me the next thing You have said. I will do it.

Open my hands around the people and the things I hold too tightly. You were with Ishmael in the desert, and You brought him home for the funeral. You can be trusted with everyone I let go.

And when my years are full, gather me to my people and to You. Until then, let me live like a man who knows that the blessing You swore to Abraham reached all the way down to me, and keep it moving through me to whoever comes next.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
