It is the hottest part of the day. The kind of heat where the animals lie down, the servants find shade, and nobody with any sense is out walking the road.

Abraham is sitting in the door of his tent. He is ninety nine years old, and he is still sore from what he did yesterday, because the selfsame day obedience of Genesis 17 is not the kind of thing a man recovers from overnight. He has a new name he is still getting used to. His wife has a new name too. And he has a promise with a date on it. Next year.

Then he looks up, and three men are standing there.

He does not know who they are. That is the part to hold on to. He does not see a halo. He sees three dusty travelers in the heat of the day, and what he does next tells you who he is before anybody tells him who they are. He runs.

By the time the meal is over, one of those travelers is going to say Sarah’s name, repeat the promise, and hear a laugh nobody was supposed to hear.

**Today the promise gets personal.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 18

Count how many times Abraham runs, hurries, or hastens in the first eight verses. The speed is the point.

Listen to what Abraham offers the visitors, and then watch what he actually brings out. The gap between the two is the whole first half of the chapter.

Sarah is behind the tent door. Pay attention to what she hears, what she does, and what God says about it.

**As you read, notice:**

* What Abraham calls the visitors, and what he calls himself
* The moment the visitors say something only God would know
* Why Sarah laughs, and where she is standing when she does
* The question God asks in verse 14

And think about this question: **Are Abraham and Sarah measuring the promise by their bodies, or by the One making it?**

> “Is any thing too hard for the LORD? At the time appointed I will return unto thee… and Sarah shall have a son.” (Genesis 18:14)

# The Visitors at Mamre

Yesterday ended with Abraham getting up off the ground and obeying God the same day. Today opens with him sitting down:

> “And the LORD appeared unto him in the plains of Mamre: and he sat in the tent door in the heat of the day.” (Genesis 18:1)

Mamre is where Abraham has been camped since Day 10, by the big trees near Hebron. The word **plains** here means those trees. This is home base. And it is the heat of the day, the stretch of afternoon when the sun is straight overhead and the whole camp slows down. Abraham is in the shade of the tent door, which is where the head of a household sat to watch over everything.

Get the picture right before we go on. When you hear tent, do not think of one lonely camping tent with two old people in it. Abraham is rich. He has silver and gold, herds that cover hillsides, servants born in his camp, and hundreds of men who can fight. His tent sits in the middle of an encampment that works like a small town. People, animals, cook fires, water, supplies. When he sits in the tent door, he is sitting at the front door of all of it.

Notice that the verse tells us the LORD appeared. We know that. Abraham does not. Watch what he sees:

> “And he lift up his eyes and looked, and, lo, three men stood by him: and when he saw them, he ran to meet them from the tent door, and bowed himself toward the ground.” (Genesis 18:2)

Three men. Not three angels, not three glowing figures. Three men standing in the heat of the day, which already tells Abraham they have been traveling, because nobody walks in that heat unless they have to. And the ninety nine year old man, who yesterday had a very personal surgery, runs.

He runs to them, and he bows to the ground. That bow is how people in his world greeted someone with honor. He has no idea yet who these men are. He is honoring strangers.

> “And said, My Lord, if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away, I pray thee, from thy servant: Let a little water, I pray you, be fetched, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree; And I will fetch a morsel of bread, and comfort ye your hearts; after that ye shall pass on: for therefore are ye come to your servant. And they said, So do, as thou hast said.” (Genesis 18:3–5)

Hear how he talks. *My Lord* is respectful, the way you would say sir to a stranger you want to honor. And he calls himself *thy servant*. The wealthiest man in the region is talking to three dusty travelers like he works for them.

Then listen to what he offers. *A little water*. *A morsel of bread*, which means a bite, a small piece. Wash your feet, rest under the tree, eat a little something, then be on your way. **Wash your feet** is not a religious ritual. These men have been walking dusty roads in sandals. Water for the feet is how you let a traveler cool down and clean up before he sits down to eat. And *comfort ye your hearts* means refresh yourselves. Get your strength back.

In Abraham’s world, taking care of travelers was not optional. There were no hotels, no restaurants, no gas stations with a cooler of water. If a stranger came to your camp in the heat, you were his only shot at shade and food for miles. So water, rest, and bread were expected. That is basic hospitality.

Now watch what Abraham actually does with his little water and his morsel of bread:

> “And Abraham hastened into the tent unto Sarah, and said, Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal, knead it, and make cakes upon the hearth. And Abraham ran unto the herd, and fetcht a calf tender and good, and gave it unto a young man; and he hasted to dress it.” (Genesis 18:6–7)

Hastened. Quickly. Ran. Hasted. Four speed words in two verses. The whole camp is moving.

Three measures of **fine meal** is a lot of the good flour, far more than three men can eat. *Cakes upon the hearth* are not birthday cakes. They are flatbread, dough slapped onto hot stones by the fire and baked fast. Sarah is told to get it going right now. And remember what kind of household this is. Sarah is not an old woman alone in a tiny kitchen doing every job with her own two hands. She is running the bread side of a big operation, with plenty of help in a camp this size.

Then Abraham runs to the herd himself and picks out a calf, *tender and good*, the choice animal, and hands it to a young man, one of his servants, who hurries to **dress it**, which means butcher it and cook it. Stop and think about that. A morsel of bread became fresh bread from the best flour and a whole calf. Nobody slaughters a calf for a snack. That is a feast. That is the kind of meal you put on for a king.

> “And he took butter, and milk, and the calf which he had dressed, and set it before them; and he stood by them under the tree, and they did eat.” (Genesis 18:8)

**Butter** in the old English is not a cold stick from the refrigerator. Think of thick, soured milk, something like curds or a rich yogurt, the kind of dairy a herding family always had on hand. So the table is fresh bread, cool dairy, and roasted calf. Simple, and rich.

And look where Abraham is. *He stood by them under the tree*. He does not sit down and eat with them. He stands nearby like a waiter, ready to bring whatever they need. The man with the servants is serving. The man with the herds is the one standing.

So here is the heart of the first half of this chapter. Abraham offered a little and gave a lot. He treated three strangers like honored guests before he had any idea who was sitting under his tree. His generosity was not a performance for God. He did not know it was God. That is exactly why it tells you so much about him. Who you are when you think nobody important is watching is who you are.

The New Testament remembers this scene. Hebrews says that some, by showing hospitality to strangers, have entertained angels without knowing it. Abraham is the man they are talking about.

Was there anything else mixed in? Maybe a little. Abraham is a wealthy man who knows that travelers talk, and it would not hurt for three men to leave his camp and tell the next town that the man in the tents at Mamre is nobody to take lightly. People are mixed like that, and Abraham was a person. But the text puts the weight on what he did, and what he did was run, bow, serve, and stand. Hold on to that.

Then the meal is over, and the day turns:

> “And they said unto him, Where is Sarah thy wife? And he said, Behold, in the tent.” (Genesis 18:9)

Feel the chill in that question. Three strangers walked in off the road, and they know his wife’s name. Not Sarai. Sarah. The name God gave her yesterday. Nobody outside this camp knows that name yet. Whoever these men are, they are not just travelers.

> “And he said, I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life; and, lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son. And Sarah heard it in the tent door, which was behind him.” (Genesis 18:10)

Notice the change. In verse 9 it is *they*. In verse 10 it is *he*. One of the three is speaking now, and in a few verses the Bible is going to call Him by name. *The LORD said*. One of the men under Abraham’s tree is God Himself, the way He chose to show up that day. The other two, when they walk into Sodom tomorrow, are called angels. So this is the LORD and two angels, not three gods and not a picture of the Trinity. God came to dinner.

And He repeats the promise from yesterday. *According to the time of life* means when this season comes back around, this time next year. It is the same date God gave in Genesis 17. *Sarah thy wife shall have a son*. Same promise, same mother, same clock.

But look at the last line. Sarah is in the tent door, behind him, listening. That is the whole reason this scene is in the Bible. Yesterday God made this promise to Abraham. Today He makes it where Sarah can hear it.

Imagine Abraham bringing the news home after Genesis 17. He walks into the tent with a new name and a wild report. Sarah, He said you. Not the servant. Not a shortcut. You. A son from you, next year. And He gave you a new name. Imagine being Sarah hearing that from your husband. Twenty four years of waiting, one shortcut you regret, and now your husband comes in talking about a baby when you are ninety.

Hearing it from your husband is one thing. Hearing it from the mouth of the One who said it is another. Today Sarah does not get the promise secondhand. She hears it herself, through the tent flap, in a stranger’s voice that knows her name.

> “Now Abraham and Sarah were old and well stricken in age; and it ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women.” (Genesis 18:11)

The Bible is being plain and polite at the same time. **Well stricken in age** means very old. And *it ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women* means she had stopped having her monthly cycle years ago. Her body could not have a baby. Not a hard case. A closed case. The verse puts that right in front of you so you understand her reaction.

> “Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?” (Genesis 18:12)

She laughs. Quietly, inside herself, behind the tent door. *Waxed old* means grown old, worn out. And her question is the most honest sentence in the chapter. I am worn out, my husband is old, and I am going to have the joy of a baby now? Picture it from where she is standing. A ninety year old woman, a hundred year old man, a newborn in the tent. It is absurd. Of course she laughed.

Now put her laugh next to the one from yesterday. Abraham fell on his face and laughed in Genesis 17. Sarah stands behind the door and laughs in Genesis 18. Same promise. Same reason. Two old people picturing themselves with a baby and finding it ridiculous. Do not do what a lot of people do with these two laughs, where Abraham’s laugh was faith and Sarah’s laugh was doubt. The Bible does not say that. Both of them laughed at the same impossible thing. They were human.

> “And the LORD said unto Abraham, Wherefore did Sarah laugh, saying, Shall I of a surety bear a child, which am old? Is any thing too hard for the LORD? At the time appointed I will return unto thee, according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son.” (Genesis 18:13–14)

Here is the detail to catch. Sarah laughed, and God turns to Abraham. *Wherefore did Sarah laugh*. Why did your wife laugh? Think about why He would ask the husband. Abraham already had this conversation. Yesterday. Face down. God had already told him, I am the Almighty God, and Sarah will have a son. It is not hard to hear what God is really saying to him. Did we not just go over this? You know who is making this promise. Why is your wife standing behind a door laughing at it?

Imagine it from that angle, and the scene gets tender instead of harsh. God is not scolding Sarah for laughing when Abraham laughed too. He is reminding the man who already heard the promise that his job now is to help his wife believe it with him. You carry this together.

And then the question that holds up the whole chapter. *Is any thing too hard for the LORD?* Yesterday God introduced Himself as El Shaddai, the Almighty God. Today He asks the question that name was always leading to. Is anything too hard for Me?

Hear what that question is really doing. Abraham and Sarah have been measuring the promise by their bodies. His age. Her closed womb. The math. And God asks them to measure it by something else. Not by what you can do. By who is saying it. That does not mean God hands out every outcome anybody wants. This is His particular promise to this particular family, and He is going to keep it on His particular schedule. *At the time appointed*. Next year. He says it a second time, so nobody behind any door can miss it.

> “Then Sarah denied, saying, I laughed not; for she was afraid. And he said, Nay; but thou didst laugh.” (Genesis 18:15)

Now she is scared. The Bible says so. *For she was afraid*. And be fair to her. Her laugh was inside. Nobody heard it. Then a stranger under the tree repeats it out loud, word for word. Imagine the jolt of that. He heard what I did not say. So she does what frightened people do. I did not laugh.

It is not a cold, calculated lie. It is a startled one. And it is still not true. Think back to the garden. Adam and Eve heard God coming and hid. Sarah hears God knowing, and she hides behind a denial. People have been hiding their honest reactions from God since Genesis 3, and it has never once worked.

Look at how God handles it. *Nay; but thou didst laugh*. No. You did. That is all. He does not crush her, and He does not pretend. He just tells the truth and lets it stand. And notice that the promise does not move an inch. She laughed, she denied it, and Sarah is still having a son next year. God’s word did not depend on her reaction. It depended on Him.

So step back and look at the whole day. It opened with a wealthy old man running to serve three strangers in the heat, treating nobodies like royalty before he knew he was feeding God. It ended with God saying a laughing, frightened woman’s name and repeating a promise her body could not carry. The welcome and the laugh belong together. Abraham gave his best to people he did not understand. God gave His promise to people who could not believe it yet.

That is still how He works. He shows up in ordinary afternoons. He knows the laugh you kept inside. He asks whether anything is too hard for Him. And He keeps His word anyway.

Tomorrow, the three men stand up from the meal and look toward Sodom, and Abraham walks them out to the road. What he says to God on that road is the boldest conversation in his whole life. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **Abraham gave his best to strangers who could do nothing for him. Who gets your best, and who gets your leftovers?** Think about the people you serve when nobody important is watching. That is the real measure of your generosity.
2. **What promise have you been measuring by your body, your age, your bank account, or your past, instead of by the One who made it?** Is anything too hard for the LORD? Say the thing you have been quietly laughing at, and then say His name over it.
3. **When God sees your honest reaction, do you bring it to Him or hide it like Sarah did?** He already knows the laugh. He already knows the fear. Denying it has never worked for anybody. Tell Him the truth and let the promise stand.

## Today's One Thing

**Set the Table**

Abraham offered a morsel and brought out a feast for people who could not repay him. Do a small version of that this week.

1. Write down the name of one person who cannot do anything for you. A neighbor, a new coworker, somebody who is always alone.
2. Feed them. Invite them to a meal, bring them dinner, or buy their lunch. Put the day on your calendar before you close this book.
3. While you are getting it ready, tell God the one promise you have laughed at, out loud, and ask Him, is anything too hard for You?

One name, one meal, one honest prayer. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You showed up at Abraham’s tent in the heat of an ordinary afternoon, and he did not even know it was You. Teach me to treat the people in front of me the way he treated three strangers. Give them my best, not my leftovers.

I have stood behind the tent door too. I have heard Your promises and laughed inside, because from where I am standing they look impossible. You heard that laugh. You know exactly which promise it was.

**Is anything too hard for You, Lord? Help me answer that question with my life.**

Forgive me for measuring Your word by my body, my age, my money, and my history, instead of by You. You are El Shaddai. You said what You said, and You do not need my permission or my faith to keep it.

And when I am afraid of what You already know about me, keep me from hiding like Sarah. Let me bring You the truth instead of a denial. You can handle my honest laugh. You cannot bless my pretending.

Thank You for saying Sarah’s name, and for saying mine. Thank You for a promise that did not move when she laughed, and will not move when I do.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
