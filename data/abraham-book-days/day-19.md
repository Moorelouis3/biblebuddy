Lot made it. That should be the end of his story. The angels dragged him out, God spared the little town he begged for, and the sun came up on a man who was alive when four cities full of people were not.

But the Bible does not end Lot’s story at the gate of Zoar. It follows him up into the hills, into a cave, and into one of the hardest scenes in the whole book of Genesis. If you have read it before, you probably read it fast and moved on. Today we are not going to move on. We are going to sit in that cave and ask the questions that need asking.

Here is the thing I want you to carry in with you. You can get a person out of Sodom in one night. Getting Sodom out of a person is a different story. Lot and his daughters walked out of that city with their lives. What they did not leave behind was the way the city taught them to think.

And this is not just Lot’s story. It runs all the way back to a lie in Egypt, a pile of gifts from Pharaoh, and a fight between two families’ herdsmen. Choices have long tails.

**Today we see what a place leaves inside a person.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 19

Verse 30 tells you where Lot ends up. Compare it to where he refused to go on Day 17.

Pay attention to who talks in this passage and who does not. Lot never says a word.

This is a hard passage with sexual content. Read it as it is written. The Bible does not hide what happened, and neither will we.

**As you read, notice:**

* Why Lot leaves Zoar, the town he begged to go to
* What the older daughter says about the men of the earth
* What the daughters say their purpose is
* The two nations that come from this cave

And think about this question: **They knew Abraham was alive. Why did they not go to him?**

> “And Lot went up out of Zoar, and dwelt in the mountain… for he feared to dwell in Zoar: and he dwelt in a cave.” (Genesis 19:30)

# The Cave

Yesterday ended with God remembering Abraham and sending Lot out of the fire. Here is where Lot goes next:

> “And Lot went up out of Zoar, and dwelt in the mountain, and his two daughters with him; for he feared to dwell in Zoar: and he dwelt in a cave, he and his two daughters.” (Genesis 19:30)

Read that slowly, because it is strange. Lot stood in the road at dawn and begged God, please, not the mountain, let me go to this little town instead. God said yes. God spared the town for him. And now Lot leaves the town and goes up into the mountain. The exact place he was too scared to go to is where he ends up living.

Why? *For he feared to dwell in Zoar*. He was afraid again. Think about what he has just seen. He stood in Zoar and watched the sky rain fire on the whole valley. Four cities, every person, every field, gone in smoke. His wife did not make it. Imagine standing in a tiny town on the edge of all that and trying to sleep. It is not hard to understand why he could not stay. Fear drove him to Zoar, and fear drove him out of it.

And notice the two trips, because they are different. Sodom to Zoar was an escape, angels holding his hand, no time to pack anything. Zoar to the cave was a move. He had time. He had a town around him. When a man leaves a town for the hills on his own schedule, he takes supplies. Food. Water. Wine. Hold on to that, because it is going to matter in a minute.

Now look at who is left. *He and his two daughters*. That is the whole household. Think about where this man started. On Day 10, Lot had so many flocks and herds and tents and herdsmen that the land could not hold him and Abraham together. A wealthy household leader with his own crew. Now he is three people in a hole in a mountain. Whatever was left of his household was in Sodom, and Sodom is gone. From a rich man’s camp to a cave. That is the full distance of Lot’s road.

> “And the firstborn said unto the younger, Our father is old, and there is not a man in the earth to come in unto us after the manner of all the earth.” (Genesis 19:31)

Now the daughters talk, and Lot never says a word for the rest of the chapter. The older one speaks first. Our father is old. And *there is not a man in the earth to come in unto us after the manner of all the earth*. In plain words, there is no man around to marry us and give us children the way every woman everywhere gets children.

Hear what she is saying and what she is not saying. She is not saying every human being on earth is dead. She just came from Zoar, a town full of people. She knows Abraham is alive. What she is saying is that her world is gone. The city she grew up in, the men she knew, the husband who was promised to her and laughed at her father’s warning, all of it burned. From inside that cave, with an old father and a dead mother and the smell of smoke still in the valley, it felt like there was nobody left.

That is her feeling, and it is a real one. But keep two things separate. Lot was afraid of the destruction. The daughter is the one who decides there are no men. The Bible never puts that thought in Lot’s head. It is her reading of their future.

And here is the question I cannot get past. They knew Abraham. They had lived in his camp. They knew exactly who he was and roughly where he was, a few days’ walk away, with hundreds of people, servants, and herdsmen. If the problem was no man to marry and no future, the answer was sitting on a hill at Mamre. Why did going to Abraham never come up?

I think the answer is Sodom. Not the city. The city is ash. I mean what the city left inside them.

> “Come, let us make our father drink wine, and we will lie with him, that we may preserve seed of our father.” (Genesis 19:32)

There is the plan. Get our father drunk, sleep with him, and have children by him. *Preserve seed of our father* means keep his family line alive. Give him descendants.

Before we go any further, answer the practical question people always ask. Where did they get wine in a cave? Go back to the two trips. They did not grab wine while angels were dragging them out of Sodom. But they left Zoar on their own time, and a family moving from a town into the hills carries provisions. Adam Clarke, a Bible commentator from two hundred years ago, said the same thing. They brought wine with them from Zoar as part of their supplies. That is how a cave in the mountains ends up with enough wine to get a man drunk two nights in a row.

Now the plan itself. Look at what the older daughter said her goal was. A family line. Children. A future. Those are not evil goals. Every woman in her world wanted those things. The problem is the road she picked to get there. She had a father who could have walked them to Abraham. She had a town at the bottom of the hill. And the plan she lands on is to get her father drunk and commit incest.

That is where Sodom shows up. These girls grew up in that city. They were raised on its streets, around its people, breathing its air. When they had to solve a real problem, the solution that looked reasonable to them was a sexual one that broke every boundary God ever set. Pour the wine, do what needs doing, deal with the consequences later. That is Sodom thinking. The city burned on Day 18. The way it taught them to think walked out with them.

I call them half Sodomites, and I do not mean their bloodline. I mean their upbringing. You can be born to a man from Ur who once traveled with Abraham and still think like the city that raised you. That is the whole warning of these three days. Lot got comfortable in Sodom, and his daughters grew up comfortable in it, and when the crisis came, Sodom was the voice in their heads.

None of that makes what they did okay. The Bible does not excuse it, and I am not going to either. They used their father’s body without his knowledge. That is a terrible thing. But understanding where a terrible decision comes from is how you keep from making one yourself.

> “And they made their father drink wine that night: and the firstborn went in, and lay with her father; and he perceived not when she lay down, nor when she arose. And it came to pass on the morrow, that the firstborn said unto the younger, Behold, I lay yesternight with my father: let us make him drink wine this night also; and go thou in, and lie with him, that we may preserve seed of our father. And they made their father drink wine that night also: and the younger arose, and lay with him; and he perceived not when she lay down, nor when she arose.” (Genesis 19:33–35)

Two nights. The older daughter the first night, the younger the second. And twice the Bible says *he perceived not when she lay down, nor when she arose*. Lot was so drunk he did not know. He did not know when it started and he did not know when it ended. The man who sat in the gate of Sodom and judged other people’s business is now passed out in a cave while his daughters decide what happens to his body.

And do not miss the quiet detail about the wine. The daughters poured it, but Lot drank it. Twice. A man who had just watched God burn a valley, who had just lost his wife, who was hiding in a cave out of fear, drank until he could not feel anything. Grief and fear and wine have been keeping company for a long time. He is not the villain of this scene, but he is not awake for it either, and that is its own kind of warning.

Now I want to pull the camera all the way back, because this cave did not come out of nowhere.

Go back to Day 9. Abraham went down to Egypt and told Sarah to say she was his sister, because he was afraid Pharaoh would kill him to get her. He put his wife in another man’s house to save his own neck. And what did he walk out with? *Sheep, and oxen, and he asses, and menservants, and maidservants, and she asses, and camels* (Genesis 12:16). Pharaoh paid him for his sister. Abraham left Egypt richer than he came.

Then Day 10. Abraham and Lot are both so wealthy that their herdsmen are fighting over grass and water. The land cannot hold them. So they split up, and Lot looks down at the green plain and picks Sodom. Then Sodom gets him captured. Then Sodom gets him a seat at the gate. Then Sodom gets a mob at his door, and then Sodom burns, and now Sodom is in a cave in the hills telling two girls what to do.

I am not saying Abraham’s lie in Egypt caused this cave. Lot chose the plain. Lot chose to sit in the gate. Lot chose to drink. The daughters chose the plan. Every person in this story owns what they did. But look at how the line runs. A man was afraid and protected himself instead of his wife, and he got rich doing it. That wealth helped push two families apart. One of them picked the wrong valley. And the valley raised two girls who thought this was how you solve a problem. Choices have long tails. The things we do out of fear and the things we get out of them do not stay in one chapter.

> “Thus were both the daughters of Lot with child by their father. And the firstborn bare a son, and called his name Moab: the same is the father of the Moabites unto this day. And the younger, she also bare a son, and called his name Benammi: the same is the father of the children of Ammon unto this day.” (Genesis 19:36–38)

Both daughters get pregnant. Two boys are born in that cave, and both of them become nations.

The older daughter names her son **Moab**, a name that sounds like the Hebrew for *from father*. She is not hiding what happened. She put it in his name. He becomes the father of the **Moabites**, who settled east of the Dead Sea. The younger names hers **Ben-Ammi**, which means *son of my people*, and he becomes the father of the **Ammonites**, who settled north of Moab, east of the Jordan.

If you keep reading your Bible, you are going to run into these two peoples over and over. They are Israel’s cousins, because they come from Lot, Abraham’s nephew. When Israel is finally marching toward the promised land, God tells Moses to leave them both alone, because He gave that land to the children of Lot. Family is family, even family that started in a cave.

But they are not friendly cousins. Moab’s king Balak is the one who hires a prophet named Balaam to curse Israel in the book of Numbers. The Ammonites go to war with Israel in the book of Judges. Two nations born out of fear and Sodom thinking spend centuries causing trouble for the nation God promised to build from Abraham.

And yet. Hold on to this, because God is not finished with that cave either. Centuries later, a young widow from Moab named Ruth leaves her country, follows her mother in law back to Bethlehem, and says, *thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God* (Ruth 1:16). She marries a man named Boaz, the same Boaz who sat in the gate with ten elders. Their son is Obed. Obed’s son is Jesse. Jesse’s son is David. The line of King David, and through him the line of Jesus, runs through a Moabite woman. God took a nation that started in the worst night of Lot’s life and wrote it into the family tree of the Messiah.

That is what He does. He does not pretend the cave did not happen. He does not call it good. But He does not let it have the last word either.

So here is where Lot’s story leaves us. A man walked out of Sodom with his life and nothing else, and then he found out that Sodom had not finished with him. It was in his fear. It was in his cup. It was in the way his daughters solved a problem. You can leave a place in one night. Getting the place out of you takes longer, and it takes God.

Look at what is in you from the places you have lived. The way you handle fear. The things you reach for when you are grieving. The solutions that look reasonable to you that would make a stranger gasp. That is the Sodom that walked out with you. Name it, and ask God to burn that too.

Tomorrow we leave the cave and go back up the hill to Abraham’s camp, and we find out that the man who prayed for Sodom still has some of his own old habits to deal with. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **What did the place you grew up in, or the place you have been living, teach you to reach for when life falls apart?** Lot’s daughters reached for wine and a plan that broke every boundary. Be honest about what looks reasonable to you that should not.
2. **Abraham was a few days’ walk away, and nobody in the cave thought of him. Who has God put in your life that you keep forgetting to run to?** The answer to their problem was already alive on a hill. Name the person or the place God has given you that you have been ignoring.
3. **What choice did you make out of fear that is still shaping things years later?** Choices have long tails. Trace one of yours back to the fear that started it, and bring that fear to God by name.

## Today's One Thing

**Walk to Mamre**

Lot’s daughters had Abraham a few days away and solved their problem in a cave instead. Do what they did not do.

1. Write down one problem you have been trying to solve alone, your own way, with a plan you would not want anybody to see.
2. Write down the name of one godly person you could actually take it to. A pastor, a mentor, a believing friend. Your Abraham.
3. Call or text them today and set a time to talk. Not someday. Put the day on the calendar before you close this book.

One problem named, one Abraham named, one conversation scheduled. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You brought Lot out of Sodom, and then Sodom came out of Lot, in his fear and his wine and his daughters’ plan. I know that story, because I have lived a version of it. You got me out of places, and the places did not get all the way out of me.

Show me what I carried out. The way I handle fear. The things I reach for when I am grieving. The solutions that look normal to me and would not look normal to You.

**Burn the Sodom that is still in me, Lord, the way You burned the one I left.**

Forgive me for solving my problems in a cave when You had already put people on a hill for me. Give me the humility to walk to Mamre. To ask for help. To take my mess to somebody who knows You, instead of pouring another cup and handling it my way.

And thank You that You are not finished with caves. You took Moab and wrote it into the line of David and the line of Jesus. You can take the worst night of my story and still build something holy out of it. I am trusting You to do that.

Tomorrow we go back to Abraham, and he is still not perfect either. Neither am I. Keep working on both of us.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
