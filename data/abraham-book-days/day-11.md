Start today where we left Lot standing. Yesterday he looked out over the plain of Jordan, saw a valley green as Egypt, and chose it. And he pitched his tent toward Sodom, a city the Bible had already flagged in the plainest words possible: the men of Sodom were wicked and sinners before the LORD exceedingly.

That was yesterday. Today, when the chapter opens, Lot is not camping toward Sodom anymore. He is living inside it.

And Sodom is about to become the most dangerous address in the world. Because far to the east, an empire is on the move, and war is coming to the plain. When it arrives, it will not check who deserves what. It will just take everything, and everyone, including the nephew we have been watching since Ur.

Today we also meet a side of Abram we have never seen. The tent dweller. The altar builder. The old man who gives away first pick. That same man is about to arm his household in the middle of the night and chase four armies across the entire length of the land.

**Today somebody has to go get Lot.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 14

The first verses are heavy with strange king names. Do not try to memorize them. Watch what the kings do, not what they are called.

Find Lot in this chapter, and notice where he is living now compared to yesterday.

Two very different kings come out to meet Abram at the end. Watch what each one offers, and what Abram does with each offer.

**As you read, notice:**

* How many years the five kings served before they rebelled
* Where Lot is living when the war reaches him
* How many trained men Abram takes, and how far he chases
* What Abram refuses to take, and why

And think about this question: **What did living in Sodom cost Lot?**

> “And when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his trained servants… and pursued them unto Dan.” (Genesis 14:14)

# The War and the Rescue

Before we touch the war, get the map back in your head.

Down by the Salt Sea, which we call the Dead Sea, sits the plain Lot chose. Green, well watered, crowded with cities. Five of those cities have their own little kings, and the two names that matter are the two you already know. Sodom and Gomorrah. This is the neighborhood Lot picked, and the Bible has already told us what kind of neighborhood it is.

Now Genesis pulls the camera all the way out, because the plain has a problem nobody mentioned yesterday. It is not free land. Those five cities answer to a foreign power.

Far to the east lives a king named Chedorlaomer. Do not worry about pronouncing him. Just understand the arrangement:

> “Twelve years they served Chedorlaomer, and in the thirteenth year they rebelled.” (Genesis 14:4)

Served means the five kings of the plain paid him. Year after year, for twelve years, tribute flowed east. Think of it as protection money on a national scale. In the thirteenth year, the five kings got tired of paying and stopped. And in the fourteenth year, Chedorlaomer came back to collect, and he did not come alone. He brought three allied kings with him.

So it is four kings against five. And the four are the stronger side. On the march down, they flatten every people in their path, one after another, like a mower going through grass. Then the five kings of the plain come out to fight them in the vale of Siddim, near the Salt Sea. One of those five cities, by the way, is a little town called Bela, which also goes by the name Zoar. File that name away. It comes back later in this book.

The battle goes exactly the way you would expect:

> “And the vale of Siddim was full of slimepits; and the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and fell there; and they that remained fled to the mountain. And they took all the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah, and all their victuals, and went their way.” (Genesis 14:10–11)

**Slimepits** are pits of tar, and that valley was full of them. The armies of the plain break and run, some falling into the tar, the rest scattering into the hills. And the four kings do what conquering armies did. They empty Sodom and Gomorrah. The goods, the food, and the people, marched off as property.

And then verse 12 drops the name this whole chapter has been waiting for:

> “And they took Lot, Abram’s brother’s son, who dwelt in Sodom, and his goods, and departed.” (Genesis 14:12)

Read the middle of that verse again. Who dwelt in Sodom. Yesterday Lot pitched his tent toward Sodom. Today he lives in it. Track the drift:

* He saw a plain that looked like Egypt
* He chose it and moved east
* He pitched his tent toward Sodom
* Now he is living inside its walls

Nobody moves into Sodom in one day. But the direction is plain on the page, and so is the price. When the war came for Sodom, it came for Lot, because Sodom was his address now. He did not do anything wicked in this chapter. He just lived where wickedness lived, and he was carried off with it.

That is how attractive choices work. They rarely ask you to jump. They let you drift, one comfortable step at a time, until the day trouble comes for the thing you moved next to. When you pitch your tent toward something, do not be surprised when you wake up inside it.

Now watch what happens next, because one man got away:

A survivor escapes the captured column and runs west, into the hills, to the terebinth trees of Mamre near Hebron, where Abram has been living since yesterday’s chapter ended. And he tells Abram that Lot is taken.

> “And when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his trained servants, born in his own house, three hundred and eighteen, and pursued them unto Dan.” (Genesis 14:14)

His brother here means his kinsman, his own flesh and blood. That is how the old language works, and it tells you how Abram felt about the nephew. Not my brother’s boy, three tents removed. My brother.

And look at what Abram has been quietly carrying this whole time. Three hundred and eighteen trained men, born in his own household. Now do some basic math with that. If 318 fighting age men were born in his house, then they had mothers. They had fathers. Wives, sisters, brothers, and children of their own. For every soldier in that column, there was a family back at the tents.

So Abram’s household was not a big family. It was a small town on the move, easily a thousand people or more, with herds spread across the hills and its own army inside it. That is what God had grown out of one obedient man while nobody was counting. And all of it stood ready on the worst night of Lot’s life.

He also does not ride alone. Three local brothers, Mamre, Eshcol, and Aner, Amorite chiefs who are allied with Abram, ride with their men beside him.

> “And he divided himself against them, he and his servants, by night, and smote them, and pursued them unto Hobah, which is on the left hand of Damascus. And he brought back all the goods, and also brought again his brother Lot, and his goods, and the women also, and the people.” (Genesis 14:15–16)

**Smote** means struck. Defeated. And feel the size of what just happened. The four kings had just crushed five armies. They were the most dangerous fighting force in the region, marching home fat with plunder. And an old shepherd chased them the whole length of the land, from Hebron up to Dan at the far northern edge, well over a hundred miles. Then he split his men into groups, hit the camp from more than one side in the dark, and drove the survivors all the way past Damascus, outside the land entirely. And he came back with everything. The goods. The women. The people. And Lot.

Do not let the military part hide the family part. Lot is the one who separated. Lot lifted his eyes, took the best land, and drifted into Sodom. Abram owed him nothing, and half of us would have said something like, he made his choice, let him live in it. Abram does not say one word like that. He hears the news and saddles up the same night. Remember Day 2. When family falls, you cover. Abram just covered his nephew with three hundred and eighteen men and a night march.

And was it all Abram’s skill and daring? Hold that question, because someone is about to answer it. On the way home, two kings come out to meet the man of the hour. Watch them closely. They are opposites, and what Abram does with each of them tells you who he has become.

The first is a mystery man:

> “And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God. And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth: and blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand. And he gave him tithes of all.” (Genesis 14:18–20)

Melchizedek is the king of a city called Salem, which many believe is the city that later becomes Jerusalem. And he is something we have not seen in the Bible before now. A **priest**. A priest is a man who stands between people and God, leading worship and bringing offerings. And do not miss whose priest he is. The most high God is not some local idol. It is the same LORD Abram worships. Abram says so himself two verses later, when he calls his God the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth. Same title, same God. Out here, in a land full of idols, Abram just met somebody else who serves Him.

Melchizedek brings bread and wine, supper for an exhausted soldier, and then he blesses Abram. And listen to who gets the credit in that blessing. Blessed be the most high God, *which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand*. There is the answer to our question. Abram marched, planned, and fought, and God delivered. Both things are true at once. Courage did the riding. God gave the victory.

And Abram agrees with every word of it, because he gives Melchizedek **tithes** of all, which means a tenth of everything. You do not hand a tenth of the spoils to God’s priest unless you know exactly where the victory came from.

Then the second king steps up, and the air changes:

> “And the king of Sodom said unto Abram, Give me the persons, and take the goods to thyself.” (Genesis 14:21)

On the surface it sounds generous. By the customs of war, the spoils belonged to the winner anyway. Keep the goods, just give me back my people. Easy money. Abram earned it. Nobody would have blinked.

> “And Abram said to the king of Sodom, I have lift up mine hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth, that I will not take from a thread even to a shoelatchet, and that I will not take any thing that is thine, lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich.” (Genesis 14:22–23)

Not a thread. Not a shoelatchet, which is the strap of a sandal. Not one string from Sodom. And Abram gives the reason out loud: so that the king of Sodom can never walk around saying, I made Abram rich.

Think about where Abram learned that. The last time a pagan king loaded him with wealth, it was Pharaoh, and we watched what came home with that caravan. Abram has seen what it is like to carry riches with another king’s fingerprints on them. Never again. From now on, if Abram is rich, there will be exactly one explanation for it, and His name is not Sodom.

And notice the fairness inside the vow, because Abram is principled, not showy. He adds one exception: *save only that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men which went with me* (Genesis 14:24). His soldiers ate on the campaign, and that stands. And Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre risked their men for this rescue, so they take their rightful share. Abram binds his own hands with the vow. He does not bind everybody else’s.

So step back and look at what this day showed us. It showed us what drifting toward Sodom costs, even for a man who did nothing wicked, because war does not check the guest list. It showed us a loyalty that rides all night for family that chose wrong. It showed us that courage and God’s help are not rivals, because Abram fought and God delivered. And it showed us a man guarding the story of his own blessings, so that no king on earth could ever claim the credit that belongs to God.

Abram rides home with nothing extra in his saddlebags. Just his nephew, his honor, and his God.

And tomorrow, in the quiet after the war, that God comes to him in the night with four words every tired soldier needs to hear, and a promise with stars in it. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **What is your tent pitched toward right now?** Lot went from toward Sodom to inside Sodom without one dramatic decision. Check the direction you are drifting, not just the address you are at.
2. **Who in your life chose wrong and needs a rescue anyway?** Lot picked the plain and left. Abram still rode all night for him. Loyalty does not wait for people to deserve it.
3. **Could anybody but God claim they made you rich?** Abram would not take a sandal strap from Sodom’s king. Look at what you have, and check whose fingerprints are on it.

## Today's One Thing

**Ride for Somebody**

Abram heard bad news about a man who left him, and he saddled up the same night. No lecture. Just a rescue.

1. Write down the name of one person who made choices you warned them about, and is now in trouble.
2. Do one concrete thing for them today. A call, a visit, real help, or praying with them, not just about them.
3. Attach no I told you so. Not one word of it. Help first, and let the lecture die.

One name, one rescue sized act, zero lectures. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You are the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth. Every victory I have ever walked home from was delivered by You.

I have people in Sodom, Father. People I love who drifted somewhere I warned them about, one comfortable step at a time. And I confess that part of me has wanted to say, they made their bed.

**Make me an Abram instead. When the news comes, let me saddle up, not sum up.**

Give me that kind of loyalty. The kind that rides at night, for family that left, without a lecture in its saddlebag. And when I go, go with me, because my courage can do the riding, but only You can give the victory.

And check my own tent, Father. Show me what I am pitched toward. If there is a Sodom on my horizon that I have been calling a nice view, turn my tent around before I wake up inside the walls.

Guard the story of my blessings too. Let no king, no boss, no company, and no man ever be able to stand up and say they are the reason I am blessed. From a thread to a shoelatchet, let everything I have carry Your fingerprints only.

And thank You for the Melchizedeks You send. For bread, and rest, and a blessing on the road when I am too tired to bless myself.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
