Yesterday ended under the stars. God pointed a childless man at the night sky and said, that many.

Then He walked between the pieces alone and bound Himself to the promise.

If there was ever a night to go to sleep believing, that was it.

Now count the years. By the time this chapter opens, Abram and Sarai have lived in Canaan for ten years.

Ten years since God first said great nation.

Ten years of the same tent. Ten years of watching other women in the camp carry babies on their hips.

Ten years of Abram getting older and Sarai getting older and the promise getting no closer.

Ten years is long enough for anybody to start wondering if they heard God right, or if maybe God is waiting on them to do something.

Today the waiting finally wins. Abram and Sarai decide to help the promise along.

And the plan works, which turns out to be the worst part.

Because the thing they build to fix their problem tears their household in half, and sends a pregnant servant girl running into the desert alone.

And that is where God shows up. Not in Abram’s tent.

In the wilderness, next to the one person nobody in this story was thinking about.

**Today we learn what a shortcut costs.**

# The Shortcut and the Well

> “And she called the name of the LORD that spake unto her, Thou God seest me.” (Genesis 16:13)

The chapter opens on the same fact it has been opening on since Day 6:

> “Now Sarai Abram’s wife bare him no children: and she had an handmaid, an Egyptian, whose name was Hagar.” (Genesis 16:1)

**Bare him no children** simply means she had not been able to have a child. Same stone, same place in the ground.

But this time the verse adds a second half, and introduces a second woman.

A **handmaid** here is an enslaved woman serving in the household.

She belongs to Sarai, and she does not have the freedom to say no. Her name is Hagar. And she is Egyptian.

Stop on that word. Where have we been?

Back on Day 9, this family went down to Egypt in a famine, and Pharaoh loaded Abram with gifts, including menservants and maidservants.

Now here is an Egyptian servant girl in Sarai’s tent. It is not hard to connect those dots.

Imagine if Hagar came into this household during that trip to Egypt.

Then someone they brought home from Egypt is about to become the centerpiece of their next attempt to solve God’s promise themselves.

Egypt has a long reach.

Then Sarai speaks:

> “And Sarai said unto Abram, Behold now, the LORD hath restrained me from bearing: I pray thee, go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her. And Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai.” (Genesis 16:2)

Hear her first sentence before you judge her plan. *The LORD hath restrained me from bearing*.

Sarai is not an unbeliever who forgot God exists. She knows exactly who is in charge of her womb. She names Him.

And then, in the very next breath, she builds a workaround.

What she proposes is plain, even if the old words are polite. *Go in unto my maid* means sleep with my servant.

*It may be that I may obtain children by her* means maybe I can get a child through her.

In Sarai’s world this was a known arrangement.

A wife who could not conceive could give her servant to her husband, and a child born to that servant could count as the wife’s child, raised inside her household and family line.

Later in Genesis, Rachel will do the same thing with her servant Bilhah, and Leah with Zilpah.

It was a known household arrangement, and people in that world could see it as a practical solution.

So this is not a crazy plan. That is what makes it dangerous.

It is a reasonable, culturally normal human solution to a real problem, built by a woman who believes God is in control and has decided God must need her help.

And look at Abram. *Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai*. Hearkened means he listened and agreed.

That is all the text gives us.

Not a man chasing a servant girl, just a man going along with his wife’s plan to get the child God promised.

But hold him responsible for what the verse shows. God gave the promise to Abram. Abram stood under the stars.

And when a shortcut was laid in front of him, Genesis records no moment where he stops and asks God whether this is what He meant.

He just agrees.

## Ten Years in the Land

Put this next to the pattern we flagged back in Egypt.

When what God promised stops matching what they can see, this couple reaches for a human solution.

Here it is again, bigger, closer to the promise itself.

> “And Sarai Abram’s wife took Hagar her maid the Egyptian, after Abram had dwelt ten years in the land of Canaan, and gave her to her husband Abram to be his wife.” (Genesis 16:3)

There is the number. Ten years in the land. Ten years since God first said great nation.

Feel the weight of that before you judge anybody in this tent.

A decade of unanswered promise will wear down the strongest faith you know.

And be honest about what happens to Hagar in this verse. Sarai *took* her and *gave* her.

Hagar is handed from one person to another like property, to be a secondary wife for the purpose of producing a baby.

She did not design this plan. Nobody in the text asks her opinion.

Two powerful people are deciding what will happen to her body and her future.

And the plan works:

> “And he went in unto Hagar, and she conceived: and when she saw that she had conceived, her mistress was despised in her eyes.” (Genesis 16:4)

After all those years, after all that waiting, Hagar becomes pregnant.

From a practical standpoint, the workaround did exactly what it was built to do.

If you were keeping score, you would mark it a success.

Then read the second half of the verse. *Her mistress was despised in her eyes*. Hagar starts looking down on Sarai.

And think about why.

In a world where a woman’s worth was measured by children, the servant can do the one thing the mistress never could.

The whole power structure of the tent just flipped. The pregnant servant walks differently now.

And Sarai, who built the plan, is standing inside the damage it caused.

Do not make Hagar a villain here either.

She is a servant who was handed to a man and is now carrying his child, and she is human.

But the shortcut has already done something nobody planned. It did not just produce a baby. It produced contempt.

## My Wrong Be Upon Thee

And now watch the plan eat the people who built it:

> “And Sarai said unto Abram, My wrong be upon thee: I have given my maid into thy bosom; and when she saw that she had conceived, I was despised in her eyes: the LORD judge between me and thee.” (Genesis 16:5)

**My wrong be upon thee** means this is your fault. The wrong being done to me is on your head.

Sarai designed the plan, handed over the servant, and is now blaming Abram for all of it.

And notice who she brings into the argument. *The LORD judge between me and thee*.

She calls God in as the referee after the plan has fallen apart.

Genesis never records this couple asking God about the plan before they ran it.

But once it goes wrong, God gets summoned to pick a side. Let that irony sit.

> “But Abram said unto Sarai, Behold, thy maid is in thy hand; do to her as it pleaseth thee. And when Sarai dealt hardly with her, she fled from her face.” (Genesis 16:6)

And here is Abram at his weakest in this whole book. Not Egypt weak. Quieter than that.

He agreed to the plan, he slept with Hagar, he fathered the child, and now, when the household explodes, he shrugs.

*Thy maid is in thy hand*. Do whatever you want with her.

The one man with the power to protect the most vulnerable person in his camp hands her back to the woman who is angriest at her.

**Dealt hardly with her** means Sarai treated her harshly. Mistreated her.

The text does not describe it, but it was bad enough that a pregnant woman chose the desert over the tent. Hagar runs.

And do not soften this because Abram and Sarai are God’s people.

One of the hard truths of this chapter is that people carrying God’s promise can still hurt somebody badly.

The covenant family just drove a pregnant servant into the wilderness.

## He Knows Her Name

Now the camera leaves the tent and follows her:

> “And the angel of the LORD found her by a fountain of water in the wilderness, by the fountain in the way to Shur. And he said, Hagar, Sarai’s maid, whence camest thou? and whither wilt thou go? And she said, I flee from the face of my mistress Sarai.” (Genesis 16:7–8)

Look at the direction she ran. The way to **Shur** is the desert road that leads toward Egypt.

It is not hard to picture the Egyptian servant trying to get home.

Pregnant, alone, on foot, fleeing mistreatment, on a road pointed toward Egypt.

She stops at a spring, and somebody is waiting for her there.

Read the first word of that verse again. *Found*. The story puts the first move with the visitor.

Hagar did not go looking for God. The angel of the LORD found her.

The household that used her for its plan has broken apart around her, and the God of that household goes out into the desert after the servant girl.

And He knows her name. *Hagar, Sarai’s maid*. In the whole chapter so far, Abram and Sarai never once call her by name.

To them she is my maid, thy maid, the Egyptian. The first voice in this story to say Hagar out loud is God’s.

Then two questions. *Whence camest thou? and whither wilt thou go?* Where did you come from, and where are you going?

Notice she only answers half. I am running from Sarai. She knows exactly what she is fleeing.

She has no answer for where she is headed. Most runaways are like that. The leaving is clear. The destination is not.

## The LORD Hath Heard Thy Affliction

Now the hard verse, and we will not throw it at you without explaining it:

> “And the angel of the LORD said unto her, Return to thy mistress, and submit thyself under her hands. And the angel of the LORD said unto her, I will multiply thy seed exceedingly, that it shall not be numbered for multitude.” (Genesis 16:9–10)

God tells her to go back and place herself under Sarai’s authority. Read that carefully for what it is.

It is a specific word to one woman in one story, in a world where a pregnant servant fleeing alone into the desert faced serious danger.

It is not a rule for every hurting person today to walk back into whatever is hurting them.

And notice that the command does not come alone.

In the same breath, God hands this servant girl a promise that sounds almost exactly like the one He gave Abram under the stars.

Your descendants will be too many to count. God is not sending her back empty. He is sending her back with a future.

> “Behold, thou art with child, and shalt bear a son, and shalt call his name Ishmael; because the LORD hath heard thy affliction.” (Genesis 16:11)

He names her baby. **Ishmael** means God hears.

**Affliction** means her suffering, the harsh treatment she has been living under. So the name is the message.

Every time she says her son’s name for the rest of her life, she will be saying, God heard me.

The servant nobody in the tent listened to was heard by heaven.

Then God describes the boy:

> “And he will be a wild man; his hand will be against every man, and every man’s hand against him; and he shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren.” (Genesis 16:12)

Do not hear wild man as an insult.

The picture behind the words is a wild donkey, an animal nobody owns, free, tough, roaming where it wants, impossible to fence in.

Ishmael will be that kind of man. Independent. Hard to tame.

Living in constant friction with the people around him, right alongside his relatives.

It is a description of the life ahead of him, not a judgment on his worth.

## Thou God Seest Me

And then Hagar does something nobody else in Genesis has done yet. She gives God a name:

> “And she called the name of the LORD that spake unto her, Thou God seest me: for she said, Have I also here looked after him that seeth me?” (Genesis 16:13)

In Hebrew the name is **El Roi**, the God who sees me.

The words that follow in the verse are hard to translate, and Bibles phrase her question differently, but the heart of it is plain.

She is amazed. Have I really just seen the One who sees me, and lived to tell it? Think about who is saying it.

A foreign servant. Pregnant by a man who handed her off. Mistreated by a woman who used her.

Alone at a well in the desert with no plan.

Every powerful person in her life had been deciding what happened to her body and her future.

Imagine how unseen she must have felt.

And out there, God looked. He found her, said her name, heard her suffering, spoke about her son, and gave her a future.

So she names Him for the one thing nobody else had done. You see me.

This is not a side story interrupting Abraham’s story. This is the story. This is who the God of Abraham is.

Back in Egypt, He went after Sarai when she was handed over. Now He goes after Hagar when she is handed over.

The God this family is learning to follow does not lose track of the people their plans run over.

> “Wherefore the well was called Beer-lahai-roi; behold, it is between Kadesh and Bered.” (Genesis 16:14)

**Beer-lahai-roi** means the well of the Living One who sees me. The spring got a name, and the name stuck.

For generations after, people in that desert could point to a well and say, that is where God saw the runaway.

> “And Hagar bare Abram a son: and Abram called his son’s name, which Hagar bare, Ishmael. And Abram was fourscore and six years old, when Hagar bare Ishmael to Abram.” (Genesis 16:15–16)

Hagar went back. And look at the name Abram gives the baby. Ishmael. The exact name God gave Hagar in the wilderness.

Which means the message from the well made it all the way back into Abram’s tent. **Fourscore and six** is eighty six.

Abram is eighty six years old, and for the first time in his life, he is holding a son.

## A Plan That Worked

So step back. The chapter started with a woman who believed God was in control and decided He needed her help anyway.

It ends with another woman learning that the God of this family sees the people everybody else was using.

In between, a plan that worked produced a real son, a real blessing, and real damage. Contempt in the tent.

A marriage fighting. A servant mistreated and running for her life.

The lesson is not never do anything while you wait on God. Abram is going to have plenty to do in this book.

The lesson is narrower and sharper than that.

Faithful action is not the same thing as forcing God’s promise into the shape and timing you have decided it has to take.

They could not make Sarai pregnant by making Hagar pregnant.

Their plan brought a real child into the world, but it did not end the waiting, and it did not protect the people caught inside their solution.

Ishmael is a son God hears and blesses, not a mistake to be dismissed.

And if you stopped reading here, you might think the problem was solved anyway. Abram has a son.

Ishmael will grow up in his tent. God blessed the boy. Case closed.

Then turn the page. The next time Genesis records God speaking, thirteen years have passed and Abram is ninety nine.

Tomorrow, God comes back, and He has a name for Abram, a name for Sarai, and a date. But today is not finished yet.

Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 16

Find the number of years in verse 3. That number is the pressure behind everything in this chapter.

Watch how Sarai talks about God while she is making her plan. She has not forgotten Him. That is what makes it human.

A new person enters the story today, and she is from a country we have been to before. Notice where she is from.

**As you read, notice:**

* 💭 What Sarai says the LORD has done, and what she decides to do about it
* 👂 What Abram says when Sarai proposes her plan
* 🤰 What changes the moment Hagar knows she is pregnant
* ❓ Who finds Hagar in the wilderness, and what He asks her

And think about this question: **What happens to the people inside our shortcuts?**

## Reflection Questions

1. **What promise have you waited on so long that you have started designing a shortcut?** Sarai knew God was in control and built a workaround anyway. Believing and forcing can live in the same heart. Name the shortcut, and think about who it could hurt.
2. **Who is standing inside your plans without ever being asked?** Nobody in the tent asked Hagar anything. Look at the people your solutions are built on top of, and ask what the plan costs them.
3. **Where in your life do you feel like the one nobody is looking at?** God found a runaway servant at a well and said her name. He has not lost track of you either. Thou God seest me.

## Today's One Thing

**Say Their Name**

Abram and Sarai never called Hagar by name. God did. Today, be the one who sees.

1. Think of one person in your daily life who gets treated like part of the furniture. A cashier, a cleaner, a coworker nobody talks to, a family member who always gets overlooked.
2. Today, learn or use their name, look them in the eye, and ask them one real question about their life. Then actually listen to the answer.
3. Before bed, thank God by name for being El Roi, the God who saw you when nobody else did.

One name said, one person seen, one thank you. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You are the God who sees me.

Before I confess anything, I want to thank You for that, because there have been seasons when I was sure nobody was looking.

I know the ten year ache.

I know what it is to believe You are in control and still start building my own way to get what You promised.

I have called it being practical. Today I am admitting what that shortcut cost.

**Forgive me for the shortcuts I built on top of other people.**

Show me who my plans have cost. The people I handed off, talked past, or used to get where I was trying to go.

Give me the courage to go back and see them, say their names, and make it right where I can.

And teach me the difference between faithful action and forcing Your hand. I do not want to sit still and do nothing.

I want to do what You have actually given me to do, and leave the rest in Your timing, even when the waiting gets long.

Thank You for going out to the well. For finding the one everybody else forgot.

For hearing affliction nobody else heard. You did it for Hagar, and You have done it for me more times than I know.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
