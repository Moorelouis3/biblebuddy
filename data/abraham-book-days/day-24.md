She left Ur with him when he did not know where he was going. She stood in Pharaoh’s house because he was afraid.

She waited twenty five years for a baby. She handed her servant to her husband and lived with what that did.

She laughed behind a tent door, and she laughed again in a nursery. She nursed the son of the promise at ninety.

Today Sarah dies.

Isaac is thirty seven years old now, a grown man.

The boy she thought she would never have has had thirty seven years with his mother.

And Abraham, who has walked beside this woman through every chapter of this book, comes in and sits beside her body, and weeps.

Then he has to stand up.

Because a man with a dead wife in a foreign land has a problem nobody wants to think about in that moment.

He does not own one square foot of the ground he is going to bury her in.

And so the second half of today is an old man, grieving, standing in a city gate, buying a cave.

**Today Abraham mourns, and then he buys a piece of the promise.**

# The Cave of Machpelah

> “I am a stranger and a sojourner with you: give me a possession of a buryingplace with you.” (Genesis 23:4)

Yesterday ended on a mountain with a ram in a thicket. Today opens with a number:

> “And Sarah was an hundred and seven and twenty years old: these were the years of the life of Sarah. And Sarah died in Kirjatharba; the same is Hebron in the land of Canaan: and Abraham came to mourn for Sarah, and to weep for her.” (Genesis 23:1–2)

A hundred and twenty seven years. Do the math one more time, because the Bible keeps handing us numbers for a reason.

Sarah was ninety when Isaac was born. So Isaac is thirty seven. Abraham is a hundred and thirty seven.

The boy on the mountain yesterday was about thirteen, so a lot of years have gone by since then.

Sarah got thirty seven years with the son she laughed about.

That is a full life with the child she thought she would never hold.

And notice what the Bible does for her here. Sarah is the only woman in Scripture whose age at death is recorded.

Kings get their years written down. Patriarchs get their years written down. And Sarah. The Bible is honoring her.

She dies at **Kirjatharba**, which the verse tells you is the old name for Hebron. You know Hebron.

That is where the big trees of Mamre are, where Abraham built an altar on Day 10 and fed three strangers on Day 15.

The family has moved back up into the hill country from Beersheba, about twenty seven miles as the crow flies, farther on foot.

We are not told why they moved. We are told that this is where Sarah’s story ends, back at Mamre.

Then two words. *Mourn* and *weep*.

The Hebrew for mourn is the word for wailing out loud over the dead, the sound people in that world made at a death, loud and public and physical.

And weep means exactly what it sounds like. Tears. Abraham comes in to where Sarah’s body is, and he breaks down.

Picture it the way the Bible pictures grief. Think of Jacob, Abraham’s grandson, when he thought Joseph was dead.

He tore his clothes, put on sackcloth, rough cloth like burlap, and refused to be comforted.

That is what mourning looked like in this family. Loud. Visible. All the way through the body.

Abraham is a hundred and thirty seven, and he is sitting beside his wife of a lifetime, and he is wailing.

Do not rush past that. This is the man of faith.

The man who just walked up a mountain with a knife and trusted God with his son. And faith did not keep him from crying.

Faith does not make you numb. He lost the woman who had been with him since Ur, and it hurt.

## A Stranger and a Sojourner

> “And Abraham stood up from before his dead, and spake unto the sons of Heth, saying, I am a stranger and a sojourner with you: give me a possession of a buryingplace with you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight.” (Genesis 23:3–4)

Then he stands up. *From before his dead*.

He has been sitting beside her body, and now he rises, because there is something only he can do.

He is not done grieving. He is a grieving man who has to handle a funeral.

And here is who he has to talk to. **The sons of Heth**. Heth is not a king. Go back to Day 3.

Heth was one of Canaan’s sons in the family of nations, and his descendants became a people living around Hebron.

The King James calls them Hittites.

So the sons of Heth are simply the local people, the community that holds the land around this city.

Listen to how Abraham introduces himself. *I am a stranger and a sojourner with you*. A foreigner. A resident outsider.

This is the man God promised the whole land to, and he is standing in it with a dead wife and nowhere he owns to put her.

He has lived in Canaan for sixty two years. He is wealthy, respected, feared, and he does not own a burial plot.

So he asks. *Give me a possession of a buryingplace*. A **buryingplace** is a tomb, a place to lay the dead.

And he says why with words that are hard to read. *That I may bury my dead out of my sight*.

That is not a man who wants to forget his wife.

That is a man who knows that a body has to be laid to rest, with dignity, soon, and he cannot keep sitting there looking at her.

## A Mighty Prince Among Us

> “And the children of Heth answered Abraham, saying unto him, Hear us, my lord: thou art a mighty prince among us: in the choice of our sepulchres bury thy dead; none of us shall withhold from thee his sepulchre, but that thou mayest bury thy dead.” (Genesis 23:5–6)

The local people answer with respect. *My lord* means sir. And then, *thou art a mighty prince among us*.

Abraham called himself a stranger. They call him a mighty prince. Both are true.

He is a foreigner who has lived among them long enough, with a household big enough and a name big enough, that they see him as a powerful man.

He is the big dog in that part of the hills, and everybody knows it.

So they offer. **Sepulchres** means tombs. Pick the best of our tombs and use it. Nobody will say no to you.

That is a generous and kind thing for a community to say to a grieving neighbor.

> “And Abraham stood up, and bowed himself to the people of the land, even to the children of Heth. And he communed with them, saying, If it be your mind that I should bury my dead out of my sight; hear me, and intreat for me to Ephron the son of Zohar, That he may give me the cave of Machpelah, which he hath, which is in the end of his field; for as much money as it is worth he shall give it me for a possession of a buryingplace amongst you.” (Genesis 23:7–9)

Abraham bows to them. That is respect, not worship.

He is a mighty prince who still bows to the people whose help he needs. Then he gets specific.

**Communed** means he talked it over with them. *If it be your mind* means if you are willing.

And *intreat for me* means speak to him on my behalf.

He has a particular spot in mind.

There is a man named Ephron who owns a field, and at the end of that field is a cave called **Machpelah**.

A cave in that country made a natural tomb, a chamber in the rock where a body could be laid and the entrance closed.

Abraham wants that cave. And look at how he wants it. *For as much money as it is worth*. Full price. Not borrowed.

Not a favor. Bought.

## At the Gate of His City

> “And Ephron dwelt among the children of Heth: and Ephron the Hittite answered Abraham in the audience of the children of Heth, even of all that went in at the gate of his city, saying, Nay, my lord, hear me: the field give I thee, and the cave that is therein, I give it thee; in the presence of the sons of my people give I it thee: bury thy dead.” (Genesis 23:10–11)

Now notice where this is happening.

*In the audience of the children of Heth, even of all that went in at the gate of his city*. The gate. Remember Day 17.

The city gate was where business got done in public, with the elders and the townspeople as witnesses.

Boaz settled Ruth’s future at a gate. Lot sat in one.

And here is Abraham, in the gate of Hebron, negotiating in front of everybody who walked through it.

This is a public, legal transaction, and the whole town is the witness.

Ephron answers big. Not just the cave. The field and the cave. *I give it thee*. In front of everybody.

Now, hear that the way the people in the gate heard it. In that world, this was how a polite negotiation started.

You said take it, I give it, and everybody understood you were opening a conversation about price, not handing over your land for nothing.

Some Bibles translate Ephron’s word as sell instead of give, because that is what the exchange is really about.

Ephron is not a liar and he is not a con man. He is doing business the courteous way, and a price is coming.

> “And Abraham bowed down himself before the people of the land. And he spake unto Ephron in the audience of the people of the land, saying, But if thou wilt give it, I pray thee, hear me: I will give thee money for the field; take it of me, and I will bury my dead there.” (Genesis 23:12–13)

Abraham bows again, and he says it a second time. I will pay for the field. Take the money from me.

He will not accept it as a gift. Think back to Day 11.

He would not take a shoelatchet from the king of Sodom, because he did not want that king able to say he made Abraham rich.

This is a different situation, and Abraham has taken plenty of gifts in his life, from Pharaoh and from Abimelech.

But for this, for the ground his wife will lie in, he wants something a gift cannot give him.

He wants it to be his, with no strings, no favor owed, nothing anybody can take back later. He wants ownership.

> “And Ephron answered Abraham, saying unto him, My lord, hearken unto me: the land is worth four hundred shekels of silver; what is that betwixt me and thee? bury therefore thy dead.” (Genesis 23:14–15)

There is the price, said the courteous way. Four hundred shekels of silver. *What is that betwixt me and thee?*

What is that between friends? Just bury your wife. Smooth. And now you understand how the dance works.

Take it, no I will pay, well it is worth four hundred but what is that between us.

Everybody in that gate knew exactly what had just happened. A price had been named.

## Four Hundred Shekels of Silver

> “And Abraham hearkened unto Ephron; and Abraham weighed to Ephron the silver, which he had named in the audience of the sons of Heth, four hundred shekels of silver, current money with the merchant.” (Genesis 23:16)

Abraham pays. And catch how he pays. He *weighed* the silver. A **shekel** was a weight, not a coin.

There were no coins yet. You put silver on a scale and weighed out the agreed amount.

Four hundred shekels comes to about ten pounds of silver, roughly four and a half kilos.

*Current money with the merchant* means it was weighed to the standard traders used, good silver, full weight, the kind any merchant would accept.

Abraham did not pay with light weights or bad metal. He paid clean, in public.

How much is four hundred shekels? Put it next to a number you already know.

On Day 20, Abimelech paid Abraham a thousand shekels of silver to clear Sarah’s name.

That was two and a half times what this whole property cost.

So the king paid Abraham two and a half fields to fix one lie.

Later in the Bible, Joseph gets sold by his brothers for twenty shekels, and King David buys a threshing floor and the oxen that go with it for fifty.

Prices move around across the centuries, so do not turn these into a fixed price list. But you can feel the scale.

Four hundred shekels was serious money for a serious piece of land, and Abraham paid every bit of it.

> “And the field of Ephron, which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field, and the cave which was therein, and all the trees that were in the field, that were in all the borders round about, were made sure Unto Abraham for a possession in the presence of the children of Heth, before all that went in at the gate of his city.” (Genesis 23:17–18)

Now the Bible reads like a property deed, and that is on purpose.

The field of Ephron, which means Ephron’s field, the man who owned it.

In Machpelah, which is the place, the location, near Mamre. The field. The cave in it. Every tree in the field.

All the way to the borders. **Made sure** means legally settled, confirmed, done.

Unto Abraham, for a possession, in front of the sons of Heth, before everybody who went in at the gate.

There was no paper deed. The gate was the deed.

The whole town saw the silver weighed and heard the terms, and that memory was what made the land Abraham’s.

This is exactly what we saw at Beersheba two days ago with the seven lambs.

A foreigner in somebody else’s land does not rely on goodwill. He secures what matters, in public, so it holds.

> “And after this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah before Mamre: the same is Hebron in the land of Canaan. And the field, and the cave that is therein, were made sure unto Abraham for a possession of a buryingplace by the sons of Heth.” (Genesis 23:19–20)

And after this, he buries her.

In the cave, in his own field, near the trees of Mamre where she once stood behind a tent door and laughed.

The chapter closes by saying it one more time. Made sure. Abraham’s possession. The deal held.

## One Grave at a Time

Now step back and think about what just happened. God promised Abraham this whole land.

Day 7, Day 10, Day 12, Day 14, Day 23. All of it, to him and to his descendants forever.

And at a hundred and thirty seven years old, the only piece of it he actually owns is a field with a cave in it that he paid ten pounds of silver for.

One field. Bought at full price. To bury his wife.

That is not the promise failing. That is the promise starting.

Abraham will never own more than this field in his lifetime.

But he puts Sarah in the ground in the land God said was his, as an owner, not a guest.

He is planting his family in the promise, one grave at a time.

And that cave becomes the family tomb. Abraham will be buried there beside her. Then Isaac and Rebekah.

Then Jacob and Leah. Jacob’s sons will carry his body all the way up from Egypt to lay him in this cave.

Three couples, three generations of the promise, in a field bought at a city gate on the worst day of Abraham’s life.

Not everybody in the family ends up there. Rachel is buried on the road to Bethlehem, and Joseph’s bones go to Shechem.

But the cave of Machpelah is where the fathers and mothers of Israel lie, and it started with Sarah.

So look at the whole day. A man of faith lost his wife and wailed over her, because faith does not stop tears.

Then he stood up, called himself a stranger, bowed to the people of the land, and bought one field at full price in front of the whole town, so that the first piece of the promised land his family ever owned would be the place where they laid their dead.

Grief and faith, in the same chapter, in the same man.

Tomorrow, Abraham turns to the last piece of unfinished business in his life.

Isaac is a grown man with no wife, and the promise cannot go anywhere without one. But today is not finished yet.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 23

Sarah is the only woman in the whole Bible whose age at death is written down.

Notice how the chapter opens with her years.

This chapter is mostly a conversation at a city gate. Remember what gates were for from Day 17.

Watch how many times Abraham refuses to take the land for free.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🚶 What Abraham calls himself in verse 4
* 👑 What the local people call Abraham in verse 6
* 💰 The price Ephron names, and how he says it
* 🌳 Everything the final deal includes in verse 17

And think about this question: **Why does a man who was promised the whole land pay full price for one field?**

## Reflection Questions

1. **Abraham wept out loud over Sarah and then stood up to do what had to be done. Which of those two is harder for you, the weeping or the standing up?** Faith did not keep him from tears, and tears did not keep him from acting. Be honest about which half you skip.
2. **Where in your life are you living on somebody’s goodwill when you should be securing what matters?** Abraham would not bury his wife on a favor. He paid full price in public so it would hold. Name what you have left unsettled.
3. **Abraham owned one field out of a whole promised land, and he planted his family in it anyway. What small piece of God’s promise can you take hold of today instead of waiting for all of it?** One field was enough to start. Find yours.

**Honor Your Sarah**

## Today's Prayer

Father,

Thank You for Sarah.

For a woman who left home with a man who did not know where he was going, and stayed through every chapter until the end.

Thank You that You wrote her years down. You do not forget the people who walk with Your people.

Thank You that Abraham wept. Thank You that faith in You does not mean pretending nothing hurts.

When I lose someone, let me grieve the way he did, all the way through, and let me know that You are not disappointed in my tears.

**And then help me stand up from before my dead and do what has to be done.**

Teach me to secure what matters.

Not to live on favors and goodwill for the things that have to hold, but to settle them plainly, pay what they cost, and make them sure.

And give me Abraham’s patience with Your promise.

He owned one field out of a whole land, and he planted his family in it.

Show me the one field You have given me right now, and let me take hold of it instead of waiting for everything.

For every person who has walked beside me through more chapters than I deserved, thank You.

Help me tell them while they can still hear it.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
