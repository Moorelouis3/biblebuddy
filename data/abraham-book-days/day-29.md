For twenty eight days we have followed Abraham.

Today, for the first time, the camera leaves him alone and goes to his son.

Isaac is forty years old. Three years ago he lost his mother.

He has been living down in the dry south country, and the Bible gives us a quiet picture of him.

A man walking out into a field alone at the end of the day, thinking.

He does not know a caravan is coming over the hill.

He does not know his father sent a servant five hundred miles to find him a wife.

He does not know a girl named Rebekah said I will go.

He looks up, and there are camels on the horizon.

This is only six verses. Read them slowly.

They are the end of the servant’s errand, the beginning of a marriage, and the first time in this whole book that the Bible stops and tells you plainly that a man loved his wife.

**Today the promise gets a mother, and a grieving son gets comfort.**

# Isaac Meets Rebekah

> “And Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent… and he loved her: and he was comforted after his mother’s death.” (Genesis 24:67)

Yesterday ended with Rebekah on a camel riding out of her hometown. Today picks up at the other end of that road:

> “And Isaac came from the way of the well Lahairoi; for he dwelt in the south country.” (Genesis 24:62)

Isaac. We have barely heard him speak since the mountain. Now the Bible tells us where he has been living.

The south country, the Negev, the dry land down toward the desert.

Abraham buried Sarah up at Hebron, but Isaac has been living farther south.

And look at the well he is coming from. **Lahairoi**. Go back to Day 13.

That is Beer-lahai-roi, the well of the Living One who sees me.

The place where a pregnant, runaway Egyptian servant girl named Hagar met God in the desert and gave Him a name.

The God who sees me. Hagar’s well.

Think about that for a second. Hagar was the shortcut. Ishmael was the son Abraham had to let go.

And now Isaac, the son of the promise, is living by the well where God found Hagar.

The Bible does not explain why he is there, and it does not need to. It just lets you see it.

The place where God saw a servant girl nobody else saw is now part of the story of the son everybody was waiting for.

God’s mercy on the outsider and God’s promise to the heir end up at the same well.

## The Camels Were Coming

> “And Isaac went out to meditate in the field at the eventide: and he lifted up his eyes, and saw, and, behold, the camels were coming.” (Genesis 24:63)

**Eventide** is evening, the end of the day, the sun going down. And Isaac goes out to **meditate** in the field.

That word is a rare one in Hebrew, and people have read it a few ways. Some say pray. Some say think, reflect.

Some say simply walk, take the air. However you read it, the picture is the same.

A quiet man, alone in a field at sunset, three years after his mother died, with something on his mind.

And then he lifts up his eyes, and there are camels on the horizon. He has no idea what that caravan is carrying.

## It Is My Master

> “And Rebekah lifted up her eyes, and when she saw Isaac, she lighted off the camel. For she had said unto the servant, What man is this that walketh in the field to meet us? And the servant had said, It is my master: therefore she took a vail, and covered herself.” (Genesis 24:64–65)

Now the same words from the other side. Rebekah lifts up her eyes. She sees a man walking across a field toward them.

And she **lighted off** the camel, which just means she got down. Dismounted.

Not a dramatic leap, just a young woman getting off her camel because she has seen something that matters.

And she asks the servant, who is that? *It is my master*.

The servant has been calling Abraham his master this whole trip. Now he says it about Isaac. The son is the heir.

Abraham has given him everything, and the servant knows who he answers to now.

So she takes a **vail**, a veil, and covers herself.

People have long understood that as the custom for a bride meeting her future husband, a sign of modesty and respect before the man she is about to marry.

Do not picture a modern wedding veil or a ceremony.

Picture a young woman, at the end of a long road, realizing that the man walking toward her is the one all of this was for, and covering her face before she meets him.

Feel the moment. They have never seen each other. He did not know she existed this morning.

She said yes to him weeks ago without a face to put to the name.

And now they are looking at each other across a field at sunset.

> “And the servant told Isaac all things that he had done.” (Genesis 24:66)

The servant tells Isaac everything. The oath. The prayer. The well. The camels. The family. I will go.

You have heard it three times now, so the Bible does not repeat it.

But picture Isaac hearing it for the first time, standing in that field, learning that his father sent a man five hundred miles, that God answered a prayer before it was finished, and that the girl on the camel watered ten animals for a stranger and then left her whole life to come to him.

That is the story he hears before he ever says a word to her.

## And He Loved Her

> “And Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent, and took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her: and he was comforted after his mother’s death.” (Genesis 24:67)

Now the last verse, and I want to take it one piece at a time, because there are five things in it and every one matters.

First. *Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent*. Sarah has been dead three years. Her tent is still standing.

And Isaac takes Rebekah into it. Think about what that says.

The tent of the first mother of this family, the woman who laughed and waited and nursed the promise at ninety, is empty.

And now the next woman walks into it. Rebekah is stepping into Sarah’s place.

Not as a replacement for a mother, but as the matriarch of the next generation.

The mother of the covenant family’s future. That is what the tent means.

Second. *Took Rebekah, and she became his wife*. In that world, this is how a marriage happened. The family agreed.

The gifts were given. The blessing was spoken. The bride traveled.

And then the man took her into his tent and she became his wife. No priest, no ceremony, no rings.

The agreement and the blessing and the coming together were the wedding.

The Bible is telling you plainly that the marriage was completed.

Third, and this is the one I want you to slow down on. *And he loved her*.

Hear how rare that is. We have spent twenty eight days with Abraham and Sarah.

We watched them travel together, scheme together, fear together, wait together, fight about Hagar, laugh at the same promise, and we watched him weep over her body.

Their marriage is all over this book. But the Bible never once stops and says, Abraham loved Sarah.

You can see it in his grief. It is there. But the word is not.

Then Isaac meets a girl in a field, and the Bible says it straight out. He loved her. This was an arranged marriage.

A servant picked her at a well. And the Bible wants you to know that inside that arrangement, there was real affection.

Rebekah was not a baby machine for the promise. She was a woman her husband loved.

## He Was Comforted

Fourth. *And he was comforted after his mother’s death*. Do the math once more. Sarah died when Isaac was thirty seven.

He marries at forty. Three years. For three years, this man has been walking out into fields at evening, thinking.

He lost his mother, the only mother he ever had, the woman who waited ninety years for him.

And the Bible says Rebekah comforted him.

Read that word carefully. **Comforted**. Not replaced. Rebekah did not fill the hole Sarah left. Nobody does that.

His mother was still gone, and three years later it still hurt.

But God brought him somebody to love, and loving her eased the grief. That is what comfort is. Not the end of the loss.

Somebody to carry it with you.

And fifth, step back and see what the Bible has done with this chapter. It is the longest chapter in Genesis.

And it ends not with the promise, not with the land, not with a covenant, but with a man loving his wife and being comforted.

The great story of nations and stars and blessing comes down, at the end of Abraham’s life, to a tent, a man, a woman, and love.

That is where God was taking all of it.

## He Comforts His People

So look at the whole road. An old man made a servant swear with his hand under his thigh.

The servant prayed at a well and God answered before he finished. A girl watered ten camels and said I will go.

And at the end of five hundred miles, a grieving son looked up from a field and saw the answer coming on a camel.

That is how God works, and I want you to see it in your own life.

He answers the prayers of the old man and the servant, and He heals the grief of the son, all with the same caravan.

He does not just keep His promises. He comforts His people while He is keeping them.

Tomorrow is the last day.

Abraham, the man we have walked beside for a month, comes to the end of his hundred and seventy five years, and two sons stand at his grave.

But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 24

The first verse names a well you have seen before. Day 13. Think about who met God there.

Both Isaac and Rebekah lift up their eyes in this passage. Notice what each one sees.

The last verse has five things in it, one after another. Read it like a list.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🌅 What Isaac is doing when the camels appear
* 👰 What Rebekah does the moment she learns who he is
* ⛺ Whose tent Isaac brings her into
* 💗 The two words at the very end of the chapter

And think about this question: **What does it mean to be comforted without being replaced?**

## Reflection Questions

1. **Isaac was walking in a field, grieving, when the answer came over the hill. Where are you still grieving something, and have you given up on comfort?** Three years is a long time to walk in fields. God sent camels. Do not assume He is finished with your sadness.
2. **The Bible says plainly that Isaac loved Rebekah. Do the people closest to you know it plainly, or do they have to read it in your grief?** Abraham’s love for Sarah only shows up at her funeral. Say yours while they can hear it.
3. **Rebekah walked into Sarah’s tent and took her place without erasing her. Who are you stepping in for right now, and how do you honor the one who came before?** Comfort does not replace. It carries. Think about what that means for your role.

## Today's One Thing

**Say It Plainly**

The Bible says it in three words. He loved her. Make sure your people have heard it that plainly from you.

1. Write down the name of the person closest to you. A spouse, a child, a parent, a friend.
2. Today, tell them you love them in a way that is not routine. Look at them. Say why. Do not let it be a reflex at the end of a phone call.
3. Then write down the name of one grieving person you know, and do one thing today that comforts without trying to fix. A visit, a meal, sitting with them.

One love said plainly, one grief carried with someone. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You brought a caravan over a hill at sunset for a man who was walking in a field with his grief.

Thank You that You see the people who are still sad three years later. You saw Hagar at that well.

You saw Isaac in that field. You see me.

You do not just keep Your promises. You comfort Your people while You keep them. Thank You for that.

I needed to hear it.

**Teach me to love plainly, Lord, so nobody has to read it in my grief.**

For the people closest to me, let me say it while they can hear it.

Let the word be in my mouth, not just in my actions, the way the Bible put it in Isaac’s story.

And for the ones I know who are walking in fields right now, make me a comfort. Not a fixer.

Not a replacement for what they lost. Somebody who carries it with them.

Thank You for Rebekah. For a woman who stepped into Sarah’s tent and became the mother of the promise.

Keep raising up people who say I will go, and keep bringing them to the ones who are waiting in the field.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
