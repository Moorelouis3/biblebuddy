Abram is home from the war. The kings are beaten, Lot has been rescued, and the king of Sodom just watched Abram turn down his goods. If you only read the headlines, Abram is having the best week of his life.

But think about what it feels like to be Abram once the dust settles. He just made enemies of four kings who will know his name now. He walked away from a fortune on principle. And underneath all of it sits the same quiet ache that has followed him since Haran. God keeps promising him descendants. He is still a childless old man married to a barren woman, living in a land that belongs to other people.

So what do you do when God has promised you something, and years go by, and from where you are standing it looks less possible every day?

Today Abram finally says the problem out loud. And God answers him with the stars, with a strange ceremony in the dark, and with a promise so serious that God Himself walks into the middle of it.

**Today God binds Himself to a promise that looks impossible.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 15

Abram is going to put his childlessness into words before God. Listen to how honestly he speaks.

Halfway through, animals get cut in half and laid out on the ground. It will look strange. Keep reading. We will walk through it together.

Watch who walks between the pieces at the end, and who does not.

**As you read, notice:**

* The first two words God says to Abram
* What Abram says he does not have
* What God tells him to count
* How many years God says Abram’s family will suffer before coming home

And think about this question: **What can Abram actually do to make this promise come true?**

> “Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars, if thou be able to number them… So shall thy seed be.” (Genesis 15:5)

# The Stars and the Covenant

The chapter opens with the words every soldier wants to hear when the fighting is over:

> “After these things the word of the LORD came unto Abram in a vision, saying, Fear not, Abram: I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward.” (Genesis 15:1)

After these things. After the war. After the rescue. After Abram told the king of Sodom he would not take a shoelace. God’s first two words are *Fear not*, which tells you something. You do not say fear not to a man who is relaxed.

Put yourself in his sandals. He just attacked and humiliated four kings. Kings do not forget things like that. It is not hard to imagine Abram lying awake wondering when the retaliation comes. And he just walked away from a mountain of wealth that was his by right. A man can second guess a decision like that in the dark.

So listen to how God answers both worries at once. *I am thy shield*. You are worried about the kings? I am the one standing between you and them. *And thy exceeding great reward*. You turned down Sodom’s money? Good. I am your reward. Not the stuff. Me.

And then Abram puts his deepest question into words. He answers God honestly.

> “And Abram said, Lord GOD, what wilt thou give me, seeing I go childless, and the steward of my house is this Eliezer of Damascus? And Abram said, Behold, to me thou hast given no seed: and, lo, one born in my house is mine heir.” (Genesis 15:2–3)

Hear what he is really saying. God, You keep talking about rewards and promises. What good is any of it to me? I have no child. He has been carrying this since Day 7. God said great nation, and Abram went. God said all families of the earth, and Abram built altars. But years have gone by, and the tent is still quiet. Today he finally says the thing out loud. *To me thou hast given no seed*. You have given me no child.

And he brings up a man named Eliezer, the steward of his house, which means the head servant who ran everything. This is Abram doing the math. If I die without a son, the man who runs my house looks like the one who ends up with everything. That is not bitterness. That is a man looking at his real life and asking how God’s words could possibly fit inside it.

Most of us know that prayer. Most of us have one thing. The one thing we have wanted so long that we almost stopped saying it. For Abram, it was a child. Sarai was barren. He was getting older every year. And God kept promising something that required the one thing he did not have.

Think about the world Abram came from, too. Ur was full of gods, and plenty of them were gods people prayed to about exactly this. Children. Fertility. Barren wombs. It is not hard to picture a younger Abram and Sarai, back in that pagan city, trying whatever their neighbors tried. Offerings. Prayers to a statue. Years of hoping. And nothing.

Now watch what the real God does with the same request:

> “And he brought him forth abroad, and said, Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars, if thou be able to number them: and he said unto him, So shall thy seed be.” (Genesis 15:5)

First God clears up the Eliezer question. Your servant will not be your heir. A son from your own body will. Then He takes Abram outside the tent, under the open night sky, and says, count them. *Tell the stars* is old English for count the stars. Try it.

And here is the scale of what just happened. Abram asked for one child. God pointed at the sky. Abram could only see the empty place in his tent. God could already see Israel. Twelve tribes, kings, prophets, a nation, and a family line that would one day lead to Jesus. All of it was already in that sky while Abram was still worrying about one baby.

That is not a promise that God gives everybody more than they ask for. Do not read it that way. It is a picture of how different His view is from ours. We stare at the one missing piece. He sees the whole story the missing piece belongs to.

Imagine the difference between hoping for a pregnancy and hearing the God Most High promise a nation. Abram longs for a child. God’s promise reaches generations beyond him.

And then comes one of the most important verses in the whole Bible:

> “And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness.” (Genesis 15:6)

Look at what Abram could not do. He could not make himself younger. He could not make Sarai fertile. He could not build a nation out of nothing or clear a land full of armies or control what would happen four hundred years after he died. The whole promise was out of his hands.

So what did he do? He believed. He took God at His word. And God *counted it to him for righteousness*. Righteousness means being right with God, standing clean before Him. And counted means God credited it to his account. Abram did not earn his standing with God by doing great things. He received it by trusting God with the thing he could not do. That single verse gets quoted again and again in the rest of the Bible, because it is the pattern for how anybody gets right with God. Not by making it happen. By believing the One who can.

But Abram has a second problem, and it is just as impossible as the first:

> “And he said unto him, I am the LORD that brought thee out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give thee this land to inherit it. And he said, Lord GOD, whereby shall I know that I shall inherit it?” (Genesis 15:7–8)

The land. God promised it twice already. But Abram did not arrive in an empty field with a for sale sign. He is a foreigner living in tents on land that belongs to walled cities with their own kings and their own armies. He cannot walk up to the gates of Hebron and announce that God gave him the place. So he asks the question any of us would ask. How will I know?

And God answers with the strangest scene in the chapter:

> “Take me an heifer of three years old, and a she goat of three years old, and a ram of three years old, and a turtledove, and a young pigeon. And he took unto him all these, and divided them in the midst, and laid each piece one against another: but the birds divided he not.” (Genesis 15:9–10)

If you have never read this before, you are probably asking what on earth is happening. A **heifer** is a young female cow. A she goat is a female goat. A **ram** is a grown male sheep. Then a turtledove and a young pigeon, two small birds. Abram kills the animals, cuts the three big ones in half, and lays the halves across from each other, so that a path runs down the middle between the pieces. The two birds he leaves whole.

Picture it. Bloody halves of a cow, a goat, and a ram, lined up in two rows on the ground, with a walkway running between them.

The scene resembles ancient covenant ceremonies, where divided animals pictured the penalty for breaking an agreement. Walking between the pieces carried a solemn message. May what happened to these animals happen to me if I break my word. In their language, you did not make a covenant. You *cut* one. So when God asks for the animals, He is telling Abram, you want to know for certain? I am about to make this official in the most binding way your world knows.

Then Abram waits:

> “And when the fowls came down upon the carcases, Abram drove them away. And when the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell upon Abram; and, lo, an horror of great darkness fell upon him.” (Genesis 15:11–12)

**Fowls** here are birds of prey, vultures circling the meat. Abram spends the afternoon chasing them off and guarding the pieces, waiting for God to do whatever God is going to do. And as the sun drops, a deep sleep takes him, and something worse than sleep. *An horror of great darkness*. A heavy, terrifying blackness settles over him. The mood of the whole chapter changes here, because God is about to show Abram something hard about his family’s future.

> “Know of a surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them; and they shall afflict them four hundred years; and also that nation, whom they shall serve, will I judge: and afterward shall they come out with great substance.” (Genesis 15:13–14)

In plain words: your descendants will live as foreigners in somebody else’s country. God describes a long future of foreign residence, service, and oppression, and He puts a span on it, four hundred years. Then I will judge the nation that did it, and your family will walk out rich.

If you know the Bible at all, you just heard the whole book of Exodus in two verses. The land that is not theirs is Egypt. The family line runs like this. Abram has Isaac. Isaac has Jacob, who is renamed Israel. Jacob’s family moves to Egypt during a famine. There the family grows into a people. Egypt enslaves them. In time God raises up Moses, judges Egypt with plagues, and brings Israel out loaded with Egyptian silver and gold, headed back toward this very land. Standing in the dark beside the cut animals, Abram just heard God preview the next four centuries.

And then, in the middle of all that heaviness, God says something tender:

> “And thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace; thou shalt be buried in a good old age.” (Genesis 15:15)

Do not skim that. Think about who is hearing it. This is the man who was so afraid of being killed in Egypt that he asked his wife to call herself his sister. This is the man who just rode into battle against four kings and probably expected them to come for him. And God tells him, you are going to die in peace, as an old man. Not next year at the end of some king’s sword. Old. At peace. Imagine what that does to a man who has spent years afraid of how his story might end. God just told him how it ends.

> “But in the fourth generation they shall come hither again: for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.” (Genesis 15:16)

God gives another reason for the delay. The fourth generation points ahead to a later generation returning to this land. And the verse ties their return to the people already living there. The **Amorites** were one of the main peoples living in Canaan, and their name often stands for everybody in the land. Their **iniquity** is their wickedness, their sin. And God says it is not yet full. In everyday language: the people living in this land are doing wrong, but they have not yet reached the point where I am going to bring judgment on them. I am giving them time.

So two clocks are running at once. Israel is waiting in Egypt to become a nation. And Canaan is on God’s timetable too, being given room before judgment comes. Abram’s family does not get the land immediately, because God is being patient with the people already on it. That does not answer every question about suffering, but it shows one important part of the delay. The delay is not God forgetting. It is God being fair to everybody in the story.

And now the moment the whole day has been building toward:

> “And it came to pass, that, when the sun went down, and it was dark, behold a smoking furnace, and a burning lamp that passed between those pieces.” (Genesis 15:17)

Full dark now. Abram is still in his deep sleep. And a **smoking furnace**, a firepot pouring out smoke, and a **burning lamp**, a blazing torch, move down the path between the cut animals. Smoke and fire. That is how God shows up in this Bible, in a burning bush and a pillar of fire. The presence of God Himself is walking between the pieces.

Now catch the striking feature of this covenant scene. Abram does not walk between the pieces. Only God does.

Remember what walking through meant. May what happened to these animals happen to me if I break this promise. God walks that path alone. He puts the whole weight of the covenant on Himself. Abram is asleep on the ground. He does not promise anything, sign anything, or swear anything. God is saying, this promise does not rest on you keeping up your end. It rests on Me. If it fails, it fails on Me, and I do not fail.

That is the answer to the whole chapter. Abram cannot create a son. He cannot build a nation. He cannot clear the land. And God says, I know. I never asked you to. Watch Me take responsibility for all of it.

> “In the same day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, Unto thy seed have I given this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates.” (Genesis 15:18)

A **covenant** is a binding promise, the most serious kind of agreement there is. We met the word in Day 1, when God made one with Noah and hung a rainbow over it. This is the next great covenant in the Bible, and notice that God is not promising new things. He has been promising Abram land and descendants since Day 7. Today He is making it official, sealing it with fire and smoke and blood.

And look at the size of the land. From the river of Egypt, a southern boundary whose exact location is still debated, all the way up to the Euphrates, the great river far to the north and east. God is not giving Abram a campsite. He is drawing borders around a whole country.

Then the chapter closes with a list of names that most readers skip:

> “The Kenites, and the Kenizzites, and the Kadmonites, and the Hittites, and the Perizzites, and the Rephaims, and the Amorites, and the Canaanites, and the Girgashites, and the Jebusites.” (Genesis 15:19–21)

Do not skip them, because they are the whole point. These are the ten peoples living in the land God just promised. Here is who they were:

* The Kenites, a people connected elsewhere in the Bible with the southern country and with Moses’s father in law.
* The Kenizzites, a small clan later connected to Caleb’s family in Judah.
* The Kadmonites, a name tied to the east, a people whose exact identity and home are uncertain.
* The Hittites, named here among the peoples of the land. Abraham will later buy a burial cave from the sons of Heth.
* The Perizzites, village people of the open country. We met them when Abram and Lot split.
* The Rephaims, a people remembered as giants, living on both sides of the Jordan.
* The Amorites, the hill country people, so widespread their name often covers everybody in the land.
* The Canaanites, Canaan’s people from Day 3, living mostly in the lowlands and along the coast.
* The Girgashites, a Canaanite tribe whose exact home has been lost to history.
* The Jebusites, the people who held the hill city of Jebus, which later becomes Jerusalem.

Ten peoples. Walled cities. Armies. Giants. God names every one of them on purpose, so that nobody can say He promised an empty field. The land is full, and He is promising it anyway.

So look at where Abram started this chapter. No son. No nation. No land he could claim. Ten peoples sitting on the promise. A future he could not control for one day, let alone four hundred years. He was looking at what he had, and from there the promise looked impossible.

And God answered by telling him what God would do. He pointed at the stars. He showed him the future. He walked between the pieces alone and bound Himself to every word.

The promise looked impossible because Abram was looking at what he had. God was telling him what God would do. Abram could only see the missing child in his house. God could already see Israel.

That is still the difference tonight, between what you can see from where you stand and what God has already said He will do.

By tomorrow’s scene, this family has spent ten years in Canaan, and they decide to help the promise along. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **What is your one thing, the thing you have wanted so long you almost stopped saying it to God?** Abram finally said his out loud. God did not punish the honesty. He answered it with stars.
2. **Where has a missing piece made it hard to trust what God has actually promised?** Abram saw one empty tent. God saw Israel. Bring Him your longing, and ask for help trusting Him with the outcome.
3. **God walked between the pieces alone. What promise have you been trying to carry that was never yours to carry?** Abram was asleep when the covenant was sealed. Some things God does not want your help with. He wants your trust.

God took Abram outside the tent to change what he was looking at. Do the same tonight.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You are my shield and my exceeding great reward. Before I ask You for anything tonight, I want to say that You are enough.

But I am going to be honest like Abram was. You know my one thing. You know how long I have carried it, and how quiet I have gotten about it. From where I am standing, it still looks impossible.

**Help me trust what You have promised, even while I bring You what I long for.**

Take me outside my tent, Father. I have been staring at the missing piece for so long that I have forgotten to look up. Show me the sky. Show me that You see a whole story where I only see a gap.

And thank You for walking between the pieces alone. Thank You that the promise does not rest on my strength, my timing, or my ability to make anything happen. You carry it. You keep it. You have never once broken Your word.

Give me the patience of a man who was told his family would wait four hundred years and still believed. And give me the peace of a man who was told how his story ends.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
