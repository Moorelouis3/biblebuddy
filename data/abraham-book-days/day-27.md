Rebekah just ran into her mother’s tents with gold on her nose, gold on her wrists, and a story about a stranger and ten camels at the well. Now somebody in that family is going to run back out.

His name is Laban. Remember that name. He is Rebekah’s brother, and if you keep reading your Bible, you are going to spend a lot of time with him. Years from now, Rebekah’s son Jacob is going to work for this man for twenty years and get cheated more than once. Today he is just a brother who saw the gold.

What follows is a dinner where the guest refuses to eat. The servant has crossed five hundred miles, found the girl, and been invited in. And before he touches a bite of food, he stands up in front of her whole family and makes his case. Who sent him. Why. What happened at the well. And then he asks the question everything has been building to.

Yes, or no?

**Today the servant lays it all on the table and waits.**

## What To Watch For In Genesis 24

A new character runs out to the well. Notice what the Bible says he saw first.

The servant retells almost everything from the last two days. You already know the story. Watch instead for what he puts first and what he leaves out.

The last verse is a question. The chapter stops before anybody answers it.

**As you read, notice:**

* What Laban sees and hears before he welcomes the stranger
* What the servant refuses to do before he tells his errand
* The first thing he tells the family about Abraham
* The two directions he names in verse 49

And think about this question: **What are you willing to put off until you have done what you came to do?**

> “And now if ye will deal kindly and truly with my master, tell me: and if not, tell me; that I may turn to the right hand, or to the left.” (Genesis 24:49)

# The Marriage Proposal

Yesterday ended with Rebekah running home. Here is who comes running back:

> “And Rebekah had a brother, and his name was Laban: and Laban ran out unto the man, unto the well. And it came to pass, when he saw the earring and bracelets upon his sister’s hands, and when he heard the words of Rebekah his sister, saying, Thus spake the man unto me; that he came unto the man; and, behold, he stood by the camels at the well.” (Genesis 24:29–30)

Meet Laban. Rebekah’s brother. He is going to be Isaac’s brother in law, and much later he will be the father of Leah and Rachel, the two sisters Jacob marries. He is one of the most important people in the rest of Genesis, and this is his first scene. Pay attention to how the Bible introduces him.

Verse 29 says he ran to the well. Verse 30 backs up and tells you why. *When he saw the earring and bracelets upon his sister’s hands, and when he heard the words of Rebekah*. The order is on purpose. He saw the gold. Then he heard the story. Then he ran.

Remember what that gold was. A nose ring of about five grams and two bracelets of about a quarter pound of gold. A stranger at the public well just hung real money on his sister, and there are ten loaded camels standing out there. It is not hard to picture what went through Laban’s head. Whoever this man is, he represents somebody rich. Now, be fair to him. The Bible does not tell us his heart, and he is about to be a generous host. But it does tell us what he noticed first, and the rest of his life is going to show us a man who always notices the money. Keep that in your pocket for Jacob’s story.

> “And he said, Come in, thou blessed of the LORD; wherefore standest thou without? for I have prepared the house, and room for the camels.” (Genesis 24:31)

Laban’s welcome is warm. *Come in, thou blessed of the LORD*. Why are you standing out here? I have the house ready and a place for the camels. He even uses God’s name, the LORD. Hold that lightly. This family uses the LORD’s name, and this same Laban will have household idols in his tent years from now. Using the right name for God is not the same as serving only Him. But the hospitality is real. *Wherefore standest thou without* means why are you standing outside. The stranger is being brought in.

> “And the man came into the house: and he ungirded his camels, and gave straw and provender for the camels, and water to wash his feet, and the men’s feet that were with him.” (Genesis 24:32)

**Ungirded** means he loosened the straps and took the loads off the camels so they could rest. Straw and feed for the animals. Water for dusty feet. And notice *the men’s feet that were with him*. The servant did not cross the desert alone. There were men with him, driving the camels and guarding the goods. They all get washed and fed.

Does this sound familiar? Go back to Day 15. Abraham ran to three strangers, washed their feet, and fed them. Day 17, Lot pulled two strangers off the street of Sodom, washed their feet, and fed them. Now Abraham’s servant gets the same welcome in Abraham’s brother’s house. The difference is what is outside the door. In Sodom, a mob was gathering. Here, it is just a quiet evening and a family that wants to hear a story. Same hospitality, completely different world.

> “And there was set meat before him to eat: but he said, I will not eat, until I have told mine errand. And he said, Speak on.” (Genesis 24:33)

**Meat** in the old English just means food. A meal. They put dinner in front of a man who has been on the road for weeks. And he will not touch it. *I will not eat, until I have told mine errand*. Not until I have said what I came to say.

Think about that. He is tired, he is hungry, he is finally sitting in a house instead of a desert. And he puts the mission ahead of his own stomach. The job he swore an oath to do comes before his dinner. That is the kind of servant Abraham sent. Laban says, *Speak on*. Go ahead.

> “And he said, I am Abraham’s servant. And the LORD hath blessed my master greatly; and he is become great: and he hath given him flocks, and herds, and silver, and gold, and menservants, and maidservants, and camels, and asses. And Sarah my master’s wife bare a son to my master when she was old: and unto him hath he given all that he hath.” (Genesis 24:34–36)

Four words to open. *I am Abraham’s servant*. Abraham. The brother who left this family sixty years ago and walked off into a land nobody had seen. Picture the room when that name lands. Nahor’s family has not seen Abraham in a lifetime, and here is his servant in their house.

Then listen to what the servant puts first. Not Isaac. Not the well. He starts with how God has blessed Abraham. Flocks, herds, silver, gold, servants, camels, donkeys. He is telling this family, in plain terms, that the household he represents can take care of a wife. In that world, a family sending a daughter away wanted to know she would be provided for. The servant answers that before anybody asks.

And then the real news. Sarah had a son when she was old. The miracle baby. And Abraham *hath given all that he hath* to that son. Isaac is not one heir among many. He is the heir. Everything Abraham has is his. The servant has just told this family that their girl would be marrying into the whole promise.

Now, for the next twelve verses, the servant tells the family everything you already read on Day 25 and Day 26. The oath. The rule about not taking Isaac back north. Abraham’s promise that God’s angel would go ahead. The prayer at the well. The girl who came before he finished praying. The drink. The camels. Whose daughter art thou. The gold. The bowing and the worship. All of it, in order, in his own words.

I am not going to walk you through it again, because you were at the well yesterday. But notice why the Bible gives it to you twice. The first time, you heard it as it happened. This time, you hear it the way Rebekah’s family heard it, as a case being made. The servant is not just telling a story. He is laying out evidence. God led me here. God answered a specific prayer in front of my eyes. God pointed at your daughter. And he says it to a family who has to decide whether to let her go.

Feel what this family is being asked. Their daughter, their sister, taken five hundred miles away to marry a man none of them has ever met, into a household they have not had contact with in sixty years, with no expectation of coming back to visit. That is what the servant is proposing. And his whole argument is this. The LORD did this. I did not pick her. He did.

> “And now if ye will deal kindly and truly with my master, tell me: and if not, tell me; that I may turn to the right hand, or to the left.” (Genesis 24:49)

And then he stops and asks. *Deal kindly and truly* means treat my master with kindness and with honesty. Will you do right by Abraham? Say yes. And if not, say no. *That I may turn to the right hand, or to the left* means so I know what to do next. If you refuse, I will go another way.

Hear how clean that is. He does not beg. He does not pressure. He does not pile on more gifts to sweeten the deal. He lays the whole thing on the table, tells them God did it, and asks for a straight answer. Yes or no. Tell me, so I can act.

And there the chapter stops for today. A hungry man with cold food in front of him, a family that just heard a long lost brother’s name, a girl with gold on her wrists, and a question hanging in the air.

Here is what to take from this scene. The servant finished his errand before he fed himself. He put God first in the story, before Isaac and before the gold. He told the truth plainly to people who had every right to say no. And then he asked for a clear answer and was willing to live with it either way. That is how you carry a mission for somebody else. All the way through, honestly, and then you let the people decide.

Tomorrow, the family answers, and then the question goes to the one person who matters most. Rebekah herself. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The servant would not eat until he had told his errand. What have you been putting off that should come before your own comfort?** He was tired and hungry and the mission still came first. Name the thing you keep eating around.
2. **Laban saw the gold before he heard the story. When you meet people, what do you notice first?** The Bible put his eyes on the bracelets on purpose. Check where yours go.
3. **The servant asked for a straight yes or no and was ready to turn right or left. Where are you pressuring people for an answer instead of asking plainly and letting them decide?** Make your case, tell the truth, ask the question, and let go of the outcome.

## Today's One Thing

**Tell Your Errand**

The servant said what he came to say before dinner. Do the same with one conversation you have been eating around.

1. Write down one thing you need to say to somebody that you have been delaying. A request, a proposal, an honest answer, an apology.
2. Say it today, plainly, and put God first in how you tell it. Then ask for a straight answer. Yes or no.
3. Whatever they say, write it down tonight, and decide before bed which way you turn, right or left.

One errand told, one straight question, one direction chosen. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

You sent a servant five hundred miles and got him to the right table.

Thank You that You lead people all the way, not just to the well but into the house.

Make me the kind of servant who finishes the errand before the meal.

Who puts the thing You sent me to do ahead of my own comfort, and tells the truth about You before I say anything about myself.

**And help me ask plainly, Lord, and then let people answer.**

I pressure.

I pile on.

I try to sweeten deals You never asked me to sweeten.

Teach me to lay the case out honestly, say You did it, ask yes or no, and be ready to turn right or left.

Guard my eyes too.

Do not let me be a Laban, seeing the gold before I hear the story.

And for every conversation I have been eating around, give me the courage to say it today.
