Picture Abraham’s camp from a distance. Not one tent. A whole spread of them across the dry southern hills, with smoke from the cook fires, servants moving between the tents, and flocks and herds grazing out in every direction as far as the grass goes. It looks like a small town that can pack up and move.

And it cannot live one day without water.

Every person in that camp drinks. Every pot of food needs it. Every sheep, goat, and cow has to be watered, every single day, or they die. Out here, grass without water is worthless. A well is not a convenience. It is the difference between a household that stays and a household that has to leave.

Today a king comes riding up to that camp with his army commander beside him. The last time we saw this king, Abraham had lied to him. Now the king has been watching Abraham for a while, and he has noticed something he cannot explain. And Abraham, before he shakes on anything, has a complaint of his own. Somebody took his well.

**Today a wrong gets settled, a place gets its name, and Abraham worships.**

# A Covenant at Beersheba

Yesterday ended in the wilderness with Hagar and Ishmael. Today the camera comes back to Abraham’s camp, and he has visitors:

> “And it came to pass at that time, that Abimelech and Phichol the chief captain of his host spake unto Abraham, saying, God is with thee in all that thou doest.” (Genesis 21:22)

Abimelech. You know him. He is the king of Gerar from Day 20, the one who took Sarah into his house, got a death threat from God in a dream, gave her back, paid a fortune to clear her name, and told Abraham to live anywhere in his land he wanted. Abraham has been living in that land ever since. Isaac was born there. Isaac was weaned there. Some real time has gone by.

And he does not come alone. **Phichol the chief captain of his host** is the commander of his army. Picture the king riding up with his top general. That is not a casual visit. That is a king who wants to settle something serious with a man he considers important.

Now listen to the first thing out of his mouth. *God is with thee in all that thou doest*. A pagan king looked at Abraham’s life and could see God in it. The herds. The household. The son born to a hundred year old man. The way everything this man touched seemed to work out. Abimelech cannot explain it except to say God is with you.

Hold on to that, because the Bible is going to show you the same thing with Abraham’s great grandson. When Joseph is a slave in Egypt, the Bible says of his master, *his master saw that the LORD was with him, and that the LORD made all that he did to prosper in his hand* (Genesis 39:3). Same pattern. Somebody outside the family looks at a man who walks with God and sees it on him. The world can tell. They may not know what to call it, but they can tell.

> “Now therefore swear unto me here by God that thou wilt not deal falsely with me, nor with my son, nor with my son’s son: but according to the kindness that I have done unto thee, thou shalt do unto me, and to the land wherein thou hast sojourned. And Abraham said, I will swear.” (Genesis 21:23–24)

So the king wants a deal. Swear by your God that you will not deal falsely with me, or my son, or my grandson. Treat me and this land the way I treated you.

Feel the weight of *deal falsely*. This is the king Abraham lied to. He has every reason to want that in writing. And Abraham says yes. I will swear. A man who once ran a con on this king is now putting his hand up and promising honesty for three generations.

But before the handshake, Abraham has something to say:

> “And Abraham reproved Abimelech because of a well of water, which Abimelech’s servants had violently taken away.” (Genesis 21:25)

**Reproved** here means Abraham confronted him. Brought up a complaint. Your men took my well. And not politely. *Violently taken away*. Seized by force.

Now, understand what a well is and why this matters. A **well** is a hole dug down into the ground until you hit water underneath, so you can draw it up. Finding a spot where water is reachable and then digging down to it is hard, slow work. Abraham did that work. We will hear him say so in a few verses. And then the king’s servants came and took it.

Go back to the picture of the camp. All those people. All those animals. Every one of them has to drink every day. You can have the best grass in the country, and without a well you cannot stay. Taking a man’s well in that world is taking his ability to live where he lives. So Abraham does not let it slide. Before he swears to deal honestly with the king, he wants to know why the king’s people have not been dealing honestly with him.

> “And Abimelech said, I wot not who hath done this thing: neither didst thou tell me, neither yet heard I of it, but to day.” (Genesis 21:26)

**I wot not** means I do not know. Break the king’s answer into three pieces. I do not know who did this. You never told me. And today is the first I have heard of it.

And that is believable. A king does not personally watch what every one of his servants does out in the countryside. Somebody under him got greedy or overzealous, grabbed a good well, and never reported it. Abimelech is not denying it happened. He is saying this is news to me, and why did you not bring it to me sooner?

Notice what Abraham does with the wrong. He does not gather his trained men and take the well back by force. He takes it to the ruler, says it plainly, and settles it in a way that will hold. That is a different kind of strength than Day 11, and it is the right kind for this moment.

> “And Abraham took sheep and oxen, and gave them unto Abimelech; and both of them made a covenant.” (Genesis 21:27)

A **covenant**, which you know by now, is a binding agreement. Here it is between two men, not God and a man. And its terms are simple. Honest dealing and kindness between Abraham and the king, and his son, and his grandson. Abraham gives sheep and oxen to go with it. Catch the reversal. On Day 20, the king gave Abraham sheep and oxen. Now Abraham gives sheep and oxen to the king. These are two men on even ground now, making a deal the way neighbors should.

And why does the king want this deal so badly? Think about Joseph again. His master prospered because Joseph was in the house. A king who can see that God is with Abraham wants Abraham close, on good terms, for a long time. Peace with a man God is blessing is a smart thing to want.

> “And Abraham set seven ewe lambs of the flock by themselves. And Abimelech said unto Abraham, What mean these seven ewe lambs which thou hast set by themselves? And he said, For these seven ewe lambs shalt thou take of my hand, that they may be a witness unto me, that I have digged this well.” (Genesis 21:28–30)

Now Abraham does something specific. **Ewe lambs** are young female sheep. He takes seven of them and sets them apart from the rest of the gift. The king notices and asks, what are these for?

And here is Abraham’s answer. *Take of my hand* means accept these from me. *That they may be a witness* means these are the proof. By accepting these seven lambs, you are agreeing, in front of everybody, that I dug this well. It is mine.

Think of it like paperwork. A deed. Abraham does not want to live on the king’s goodwill, hoping nobody takes his well again. He wants a recognized claim that holds up after the king rides home, and after the king is dead, and when his grandson is on the throne. The big covenant settles that they will be honest with each other. The seven lambs settle one specific thing inside that covenant. This well belongs to Abraham, and the king accepted the proof with his own hands.

Keep that instinct in mind, because in two days Abraham is going to do it again, in front of witnesses at a city gate, with silver instead of lambs. He has learned that in a land where you are a foreigner, you secure what matters. You do not just hope.

And he is right to. Years from now, Isaac is going to come back to this same region and find the wells his father dug stopped up with dirt, and he is going to fight the herdsmen of Gerar over water, well after well, until he finally digs one nobody argues about. Water was never a small thing for this family.

> “Wherefore he called that place Beersheba; because there they sware both of them. Thus they made a covenant at Beersheba: then Abimelech rose up, and Phichol the chief captain of his host, and they returned into the land of the Philistines.” (Genesis 21:31–32)

Now the place gets its name. **Beersheba** means the well of the oath, and it carries a wordplay, because the Hebrew for seven and the Hebrew for swearing sound alike. Seven lambs, an oath, a well. The name holds all of it. Well of the Seven. Well of the Oath. Both are in there.

And remember yesterday. Hagar wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba, before the place had this name. The Bible did that on purpose. The narrator, Moses, is writing for people who know where Beersheba is, so he uses the name they know to tell them where she was. It is like saying a story happened in a town by its current name, even though the town was called something else back then. Beersheba sits at the dry southern edge of the land, where the farmland gives out and the desert begins. It is still a city today, and you can still visit the ruins of the ancient town nearby.

Then the king and his general ride home to *the land of the Philistines*. One quick word on that name, because you are going to hear it a lot later in your Bible. We first met it on Day 3, in the family of nations, where it is spelled Philistim. The Philistines you probably know are the ones from much later, the five coastal cities, Gaza and Ashkelon and Ashdod and Ekron and Gath. Samson fights them. They capture the ark. Goliath comes from Gath. Those later Philistines were a tough, hostile people on the coast. The Bible uses the same name for the region Abimelech rules here, in the southwest of the land. But look at how different this scene is. No war. No giants. A king who fears God and makes peace. Do not read Goliath into Abimelech.

> “And Abraham planted a grove in Beersheba, and called there on the name of the LORD, the everlasting God. And Abraham sojourned in the Philistines’ land many days.” (Genesis 21:33–34)

Now look at what Abraham does once the well is secured. **Grove** in the King James sounds like a cluster of trees, but the Hebrew word is usually understood as one tree, a tamarisk, a hardy tree that can grow in dry country and give shade for a long time. Think about what it means to plant a tree. You do not plant a tree if you are leaving next week. You plant a tree when you intend to stay, and when you expect somebody to be sitting in its shade long after you are gone. The sojourner is putting down roots by his well.

And then he worships. *Called there on the name of the LORD*. We have seen him do this before, at Bethel on Day 8 and again on Day 10. But this time he uses a name for God we have never heard. **The everlasting God**. In Hebrew, **El Olam**. El is God. Olam means forever, lasting, without end.

Put that next to Day 14. There God introduced Himself as El Shaddai, the Almighty God, the God of power. Now Abraham calls Him El Olam, the God who lasts forever. Same God, different side of Him. And think about why this name comes out of Abraham here. He just made a deal with a king for three generations. He just planted a tree that will outlive him. He is a hundred year old man with a toddler. Everything around him is about lasting, about what holds after he is gone. And he looks up and names the One who was there before the well and will be there after the tree falls. The everlasting God. Much later the prophet Isaiah picks up the same name and says the everlasting God does not faint and does not get tired. That is who Abraham is calling on by his well.

*Many days* just means a long time. Abraham stays in that region, by his well, under his tree, for years. Not a fixed number. A season of peace.

So step back and look at the whole day. A king looked at Abraham and saw God on him. Abraham swore honesty to the man he once deceived. He confronted a wrong plainly and settled it in a way that would last. And then, with the water secured and the peace made, he planted a tree and called on the God who outlasts everything.

That is a good order for things. Deal honestly, even with people you have wronged before. Handle what is wrong plainly instead of swallowing it or grabbing a sword. Secure what matters. And then, when you have done what a man can do, do what only a man of God does. Look past the well and the tree and the king, and call on the One who is everlasting.

Tomorrow, after these quiet years, God speaks to Abraham again. And what He asks for this time is the hardest thing He has ever asked anyone. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

1. **Abimelech could see that God was with Abraham just by watching his life. What would somebody watching your life from the outside say?** Not what you say about God. What your life says. Be honest about what shows.
2. **Is there a wrong you have been swallowing, or a wrong you have been planning to settle by force, that needs to be brought up plainly instead?** Abraham did not pretend about the well and he did not raid for it. He named it to the person who could fix it. Name yours.
3. **What are you building or planting right now that is really about what lasts after you?** Abraham planted a tree and called on the everlasting God. Ask yourself what you are leaving in the shade for the next generation.

## Today's One Thing

**Settle It Plainly**

Abraham brought up the well before he shook hands. Do what he did with one thing you have been carrying.

1. Write down one wrong done to you that you have never actually said out loud to the person who could do something about it.
2. Today, say it to them plainly and without heat. One sentence that states what happened. Give them the chance to say, I did not know.
3. Then, before bed, call on the everlasting God by that name, and thank Him for one thing in your life that will outlast you.

One wrong named, one honest sentence, one prayer to El Olam. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You are the everlasting God.

You were there before Abraham dug his well and You are still there now that the tree is gone.

Everything I am worried about today is temporary.

You are not.

Let people see You on me the way Abimelech saw You on Abraham. Not because I talk about You, but because the way I live cannot be explained without You.

**Make me a man who deals honestly, even with the people I have wronged before.**

And give me the courage to bring up what is wrong plainly.

Not to swallow it until it poisons me, and not to grab for it by force.

To say it to the right person, the right way, and let it be settled.

Help me secure what matters and hold loosely to what does not. Show me the wells in my life that are worth seven lambs, and the things I have been fighting over that are not.

And teach me to plant trees.

To build things whose shade I will never sit in.

To live like a man who knows that You last forever, and that what I do for You lasts with You.

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
