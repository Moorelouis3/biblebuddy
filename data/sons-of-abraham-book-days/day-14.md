Yesterday we counted eight sons in Jacob’s camp. Four from Leah. Two from Rachel’s maid Bilhah.

Two from Leah’s maid Zilpah. Eight boys running between the tents, and two sisters who are still not happy.

Because the sons were never really the point. Leah has a tent full of children and a husband who sleeps somewhere else.

Rachel has the husband and an empty cradle. Each one has exactly what the other one is dying for.

Today a little boy walks in from the wheat field with a handful of plants, and the whole thing boils over.

Two sisters make a trade. A wife hires her own husband for a night.

And then, after years of silence, God turns toward the woman nobody could help.

This is one of the most human passages in Genesis.

It is about jealousy and longing and bargaining, and about a God who answers in His own time, not ours.

By the end of today, the camp will have eleven sons and a daughter, and one of those sons will have a coat of many colours waiting for him years down the road.

# Hiring Her Own Husband

> “And God remembered Rachel, and God hearkened to her, and opened her womb.” (Genesis 30:22)

Today we are reading Genesis chapter 30, verses 14 to 24.

Let me set the scene before we read, because the Bible drops us straight into the middle of it.

Jacob has two wives, and they are sisters.

On Day 12 we watched Laban trick him into marrying Leah first, and then we read the line that explains everything that follows.

*“And he loved also Rachel more than Leah.” (Genesis 29:30)* Jacob did not hide it.

Everybody in the camp knew which tent he walked to at night. I think Jacob ordinarily lived with Rachel.

She was the wife he worked seven years for, the one he kissed at the well.

Leah was the wife he woke up next to and did not choose. Yesterday we saw God step in for her.

*“And when the LORD saw that Leah was hated, he opened her womb: but Rachel was barren.” (Genesis 29:31)* So Leah has four sons of her own, and Rachel has two through her maid, and Leah has two more through her maid.

Eight boys. And now a ninth is about to come from a very strange arrangement.

> “And Reuben went in the days of wheat harvest, and found mandrakes in the field, and brought them unto his mother Leah. Then Rachel said to Leah, Give me, I pray thee, of thy son’s mandrakes.” (Genesis 30:14)

## Love Apples

Reuben is Leah’s oldest.

Wheat harvest in that part of the world is late spring, so picture a small boy, maybe four or five years old, out in the fields while the men cut grain, and he finds something and runs home with it for his mother.

What did he find? Mandrakes are a plant with a thick forked root and small yellowish fruit with a strong smell.

In the ancient world people believed mandrakes helped a woman get pregnant. Some called them love apples.

Nobody in that camp had a doctor.

They had old remedies and old beliefs passed down from the land Abraham came out of, and this was one of them.

Reuben does not know any of that. He just brought his mother some fruit.

But Rachel knows exactly what it is, and the moment she sees it she wants it.

Think about what that tells you. Rachel is desperate.

Back in verse 1 of this chapter she grabbed Jacob and said, *“Give me children, or else I die.” (Genesis 30:1)* And Jacob snapped back, *“Am I in God’s stead, who hath withheld from thee the fruit of the womb?” (Genesis 30:2)* She has tried giving Jacob her maid.

She has watched her sister have baby after baby.

Now a child walks in with a plant that people say works, and she asks for it. Not demands. *I pray thee.* Please.

The loved wife is asking the unloved wife for a favor.

And Leah lets her have it. Years of hurt come out in one sentence.

## Thou Hast Taken My Husband

> “And she said unto her, Is it a small matter that thou hast taken my husband? and wouldest thou take away my son’s mandrakes also? And Rachel said, Therefore he shall lie with thee to night for thy son’s mandrakes.” (Genesis 30:15)

Read Leah’s words again. *Thou hast taken my husband.* Leah was married to Jacob first. By law he is her husband.

But in her own words, Rachel has taken him. That is how it felt from inside Leah’s tent.

The man she is married to belongs to someone else, and now that someone wants the one nice thing her little boy brought her.

Now look at Rachel’s answer, because this is where it gets striking. She does not argue. She offers a deal.

*He shall lie with thee to night.* Rachel is handing out a night with Jacob like it is hers to give. And it was.

That is the power imbalance in this family. Rachel decides where Jacob sleeps.

Leah has to be granted a night with her own husband, and she has to pay for it.

I want you to see clearly what these two women are trading, because I think a lot of readers miss it.

They are trading chances to get pregnant. Rachel wants the mandrakes because she believes they will help her conceive.

Leah wants the night because that is the only way she will conceive.

Both of them know exactly what the other one is after. Nobody is fooling anybody.

Two sisters, each holding something the other needs, making a swap in the middle of the camp.

Does that sound familiar?

On Day 3 we watched Esau come in from the field, faint with hunger, and Jacob had a pot of stew.

Esau wanted the stew badly enough to give up his birthright for it.

Jacob saw what his brother wanted and used it to get what he wanted.

Now Jacob’s own wives are doing the same thing to each other.

One sister has mandrakes, the other has the husband, and each one bargains with the other’s deepest want.

This family bargains. It is in the blood.

## I Have Hired Thee

> “And Jacob came out of the field in the evening, and Leah went out to meet him, and said, Thou must come in unto me; for surely I have hired thee with my son’s mandrakes. And he lay with her that night.” (Genesis 30:16)

Picture it. The sun is going down.

Jacob is walking in from the flocks, tired, probably headed for Rachel’s tent like always.

And Leah is standing in the path waiting for him. *Thou must come in unto me.* Not would you. You must.

She has already paid. *I have hired thee.* The word hired means exactly what it sounds like. Wages. A deal.

She has bought this night the way you would hire a worker for a day.

Sit with that for a second. A wife, a real wife, has to hire her own husband. She does not get to simply ask.

She has to go through her sister, pay a price, and then meet him on the road to tell him the arrangement has been made.

I do not know how to read that without feeling for her. And Jacob goes. *He lay with her that night.*

No words from him at all. He just does what was arranged.

Now, I want to connect this to something from The Obedience of Abraham. Remember Hagar.

Sarah gave her to Abraham, Hagar got pregnant, and from then on she was a woman with a child in a tent where she was never going to be the real wife.

Leah is not a servant. She is Jacob’s lawful wife, the first one, with full standing. But look at how she is living.

Emotionally, she is in Hagar’s place. She has the children and not the heart of the man.

Rachel holds the place Sarah held. The loved one. The one whose tent is home. The law said Leah was a wife.

The nights said otherwise.

## God Hearkened Unto Leah

> “And God hearkened unto Leah, and she conceived, and bare Jacob the fifth son. And Leah said, God hath given me my hire, because I have given my maiden to my husband: and she called his name Issachar.” (Genesis 30:17–18)

Now watch what happens with the trade. Rachel got the mandrakes. Leah got the night. And it is Leah who gets pregnant.

Not Rachel. The woman holding the fertility plant stays barren. The woman who simply went in to her husband conceives.

The Bible is quietly making a point. The mandrakes did nothing. *God hearkened unto Leah.*

Hearkened means listened and answered.

The Bible does not write out Leah’s prayer, but you do not get hearkened to unless you have been asking.

Behind the bargaining was a woman praying, and God heard her, not the plant.

A fifth son, and she names him Issachar, which sounds like the Hebrew for hire or reward. *God hath given me my hire.*

The same word she used on the road. She hired Jacob with mandrakes, and God paid her back with a son.

Her own explanation is that God rewarded her for giving Zilpah to Jacob. That is Leah talking, how she made sense of it.

I would not build a rule on it. But the name sticks. Issachar, the wages boy.

## She Named Her Sixth Son Home

> “And Leah conceived again, and bare Jacob the sixth son. And Leah said, God hath endued me with a good dowry; now will my husband dwell with me, because I have born him six sons: and she called his name Zebulun.” (Genesis 30:19–20)

A sixth son. A dowry was the valuable gift that came with a marriage, the wealth a bride brought into her new home.

Endued just means given, supplied. Leah is saying God has given her a bride gift worth having. Six sons.

In that world, that was a fortune. More heirs than most men ever saw.

But listen to what she says next, because this is the sentence I keep coming back to.

*Now will my husband dwell with me.* Dwell means live with. Share a tent. Share a life.

Leah has six sons and she is still hoping Jacob will move in. That tells you where he has been sleeping all these years.

Not with her. Count her hopes across the chapter. When Reuben was born, *now therefore my husband will love me*.

When Levi was born, *now this time will my husband be joined unto me*. Now Zebulun, *now will my husband dwell with me*.

Three sons, three hopes, and every one of them is the same hope. She does not want to be the mother of his sons.

She wants to be his wife. The name Zebulun carries that longing. It sounds like the Hebrew for dwelling, for a home.

She named her sixth son Home, because she was still waiting for her husband to come home.

> “And afterwards she bare a daughter, and called her name Dinah.” (Genesis 30:21)

One short verse, and no speech from Leah this time.

A daughter, Dinah, a name built on the Hebrew word for judgment, the same root as her brother Dan’s name.

The Bible does not stop to explain it the way it did the boys, and in that world a daughter did not carry the family name forward, so the writer moves on.

But hold onto Dinah.

She is the only daughter of Jacob named in Genesis, and when we get to the next book, she is at the center of one of the hardest chapters in it.

For now, Leah has seven children of her own, and the Bible turns to the other tent.

## God Remembered Rachel

> “And God remembered Rachel, and God hearkened to her, and opened her womb.” (Genesis 30:22)

Here it is.

After everything, after the maid and the mandrakes and the years of watching her sister’s tent fill up, God remembered Rachel.

Let me explain that word, because it does not mean what it sounds like.

God did not forget Rachel and then suddenly recall her.

Remembered in the Bible means God turned His attention toward someone and acted for them. It is a doing word.

Watch it through Genesis. *“And God remembered Noah” (Genesis 8:1)*, and the waters went down.

*“God remembered Abraham” (Genesis 19:29)*, and Lot was pulled out of Sodom before the fire fell.

Now *“God remembered Rachel”*, and her womb opened. Every time God remembers somebody in this book, something moves.

And He *hearkened to her*. The same word as with Leah. God listened and answered. That means Rachel had been praying.

All those years, behind the envy and the bargaining and the shouting at Jacob, there was a woman on her knees.

The Bible gave us her worst moment, *give me children, or else I die*, and it tells us here that God heard the rest.

The tears we did not get to see. The nights she lay in the loved wife’s tent and still felt like the one who had failed.

*Opened her womb.* That is the exact phrase from Genesis 29:31 about Leah. God opened Leah’s womb because she was hated.

Now God opens Rachel’s womb because He remembered her. Same God, same hand, two sisters.

And notice what did not open it. The mandrakes have been in Rachel’s tent for years at this point.

Leah has had three more children since that trade. Rachel’s remedy did nothing. God did everything.

## God Hath Taken Away My Reproach

> “And she conceived, and bare a son; and said, God hath taken away my reproach: And she called his name Joseph; and said, The LORD shall add to me another son.” (Genesis 30:23–24)

Reproach means shame. Disgrace. The thing people whispered about.

In that world a woman without children carried a weight we can barely imagine.

Her whole purpose, as everyone around her saw it, was to give her husband sons, and she could not.

Every year that passed added to it. It was not her fault. It was never proof she had done anything wrong.

But it felt like a mark on her, and everyone could see it.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you have met this before.

Sarah carried that same reproach for decades.

A beautiful, loved wife with an empty tent, who finally handed her husband a servant girl because she could not bear the waiting anymore.

Rachel is Sarah all over again. Loved, beautiful, barren, and desperate enough to try anything.

And just like with Sarah, the child came when God said, not when the remedies said.

She names him Joseph, and the name does two things at once.

It sounds like the Hebrew for taken away, as in *God hath taken away my reproach*.

And it sounds like the Hebrew for add, as in *the LORD shall add to me another son*. Taken away and added.

The shame removed, and more to come. Rachel got her boy and immediately asked for one more.

I love that about her, and it is also a little sad. That last line is not a promise from God.

It is Rachel’s hope spoken out loud. She does get another son, years later, on the road to Bethlehem.

His name is Benjamin, and giving birth to him costs Rachel her life.

When we reach that scene in the next book, remember this verse. The LORD did add to her another son.

Just not the way she pictured it standing here with Joseph in her arms.

Let me give you a quick sense of time before we close, because it will matter later.

Jacob worked seven years before the weddings.

The children in these two chapters, all eleven sons and Dinah, are born in the seven years after that.

Joseph comes right at the end, fourteen years after Jacob walked into Laban’s camp.

So Reuben, the oldest, is maybe six or seven years older than Joseph. Not twenty.

When Joseph is seventeen and his brothers throw him in a pit, Reuben is a young man in his twenties, not a greybeard.

These boys grew up close together, in overlapping tents, with four mothers, and every one of them knew whose son was loved most.

So step back and look at the whole day. Two sisters traded what they had for what they wanted.

Leah hired her husband and got two more sons and a daughter, and still ended the day hoping he would come home.

Rachel got the mandrakes and got nothing from them, and then got everything from God.

Here is the lesson I want you to carry. The bargaining did not work. The remedies did not work.

What worked was that God hearkened. He listened to Leah, who had the children and wanted the love.

He listened to Rachel, who had the love and wanted the children. He did not fix either sister’s marriage.

But He heard both of them, and He remembered.

Whatever you are bargaining for right now, whatever remedy you have been counting on, the thing that moves is God turning toward you.

He has not forgotten. He remembers.

Tomorrow, with Joseph born, Jacob walks up to Laban and says the words he has been holding for fourteen years.

Send me away. And Laban, who has never let go of anything in his life, makes him an offer.

But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 30

Eleven verses, and they move fast.

A trade, three births, a daughter, and then the birth the whole chapter has been waiting for.

Listen to how the two sisters talk to each other in verses 14 and 15. That is the real state of this household.

Every son gets a name, and every name gets a reason. Read the reasons slowly.

They are Leah’s heart and Rachel’s heart written down.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🌿 Who finds the mandrakes, and who ends up with them
* 🌇 What Leah says to Jacob when he comes in from the field
* 🏠 What Leah hopes for when she names Zebulun in verse 20
* 🙏 The three things God does for Rachel in verse 22

And think about this question: what does it tell you about a marriage when a wife has to bargain for one night with her husband?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Leah had six sons and still said, now will my husband dwell with me. What is the thing you keep hoping will finally make someone love you?** Name it honestly. Then ask whether it has ever worked.
2. **Rachel held the mandrakes for years and they did nothing. What remedy or shortcut are you trusting more than you are trusting God?** The plant stayed in her tent while God did the work. Write down what you have been leaning on instead of Him.
3. **God remembered Rachel after years of silence. Where in your life does it feel like God has forgotten?** Remembering means He turns and acts. Tell Him about the thing you have stopped expecting.

## Today's One Thing

**Name the Longing**

Leah and Rachel both prayed, and God hearkened to both. Today, put your own longing into words the way they did.

1. Write one sentence that starts with the words: God, what I am really longing for is. Finish it honestly, not nicely.
2. Under it, write the bargain or remedy you have been using to get it on your own. Your mandrakes.

One longing named, one remedy named, four names on the map. Do it, and check it off.
