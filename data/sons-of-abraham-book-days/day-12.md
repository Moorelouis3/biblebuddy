Yesterday Jacob found his family. Laban ran to meet him, kissed him, called him bone and flesh, and brought him home.

A month went by. The runaway had a roof again.

Now I want you to remember why he came. Yes, he was running from Esau.

But his father also sent him with an order: take a wife from your mother’s family.

And at the well, a girl with her father’s sheep walked right into that order. Her name was Rachel.

Today the month ends and the real story of Jacob’s years in Haran begins. His uncle asks him what he wants to be paid.

And Jacob names his price. Not silver. Not sheep. Seven years of his life for the girl from the well.

He pays it. Every day of it. And on his wedding night, in the dark, he gets handed someone else.

Keep one thing in your mind as you read.

This is the man who put on his brother’s clothes and told his blind father, I am Esau.

He knows how to trick a man who cannot see. Today, he finds out what that feels like from the other side.

Let’s read it.

# The Deceiver Deceived

> “What is this thou hast done unto me? did not I serve with thee for Rachel? wherefore then hast thou beguiled me?” (Genesis 29:25)

> “And Laban said unto Jacob, Because thou art my brother, shouldest thou therefore serve me for nought? tell me, what shall thy wages be?” (Genesis 29:15)

A month in, and Laban sits his nephew down. *Because thou art my brother.* There is that wide family word again.

Laban is Jacob’s uncle, and he calls him brother, the same way Abraham once looked at his nephew Lot and said, *we be brethren*.

In this family, brother can mean any close relative. Keep that straight and the verse makes sense.

*Serve me for nought* means work for me for nothing. *Wages* means pay.

So here is what Laban is saying: just because you are family does not mean you should work for free.

Tell me what you want. And notice what that tells you. Jacob has been working.

For a month he has been out with the flocks, earning his keep, and Laban has noticed.

These are two grown men talking about real labor and real payment.

And I do mean grown.

If you work the numbers backward from later in Genesis, Jacob is somewhere around seventy seven years old when he arrives in Haran.

I know. That is not the picture most of us carry.

But remember, Isaac was forty when he married and sixty when the twins were born, and people in this family lived a long time.

Jacob is not a teenager running away from home. He is a mature man who has never married, finally standing on his own.

## Tender Eyed

> “And Laban had two daughters: the name of the elder was Leah, and the name of the younger was Rachel. Leah was tender eyed; but Rachel was beautiful and well favoured.” (Genesis 29:16–17)

Two daughters. Leah is the older, Rachel the younger.

And the Bible describes them in one line each, so let’s be careful with it.

*Tender eyed.* The Hebrew word behind tender means soft, delicate, gentle.

Some people read it as weak eyes, dull eyes, eyes with no sparkle.

Others read it as soft, lovely eyes. It does not mean crossed eyes, and it does not say she was ugly. Read it again.

The Bible does not say Leah was not beautiful.

It says her eyes were tender, and then it turns to Rachel and spends more words on her. That is the point.

Rachel is being singled out.

*Beautiful and well favoured.* In Hebrew that is two kinds of beautiful, beautiful in form and beautiful to look at.

Her figure and her face. *Well favoured* does not mean God favored her more, or that everyone in town favored her.

It just means good looking. Rachel was the kind of woman people noticed.

## Seven Years for Rachel

> “And Jacob loved Rachel; and said, I will serve thee seven years for Rachel thy younger daughter.” (Genesis 29:18)

There it is. Jacob loved Rachel. The girl from the well. And look at how precise he is.

Not I will serve you for a daughter. *Rachel thy younger daughter.*

Her name and her place in the family, so there can be no confusion. Remember that. It matters a great deal later.

Now, why seven years of work? In that world, a man did not just take a wife.

He gave something to her family, a bride price, to show he was serious and to make the marriage official.

When Abraham’s servant came to this same town for Rebekah, he brought gold and silver and fine clothes for her and gifts for her family.

That is what the price looked like when the family was rich. But Jacob walked in with a staff.

He has no camels to hand over, no silver to weigh out. What he has is his hands and his skill.

He grew up in Isaac’s flocks. His mother sent him for two goats and he came back with them.

He watered Rachel’s sheep the first hour he met her. So he offers the one thing he has.

Seven years of a skilled herdsman’s labor.

Seven years is not a rule in the Bible, and I am not going to pretend the number has a secret meaning.

It is simply a big price, and Jacob offers it without blinking.

> “And Laban said, It is better that I give her to thee, than that I should give her to another man: abide with me. And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her.” (Genesis 29:19–20)

Laban says yes. Better you than a stranger. *Abide with me* means stay here.

And then comes one of the sweetest lines in Genesis.

Seven years, and they *seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her*.

Do not rush past that. Seven years of early mornings and lambing seasons and sheep that wander off.

Seven years of working for another man and sleeping in another man’s house. And it felt short.

Not because time actually sped up, but because of what he was working toward.

When you love someone, the waiting changes shape.

Rachel was worth it to him, and the years went by like they were nothing.

## Give Me My Wife

> “And Jacob said unto Laban, Give me my wife, for my days are fulfilled, that I may go in unto her.” (Genesis 29:21)

*My days are fulfilled* means the time we agreed on is done.

*Go in unto her* is the Bible’s plain way of saying consummate the marriage. Let’s be honest about this verse.

The man has worked seven years. He is ready to get married. And notice something. He has to ask.

Laban does not come to him and say, congratulations, the time is up.

Jacob has to go to his uncle and say, give me my wife. Keep an eye on Laban.

> “And Laban gathered together all the men of the place, and made a feast.” (Genesis 29:22)

A feast. A wedding celebration, the men of the town gathered, food and drink and noise.

This family marks big moments with feasts. Abraham made a great feast the day Isaac was weaned.

Isaac made a feast for Abimelech when they made peace.

A feast means something important is happening and everybody knows it. The whole town is there for Jacob’s wedding.

Which makes what happens next even more shocking.

> “And it came to pass in the evening, that he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him; and he went in unto her. And Laban gave unto his daughter Leah Zilpah his maid for an handmaid.” (Genesis 29:23–24)

Read it slowly. Evening. Laban takes Leah. Not Rachel. Leah. He brings her to Jacob.

And Jacob, believing this is the woman he worked seven years for, takes her as his wife.

Now, the question every reader asks. How could he not know?

I will give you the honest answer, which is that the Bible does not stage the scene for us. But think about it.

It is night, in a tent, with no electric light. Brides in this world were veiled.

Remember when Rebekah first saw Isaac, she took a veil and covered herself.

There has been a feast, and wine at a feast is likely, though the Bible never says Jacob was drunk, so I will not say it either.

Add to that the fact that Leah went along with it, and said nothing. Dark, veiled, quiet.

A man who had waited seven years, who had no reason on earth to suspect his own uncle.

It is not hard to see how it worked.

And in the middle of the betrayal, a name. Zilpah.

A handmaid is a female servant, a woman who belongs to the household and is given to serve a daughter when she marries.

Hagar was Sarah’s handmaid. When Rebekah left this same town, she went with her nurse and her maids.

So Laban gives Leah a servant girl named Zilpah. Remember her name. She is going to matter.

## Behold, It Was Leah

> “And it came to pass, that in the morning, behold, it was Leah: and he said to Laban, What is this thou hast done unto me? did not I serve with thee for Rachel? wherefore then hast thou beguiled me?” (Genesis 29:25)

Morning. Daylight. *Behold, it was Leah.* Feel that.

Seven years, a wedding feast, a night with the woman he thought was Rachel, and the sun comes up on the wrong sister.

So he goes to Laban. What have you done to me? Did I not serve you for Rachel? Why have you *beguiled* me?

That word means tricked, deceived, cheated. And here is what stopped me cold when I studied it.

The Hebrew word behind beguiled belongs to the same family as the word Isaac used in chapter 27, when he told Esau, *Thy brother came with subtilty, and hath taken away thy blessing*.

Subtilty. Deceit. The same idea, the same root.

Do you see it? Jacob stood in front of a father who could not see and let him believe he was the other brother.

Now Jacob lies in the dark with a woman he cannot see and is made to believe she is the other sister.

He deceived his father about which son was in front of him. Laban deceives him about which daughter is in front of him.

Jacob asks Laban the very question his own father could have asked him. What is this you have done to me?

I want to say this carefully. Laban is not off the hook.

What Jacob did to Isaac does not make what Laban did to Jacob all right.

Deception is wrong because it takes away a person’s ability to choose. Jacob agreed to seven years for Rachel.

He did not agree to Leah.

Laban took his labor under one deal and paid him under another, and he used his own daughters to do it.

That is theft with a wedding veil on it.

But here is what I see God doing. I see God humbling Jacob.

Jacob has spent his whole life getting what he wanted by being clever. The birthright over a bowl of stew.

The blessing in a goatskin. And it worked, every time.

Now, far from home, with no mother to coach him, he meets a man who is better at it than he is.

And for the first time, Jacob feels it from the other side. The shock in the morning. The anger.

The sense of being used. I think God is teaching him something no sermon could, and I think it is a stage in his growth.

Not the finish line. Jacob is not suddenly a wise and perfect man.

But a man who has been deceived understands deception differently. He has to.

## The Younger Before the Firstborn

> “And Laban said, It must not be so done in our country, to give the younger before the firstborn.” (Genesis 29:26)

Laban’s excuse. Around here, the older daughter marries first. Now, maybe that was a real custom in Haran.

But think about it. Laban had seven years to mention it.

Jacob said Rachel thy younger daughter out loud, and Laban said yes.

If the custom was real, the honest thing was to say so on day one, not to spring it on the morning after.

Whether or not the rule existed, using it this way is dishonest.

And do you hear the echo? *The younger before the firstborn.* Jacob is the younger who took the firstborn’s place.

Now the firstborn daughter has been put in the younger daughter’s place, on him.

The man who jumped the line gets handed the line.

## Fulfil Her Week

> “Fulfil her week, and we will give thee this also for the service which thou shalt serve with me yet seven other years. And Jacob did so, and fulfilled her week: and he gave him Rachel his daughter to wife also.” (Genesis 29:27–28)

Now I need to clear up something that trips up a lot of readers. *Fulfil her week* does not mean seven more years.

It means finish Leah’s wedding week, the seven day celebration that went with a marriage. Let Leah have her week.

Then you can have Rachel too, and you will owe me another seven years of work for her.

Here is the order, plain and simple:

* 🐑 Jacob works seven years, expecting Rachel.
* 🌙 He is tricked into marrying Leah.
* 🎉 He finishes Leah’s seven day wedding week.
* 💍 He marries Rachel.
* ⏳ Then he works the second seven years, after the wedding.

So Jacob did not wait fourteen years for Rachel.

About a week after the morning he woke up next to Leah, he married Rachel. The second seven years were worked afterward.

The best way I can put it is this. He got her on credit.

Rachel moved into his tent first, and he paid off the price over the next seven years.

I am not comparing a woman to a purchase.

I am explaining when the work happened, because people get this wrong all the time.

## Rachel More Than Leah

> “And Laban gave to Rachel his daughter Bilhah his handmaid to be her maid. And he went in also unto Rachel, and he loved also Rachel more than Leah, and served with him yet seven other years.” (Genesis 29:29–30)

Another servant girl. Bilhah to Rachel, like Zilpah to Leah.

Two sisters, two handmaids, one husband, all under one roof. Write those four names down. Leah, Zilpah, Rachel, Bilhah.

The family that is about to grow comes from all four of them.

And then the last line, the one I want you to carry out of today. *He loved also Rachel more than Leah.*

Does that sound familiar? Go back to the tent Jacob grew up in. Isaac loved Esau. Rebekah loved Jacob.

Two parents, two sons, and each parent had a favorite. We watched that split the family right down the middle.

It gave us the stolen blessing and the murder threat and this whole trip to Haran.

And now Jacob, who knows exactly what favoritism cost his family, sets up his own tent with a loved wife and a less loved wife.

I am not saying he was forced to. I am not saying everything that goes wrong later is his parents’ fault.

I am saying he carried a pattern from his father’s house into his own, and he did not even seem to notice he was doing it.

So here is where Day 12 leaves us. Jacob has the marriage he worked seven years for.

But he got there by being deceived, and I believe God used that to humble him, to let him feel the thing he had done to his father.

That is growth, not perfection. And now he has two wives. Rachel is loved more.

Leah is right there in the same household, watching. Where does that leave this family?

Children are coming, and they are going to be born into this.

How is God going to keep His promise of a people as many as the dust through a family already carrying this much tension?

The answer starts tomorrow, with a woman the Bible says was not loved, and a God who saw her.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 29

Sixteen verses that cover more than seven years.

The Bible moves fast through the waiting and then slows way down for one night and one morning. Let it.

Watch how carefully Jacob says what he wants in verse 18. Then watch what Laban actually gives him in verse 23.

Two servant girls get named in passing, Zilpah and Bilhah. Do not skip them.

They are going to be mothers in this family.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🤝 What Laban calls Jacob in verse 15
* 👀 How the two sisters are described in verse 17
* 💗 Which daughter Jacob names in verse 18
* ❓ The question Jacob asks in verse 25
* 💞 The last six words of verse 30

And think about this question: what does it feel like to be on the receiving end of something you once did to somebody else?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Jacob felt from the other side what he had done to his father. When has God let you feel the weight of something you once did to someone else?** That is not God being cruel. That is God teaching. What did you learn from it?
2. **Seven years felt like a few days because of who Jacob was waiting for. What are you willing to work and wait for that long?** If nothing comes to mind, ask whether you have been settling for things that come quick.
3. **Jacob carried his parents’ favoritism into his own tent without noticing. What pattern from the house you grew up in have you carried into yours?** Name it honestly. You cannot break a pattern you will not look at.

## Today's One Thing

**Add the Sisters**

Four women just entered the story, and the next generation of Abraham’s family is going to come through all of them.

Get them on the map, then do one thing about the pattern you named.

1. On your family map, write Leah beside Rachel under Laban. Draw a line from both of them to Jacob.
2. Under Leah, write Zilpah in smaller letters. Under Rachel, write Bilhah. Leave plenty of room under all four. You will need it.
3. Then think of one person in your life who gets less of your attention than they should. Do one thing for them today that you would normally do for your favorite.

Four names on the map, one act of fairness. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You let Jacob wake up to the wrong sister, and You used it.

The man who tricked his blind father learned what it feels like to be tricked in the dark. You did not shame him.

You taught him.

Thank You for the lessons I did not ask for. The mornings I woke up to the other side of my own behavior.

You were humbling me, not punishing me, and I did not always know the difference.

Make me honest, Lord. In my work, in my word, in my house.

Show me the patterns I carried from the home I grew up in. The favorites I play.

The people I love less without meaning to.

Give me eyes to see what I am doing before it splits my own family the way it split Jacob’s.

And teach me to wait like Jacob waited, with a love that makes seven years feel like a few days.

Give me something worth working that long for, and the patience to see it through.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
