Ask most people how many sons Abraham had, and they will say two. Isaac, the son of the promise.

Ishmael, the son of the shortcut. Maybe they remember the knife on the mountain.

Maybe they remember the boy and his mother walking into the desert with a skin of water.

Two sons. That is the Abraham most of us carry around.

But open your Bible to Genesis 25 and count. There are eight.

Eight sons with names, and from those eight sons come grandsons, and from the grandsons come whole peoples who are going to show up again and again in the rest of the Bible.

Traders on a desert road. A priest with seven daughters. An army so big it is compared to grasshoppers.

A friend sitting in the ashes with Job.

God promised Abraham he would be the father of many nations. Not one nation. Many.

This book is about what that promise actually looked like, and we are going to start with the six sons almost nobody remembers.

In the last book we buried Abraham.

Today we go back a few verses, to the family he left behind, and we meet a woman named Keturah.

# More Than Two Sons

> “And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts.” (Genesis 25:5–6)

Let me start with the verse that surprised me the first time I really read it.

> “Then again Abraham took a wife, and her name was Keturah. And she bare him Zimran, and Jokshan, and Medan, and Midian, and Ishbak, and Shuah.” (Genesis 25:1–2)

Another wife. Six more sons.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you know Sarah died at a hundred and twenty seven, and Abraham lived almost forty more years after her.

We touched on Keturah at the very end of that book, but we were busy burying Abraham.

Today we slow down and actually look at her and her boys.

## So Who Was Keturah

So who was Keturah?

Genesis 25 calls her Abraham’s *wife*.

Over in 1 Chronicles, where the family tree is repeated, she is called his *concubine*.

Both are true, and I want to explain that word, because it does not mean what it sounds like today.

A concubine in the ancient world was not a girlfriend and not a secret.

She was a recognized partner in the household, a wife of a second rank. Everybody knew about her.

Her children were real children of the man, with his name on them.

But she did not hold the place of the main wife, and her sons did not stand where the main wife’s son stood when it came time to hand down the estate.

You have already met a woman in this position. Hagar.

Genesis 16 says Sarah gave Hagar to Abraham *to be his wife*, and yet Hagar never once stood where Sarah stood.

That is what the Bible means when it calls Keturah both a wife and a concubine.

A real wife, a real place in the tent, but not Sarah’s place.

Where did she come from? Genesis does not say. No father is named, no hometown, no story of how they met.

But think about Abraham’s situation. Sarah is gone. Isaac is married and living in his own tent.

Abraham is an old man at the head of an enormous household, hundreds of people, servants born in his house and servants bought with his money, herds spread across the hills.

Somebody runs that house. And it makes sense to me that Keturah was already part of it.

Maybe a woman born inside that household. Maybe the daughter of one of his servants.

Someone who had been there for years, who knew how the tent worked, who became his companion after Sarah died and helped him rebuild a life.

There is even an old Jewish tradition that places Keturah among the daughters of Abraham’s household servants.

So the idea that she already belonged to this family has been around for a long time.

That is a picture, not a verse, so hold it loosely. But it is a human picture.

An old man who lost the wife he walked out of Ur with, and who did not spend his last forty years alone.

## A Family Map

Now the six sons. Say them with me, because you are going to meet their families again. Zimran. Jokshan. Medan. Midian.

Ishbak. Shuah.

> “And Jokshan begat Sheba, and Dedan. And the sons of Dedan were Asshurim, and Letushim, and Leummim. And the sons of Midian; Ephah, and Epher, and Hanoch, and Abida, and Eldaah. All these were the children of Keturah.” (Genesis 25:3–4)

I want to pause here and tell you why the Bible does this. Why it stops a story to give you a list of names.

A genealogy is a family map.

It is the Bible showing you who came from whom, so that when you meet a people four hundred years later you know where they started.

Think of it like this.

If someone tells you a story about the Smith family, and halfway through a stranger named Smith shows up, you want to know if he is related.

The genealogy answers that before you ask. These names are not filler.

They are the Bible saying, remember these, you will need them.

And here is something important for the whole book.

In the Bible, *father* can mean ancestor and *son* can mean descendant.

When God speaks to Jacob in Genesis 28 He calls Himself *the God of Abraham thy father*, and Abraham was Jacob’s grandfather.

So when you read that a people are the sons of Midian, it does not mean Midian personally fathered every one of them.

It means they came from him. Keep that in your pocket. We will use it every day.

Notice that only two of the six sons get their children listed.

Jokshan has Sheba and Dedan, and Dedan’s line turns into groups of people, the Asshurim and Letushim and Leummim.

Those endings tell you that by now the writer is talking about clans, not babies. And Midian has five sons named.

The others, Zimran, Medan, Ishbak and Shuah, are named and then the Bible moves on.

That does not mean they had no children.

It means the writer is pointing at the branches that matter most for the story ahead.

## Bildad the Shuhite

Let me give you the quick version of each, and then we will spend some time on the one that matters most.

* 🤫 Zimran is named here and in Chronicles and nowhere else. A son, a branch, and then silence.
* 🐪 Jokshan is the father of Sheba and Dedan. Those two names show up later as trading peoples on the desert routes south and east of Canaan, caravans and spices and gold.
* 👥 Medan is not Midian. The names are one letter apart, and they are two different brothers. Medan gets no story of his own.
* ⭐ Midian is the one to remember. We are coming back to him.
* 📜 Ishbak is a name and nothing more in Scripture.
* 🪨 Shuah is probably the ancestor of a people called the Shuhites. If that sounds familiar, it is because one of Job’s three friends is Bildad the Shuhite. The man who sat in the ashes with Job and then argued with him for chapters may well have been a descendant of Abraham through Keturah.

## Cousins Are Selling Cousins

Now, Midian.

Of all six brothers, this is the one whose family keeps walking back into the Bible.

His descendants are the Midianites, and they settle mostly in the desert country east and south of Canaan, down toward the top of the Red Sea.

Watch how often they show up.

When Joseph’s brothers throw him in a pit, who comes by?

Genesis 37 says *there passed by Midianites merchantmen*, and Joseph is pulled out of the pit and sold on his way to Egypt.

The traders who carry Abraham’s great grandson into slavery are, by blood, Abraham’s family too. Think about that.

The family is already so spread out that cousins are selling cousins without knowing it.

When Moses kills an Egyptian and runs for his life, where does he run? Exodus 2 says he *dwelt in the land of Midian*.

He sits down by a well, and the daughters of the priest of Midian come to water their flocks.

Moses marries one of them, Zipporah.

Their two boys, Gershom and Eliezer, have an Israelite father and a Midianite mother, which means they come from Abraham on both sides, through Isaac and through Keturah.

Moses’s father in law, Jethro, the priest of Midian, is the man who later gives Moses the best leadership advice in the book of Exodus.

Midian was a place of shelter and family for the man who would lead Israel out of Egypt.

And then it turns. In the book of Numbers, Midian helps lead Israel into sin at a place called Peor, and there is war.

In the book of Judges, the Midianites come up every harvest with their camels *as grasshoppers for multitude* and strip Israel’s fields bare for seven years, until God raises up Gideon.

Family can be a refuge and family can be a threat, sometimes in the same century. That is the Midianites.

So when you hear that name from now on, you will know. Midianites are the sons of Abraham.

Not the covenant line, but his blood, out there in the desert, living their own story next to Israel’s.

## All That He Had

Now the hard part of the passage.

> “And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts, and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he yet lived, eastward, unto the east country.” (Genesis 25:5–6)

Read that slowly. *All that he had* goes to Isaac.

The herds, the silver and gold, the servants, the camels, the field with the cave where Sarah is buried. All of it.

The other sons get *gifts*. And then they are sent away, east, while Abraham is still alive to see them go.

Why?

Because Isaac is the covenant son, and Abraham has known that for a long time. Go back to Genesis 17.

God told him, *Sarah thy wife shall bear thee a son indeed; and thou shalt call his name Isaac: and I will establish my covenant with him*.

In Genesis 21 God said it again, *in Isaac shall thy seed be called*.

And when Abraham sent his servant to find Isaac a wife, the servant told Rebekah’s family that Abraham *unto him hath given all that he hath*.

This was settled years before Keturah’s boys were grown. Abraham is not picking a favorite. He is protecting a promise.

## While He Yet Lived

Now I want you to see the wisdom in how he does it. He does not leave it for after his death.

He settles it *while he yet lived*.

He gives each of the other sons real gifts from a very rich man, livestock and goods and whatever a young man needs to start a household, and he sends them east, away from Isaac, to build their own lives.

Nobody is left standing around the grave arguing over the camels. Nobody is tempted to push Isaac out of his tent.

Abraham handles it with his own hands, in his own lifetime, and when he dies the family knows exactly where everyone stands.

That does not mean it was easy. Put yourself in Zimran’s sandals. Your father is Abraham.

You grew up in the biggest tent in the land.

And one day he calls you in, hands you a herd and some silver, and points east.

Your half brother keeps everything else, including the promise. How does that feel? The Bible does not tell us.

No complaint is recorded, no fight. But I do not think these were cold goodbyes.

Abraham had already walked Ishmael to the edge of the camp years before. He knew what it cost to let a son go.

Now he does it six more times.

## A Father of Many Nations

And here is what I want you to hold on to. Being sent east was not being thrown away. Those gifts were a start.

Those sons went out and became fathers of peoples. The Bible keeps track of them. God keeps track of them.

When He told Abraham he would be *a father of many nations*, these six were part of the answer.

Not the covenant, but still the blessing. Still the family.

That is the lesson I want you to carry out of Day 1.

God’s promise to Abraham was bigger than the one son we usually remember.

Isaac carries the covenant, the special line that leads to Israel and eventually to Jesus.

But the promise of many nations was fulfilled in the whole family, in sons who were sent east with gifts and became traders and priests and warriors and, yes, enemies.

God was faithful to all of it. He said many, and He meant many.

There is one more son of Abraham we have not talked about today, and he had twelve sons of his own.

Tomorrow we follow Ishmael and his twelve princes, and we find out what God did with a promise He made to a servant girl in the desert.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 25

This is a short passage, only six verses, and most of it is names. Do not skip the names.

Every one of them is a door into another part of the Bible.

Count the sons of Keturah as you read. Then notice which of them get their own children listed and which do not.

Watch what Abraham does with his property at the end. Who gets what, and where do the others go?

**As you read, notice:**

* 💍 What Keturah is called in verse 1
* 👶 The six names in verse 2
* 🌿 Which son’s children are listed in verse 4
* 🎁 The two different things Abraham gives in verses 5 and 6

And think about this question: why would a father give everything to one son and send the others away?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Before today, how many sons did you think Abraham had? What does it change for you to know there were eight?** Write down the two you knew and the six you did not. God kept track of all of them.
2. **Abraham settled his family while he was still alive, so nobody had to fight after he was gone. Is there something in your own life you are leaving for other people to sort out later?** A decision, a conversation, a piece of unfinished business. Name it honestly.
3. **The Midianites were Abraham’s family and still became both a refuge and a threat to Israel. Who in your family story has been both to you?** Family is complicated in the Bible too. Think about how God stayed faithful through all of it.
