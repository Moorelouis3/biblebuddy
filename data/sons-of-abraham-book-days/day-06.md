Yesterday we left Isaac in the best place he has ever been. The wells were settled.

The king of the Philistines had come to his camp, eaten his food, sworn peace and gone home.

God had spoken to him in the night at Beersheba and told him not to be afraid.

For once, nobody was fighting over anything.

Enjoy that for a second, because it does not last.

Today the trouble does not come from outside. No famine, no foreign king, no herdsmen arguing at a well.

It comes from inside the tent. An old man who cannot see. A wife who hears something she was not meant to hear.

A son out in the field with a bow.

And another son standing in his mother’s tent, being told to put on his brother’s clothes.

By the end of today nobody has lied to anybody yet. That is the strange thing about this passage.

It is seventeen verses of getting ready. A meal is cooked, skins are tied on, a plan is argued over and settled.

And the whole time you can feel what is coming, like watching someone lay out the pieces of a trap.

I want you to watch three people today and ask one question about each of them. What do they want?

Because everyone in this scene wants something good. That is what makes it so hard to read.

Let’s go into the tent.

# A Plan in the Dark

> “Upon me be thy curse, my son: only obey my voice, and go fetch me them.” (Genesis 27:13)

Yesterday we left Isaac at Beersheba.

The wells were settled, the king had gone home full, and God had spoken to Isaac in the night and told him not to be afraid.

It was the most peaceful moment of his life.

Hold that picture, because today the peace is gone, and the trouble comes from inside his own tent.

> “And it came to pass, that when Isaac was old, and his eyes were dim, so that he could not see, he called Esau his eldest son, and said unto him, My son: and he said unto him, Behold, here am I.” (Genesis 27:1)

How old is old?

The verse does not give a number, but we can work it out from the ages the Bible does give, and by a common calculation Isaac is around a hundred and thirty seven here.

That makes the twins about seventy seven.

I want you to get that in your head right now, because most of us picture this scene with two young men.

They are not young.

Esau and Jacob are grown men with grey in their beards, and their father is a very old man who can no longer see.

*Dim* here means more than needing to squint. In this scene Isaac is blind for every practical purpose.

He can hear, he can touch, he can smell, but he cannot look at a face and know who is standing in front of him.

Everything that happens today depends on that.

## One More Plate of Esau’s Cooking

> “And he said, Behold now, I am old, I know not the day of my death: Now therefore take, I pray thee, thy weapons, thy quiver and thy bow, and go out to the field, and take me some venison; And make me savoury meat, such as I love, and bring it to me, that I may eat; that my soul may bless thee before I die.” (Genesis 27:2–4)

Isaac thinks the end might be near. It was not.

He lives to a hundred and eighty, which means he has more than forty years left.

But he does not know that, and an old blind man who feels his strength going does what wise fathers do.

He puts his house in order.

Notice what he asks for first. Not the elders of the camp. Not a witness. He asks for a meal.

Venison in the King James Bible means wild game, meat that has been hunted, not only deer.

Esau is to take his quiver and his bow, walk out into open country, find an animal, kill it, carry it home and cook it the way his father likes it.

Go back to Day 3 and remember what Genesis 25 told us about these two.

*“And Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob.” (Genesis 25:28)* That has been the shape of this family for seventy years.

The father and the hunter. The mother and the quiet one.

And now, at the most important moment of Isaac’s life, the thing he reaches for is the food he loves from the son he loves.

One more plate of Esau’s cooking, and then the blessing.

Savoury meat just means a tasty dish, well seasoned, the kind of meal you remember.

And *that my soul may bless thee* means Isaac is going to bless Esau with everything in him.

The soul here is his whole self, his heart and his will. This is not a casual goodbye.

It is the solemn moment he has been saving.

## The Birthright and the Blessing

So we need to stop and ask the question I had the first time I read this. What exactly is Isaac about to hand over?

On Day 3 Esau sold his birthright to Jacob for a bowl of stew. If the birthright is already gone, what is left to bless?

Here is the way I have come to understand it, and I want to keep both pieces together, because they belong together.

The birthright was the firstborn’s claim on the family estate. In a household like this one, that estate was enormous.

Flocks and herds spread across the hills, stores of grain, silver, tents, servants born in the house.

Later in the Bible, in Deuteronomy, the rule is written down that the firstborn gets a double portion.

Picture it like this. Two sons, an estate worth three hundred thousand. Split it into three shares.

The firstborn gets two of them, two hundred thousand, and the other son gets one.

That is the birthright, and Esau swore it away for lunch. Jacob already has his brother’s word on that.

The blessing is something else, and it is bigger.

When a father like Isaac blesses his heir, he is naming the next leader of the family.

He is saying, this is the man who will stand where I stood. He will decide where the camp moves.

He will settle the quarrels. He will answer for every woman and child and servant and animal in this household.

And as he names him, the father prays over him.

He asks God for rain and harvest and protection, for authority over his brothers and over the peoples around him, for a curse on anyone who tries to harm him.

You will hear every one of those words tomorrow when Isaac actually speaks.

Leadership, responsibility and God’s favor, all tied together in one speech over one son.

And in this family the blessing carries something no other family on earth has. It carries Abraham.

God told Abraham, *“I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee” (Genesis 12:3)*, and tomorrow you will hear Isaac say almost the same words.

Later, in chapter 28, he will say it plainly and ask God to give Jacob *the blessing of Abraham*.

Then God Himself will speak to Jacob and confirm it. So this is not just a rich old man picking his manager.

It is the covenant moving to the next generation. Isaac met God face to face in chapter 26. Isaac has God’s ear.

Now he means to use it for Esau.

That is why the birthright and the blessing are two different losses.

When Esau cries out on Day 8, he will say Jacob took them *two times*, the birthright and the blessing. He is right.

One was the estate. The other was the leadership, the prayer and the promise.

One more thing before we leave this, because it will help you later. A father could split these things.

Years from now Jacob will give the double portion to Joseph by adopting Joseph’s two boys, Ephraim and Manasseh, as his own sons, while the rulership goes to Judah.

The book of Chronicles says it straight out.

Reuben lost the birthright and it went to Joseph’s sons, but the prince came from Judah. Keep that in your pocket.

For now, the point is that Isaac has one leading blessing to give, and he has decided who gets it.

## Rebekah Has Heard Everything

> “And Rebekah heard when Isaac spake to Esau his son. And Esau went to the field to hunt for venison, and to bring it.” (Genesis 27:5)

Two things happen in one verse. Esau leaves, and Rebekah has heard everything.

Think about what hunting means. This is not a walk to the flock.

Esau has to go out into open country with a bow, find an animal, get close enough to kill it, and bring it back.

That can take a whole day. It can take longer. And every hour he is gone is an hour the tent belongs to Rebekah.

Rebekah is not a bystander here.

Go back to the day she was pregnant and went to ask God why the babies were fighting inside her.

God told her, *“the elder shall serve the younger” (Genesis 25:23)*. She has carried that word for seventy seven years.

She loves Jacob. And now she hears her husband about to put everything on Esau.

I think all three of those things were in her at once, the promise, the love, and the fear of watching it go the other way.

The Bible does not tell us which one was loudest. It just shows us what she did.

> “And Rebekah spake unto Jacob her son, saying, Behold, I heard thy father speak unto Esau thy brother, saying, Bring me venison, and make me savoury meat, that I may eat, and bless thee before the LORD before my death.” (Genesis 27:6–7)

Read her report carefully next to what Isaac actually said. Isaac said *before I die*.

Rebekah says *before the LORD before my death*. She adds God’s name.

Now, people retell things in their own words all the time, and *before the LORD* is a true description of what this blessing is.

It is a solemn thing done in God’s presence. But notice what those three words do to Jacob.

They turn a family matter into a holy matter.

They say, this is not just your father’s preference, this is being settled in front of God, and once it is said, it is said.

If she wanted Jacob to feel that there was no time to think, that is exactly how to do it.

## Obey My Voice

> “Now therefore, my son, obey my voice according to that which I command thee. Go now to the flock, and fetch me from thence two good kids of the goats; and I will make them savoury meat for thy father, such as he loveth: And thou shalt bring it to thy father, that he may eat, and that he may bless thee before his death.” (Genesis 27:8–10)

Here is the plan, and it is fast. Esau is out in the field with a bow.

Jacob will walk to the flock, which is right there, and bring back two good kids of the goats, two choice young goats from their own animals.

Rebekah will cook them the way Isaac likes.

Goat is not wild game, but she has cooked for this man for most of a century, and she knows what he loves and how he loves it.

Then Jacob will carry it in, and the blind old man will eat and bless the son standing in front of him.

Look at the word *obey*. She will say it three times before this scene is over. *Obey my voice.* She is not asking.

She is commanding, and she is commanding him to lie to his father.

## What If I Get Caught

And here is what I want you to see about Jacob’s answer.

> “And Jacob said to Rebekah his mother, Behold, Esau my brother is a hairy man, and I am a smooth man: My father peradventure will feel me, and I shall seem to him as a deceiver; and I shall bring a curse upon me, and not a blessing.” (Genesis 27:11–12)

Jacob objects. But listen to what he objects to. Not, Mother, this is wrong. Not, this is my father and my brother.

His objection is, what if I get caught? Peradventure means perhaps, what if.

Remember, these two are twins, but they were never interchangeable.

On Day 3 we read that Esau came out *red, all over like an hairy garment*, and Jacob came out smooth, holding his brother’s heel.

Their father cannot see them, but he can feel them. And Jacob has found the hole in the plot.

If Isaac reaches out and touches smooth skin, the whole thing collapses, and instead of a blessing Jacob walks out with a curse, a solemn word spoken against him, the exact opposite of what he came for.

That is a practical objection. It is a man checking the plan for weak spots, not a man checking his conscience.

And I think that is the most honest moment in the whole scene, because it is exactly how most of us think when we want something badly enough.

We stop asking whether it is right and start asking whether it will work.

## Upon Me Be Thy Curse

> “And his mother said unto him, Upon me be thy curse, my son: only obey my voice, and go fetch me them.” (Genesis 27:13)

That is your key verse for today. *Upon me be thy curse.* Rebekah says, if it goes wrong, let it fall on me.

Just do what I say.

I want you to feel how easily that is said.

A curse from a patriarch is a serious thing, and she waves it away in five words so the plan can keep moving.

She cannot actually do that. A mother cannot stand in the road and catch a consequence meant for her son.

But in the moment, when the thing you want is right there and the only thing in the way is the risk, it is amazing how small the risk starts to look.

Keep this verse in mind on Day 9, when the consequences do come and we watch who ends up carrying them.

Here is the thing I keep coming back to. God had already spoken.

He told Rebekah before the boys were born that the younger would come out on top.

He did not need goat skins to make that true. Rebekah is not tricking God, and she is not trying to.

She is trying to get ahead of Isaac.

But what she is really doing is deciding that God’s promise needs her help, and that it needs it badly enough to be worth deceiving a blind man.

The disguise was never for God. It was for a husband who could not see and a son who was out hunting.

God saw every bit of it.

## Dressed as His Brother

> “And he went, and fetched, and brought them to his mother: and his mother made savoury meat, such as his father loved. And Rebekah took goodly raiment of her eldest son Esau, which were with her in the house, and put them upon Jacob her younger son: And she put the skins of the kids of the goats upon his hands, and upon the smooth of his neck: And she gave the savoury meat and the bread, which she had prepared, into the hand of her son Jacob.” (Genesis 27:14–17)

And Jacob goes. No more argument. Watch how fast the setup comes together once the objection is out of the way.

The goats are killed and cooked.

Goodly raiment means Esau’s good clothes, his best garments, kept in the main tent with his mother. Not godly.

Goodly, fine.

Rebekah dresses Jacob in his brother’s clothes, and those clothes carry his brother’s smell, the field and the sweat and the animals.

Remember that tomorrow.

Then she takes the skins from the same two goats and puts them on his hands and on the smooth of his neck, the exact places an old man’s fingers would go.

The same animals supply the meal and the costume. Nothing is wasted.

I cannot help noticing that the last time animal skins were put on somebody in Genesis, it was God clothing Adam and Eve to cover their shame.

Here skins are used to cover a man’s identity so he can lie. The same material, the opposite purpose.

I will not push that any further, but it stayed with me.

Then she puts the dish and the bread into his hands. And that is where today stops. The meal is hot. The clothes are on.

The skins are tied.

Jacob is standing in his mother’s tent dressed as his brother, holding a plate, with his father waiting somewhere in the dark.

So let me say plainly what I think today is about. Everybody in this scene wants to secure the future of the family.

Isaac wants it settled before he dies. Rebekah wants it to go the way God said.

Jacob wants what he has already been promised. Nobody is wrong about what they want.

But Rebekah and Jacob have decided that the way to get there is to lie to a blind man, and the only thing they argued about was whether they would get caught.

That is the danger I want you to see.

It is possible to want the right thing, even the thing God promised, and go after it in a way God never asked for and would never bless.

The want is not the problem. The shortcut is.

Tomorrow we follow Jacob to his father and watch what happens when the old man says, *who art thou, my son?*

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 27

This passage is all preparation. Nobody walks in to see Isaac today.

Read it slowly and notice how much work goes into setting up one lie.

Keep Day 3 in mind.

The twins were born different, one hairy and one smooth, and Esau sold his birthright for a bowl of stew.

Both of those facts matter today.

Listen to how Isaac describes the blessing in verse 4, and then how Rebekah repeats it in verse 7.

The words are not the same.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🍖 What Isaac asks for before he will give the blessing, and which son he asks
* 👂 Where Esau goes in verse 5, and what Rebekah does the moment he is gone
* 🤔 What Jacob objects to in verses 11 and 12, and what he does not object to
* 🔢 How many times Rebekah says the word obey

And think about this question: can you want the right thing and still go after it the wrong way?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Jacob’s only objection was, what if I get caught? When you want something badly, which question comes first for you: is it right, or will it work?** Be honest. Think of the last time you planned something you knew you would have to hide.
2. **Rebekah believed God’s promise and still felt she had to secure it with a lie. Where in your life are you trying to help God along in a way He never asked for?** Name the promise you are holding, and name the shortcut you are tempted to take to get there faster.
3. **Upon me be thy curse. Have you ever waved away a consequence just to keep a plan moving?** Who actually ended up paying for it? Write it down, even if it was you.

## Today's One Thing

**Name the Shortcut**

Rebekah and Jacob wanted something good and chose a dishonest way to get it.

Today, find the place where you are doing the same thing.

1. Write down one thing you want badly right now. A job, a relationship, a result, a yes from somebody.
2. Under it, write the shortcut. The thing you have been tempted to shade, hide, exaggerate or arrange so it goes your way. Then write who it would cost.
3. Under that, write the straight road. One honest step you can take today toward the same thing, with no disguise. Then take it before you go to bed.

One want, one shortcut named, one honest step taken. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You spoke to Rebekah before her sons were born, and You meant it.

You did not need her goat skins and You did not need her lie.

Your promises do not depend on my cleverness, and I need to hear that today.

Forgive me for the times I wanted something good and reached for it in a way You never asked for.

For the plans I made in the dark. For the times my only question was whether I would get caught.

Teach me to want what You promised and to wait for it Your way.

When I am tempted to help You along, slow me down. Show me the hole in the plot before I walk through the tent flap.

Give me the courage to say, this is wrong, and not just, this is risky.

And guard the people who trust me. Do not let me use anyone’s blindness, or anyone’s love, to get what I want.

Make me a person my family can believe.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
