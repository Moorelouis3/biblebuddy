Yesterday Jacob got on the camels and ran.

Wives, children, servants, flocks, everything he owned, across the Euphrates and south toward the hills of Gilead, while Laban was off with the shearers and his sons were three days away with the flocks.

It takes three days for the news to reach Laban. Then he moves.

He gathers his kinsmen and rides hard for a week, and he catches the caravan in the mountains.

Now picture that camp. Jacob’s tents spread across a hillside. Laban’s men pitched close enough to see the fires.

A father who says he has been robbed of his daughters.

A son in law with twenty years of anger finally ready to come out.

And somewhere in Rachel’s tent, a bag of stolen gods that Jacob does not know about.

This could end in blood.

The only reason it does not is a dream God sent to Laban the night before, and six words that tie his hands.

Today Laban gets to say everything he has been saving up. Then Jacob gets his turn.

And when the shouting is done, two men who do not trust each other pile up a heap of stones and ask God to watch the line between them.

# The Heap of Witness

> “God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight.” (Genesis 31:42)

Today we are reading Genesis chapter 31, verses 22 to 55. Yesterday ended with Jacob’s caravan pointed at mount Gilead.

Today starts with Laban finding out.

> “And it was told Laban on the third day that Jacob was fled. And he took his brethren with him, and pursued after him seven days’ journey; and they overtook him in the mount Gilead.” (Genesis 31:22–23)

The third day. Here is a little irony I want you to catch.

On Day 15 Laban set three days’ journey between his flocks and Jacob’s, to keep the marked animals away from him.

Now that same distance works against him. Jacob is three days gone before anyone can even tell Laban he is gone.

The gap Laban built to cheat Jacob is the gap that lets Jacob get away.

Then Laban moves. His brethren here means his kinsmen, the men of his extended family, probably his sons among them.

Armed men on fast animals, with no children and no flocks to slow them.

Jacob’s caravan has toddlers on camels and sheep that have to graze.

Laban pursues seven days and overtakes him, catches up with him, in the hill country of Gilead.

## God Reaches Laban

> “And God came to Laban the Syrian in a dream by night, and said unto him, Take heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad.” (Genesis 31:24)

Before Laban reaches the camp, God reaches Laban.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you have seen this before.

When Abimelech took Sarah into his house, *“God came to Abimelech in a dream by night” (Genesis 20:3)* and warned him.

When someone is about to hurt the family God has promised to bless, He goes to that person first, in the dark.

Jacob got a dream at Bethel. Now Laban gets one in Gilead.

And I want to explain what God says, because it reads strangely. *Speak not to Jacob either good or bad.*

That does not mean do not talk to him at all. Laban is about to talk for a solid page.

It is an old way of saying, do not try to change his course. Not with sweet talk, not with threats.

Do not bribe him into staying and do not frighten him into staying.

God has told Jacob to go home, and Laban is not to interfere in either direction.

Then Laban overtakes Jacob, and the Bible says both men pitched in mount Gilead. Picture the ground.

Gilead is not one peak.

It is a wide stretch of high rolling country east of the Jordan, ridges and valleys and grazing land.

Jacob’s household is spread across it, tents and pens and fires, a small town on the move.

Laban’s men pitch close enough to walk over. Everybody in both camps can feel what is about to happen.

## Here Comes the Loving Father

> “And Laban said to Jacob, What hast thou done, that thou hast stolen away unawares to me, and carried away my daughters, as captives taken with the sword? Wherefore didst thou flee away secretly, and steal away from me; and didst not tell me, that I might have sent thee away with mirth, and with songs, with tabret, and with harp? And hast not suffered me to kiss my sons and my daughters? thou hast now done foolishly in so doing.” (Genesis 31:26–28)

Here comes the loving father. Listen to how he builds it.

*Carried away my daughters, as captives taken with the sword.* That is war language.

When the four kings raided Sodom in The Obedience of Abraham, they took Lot and his goods and marched off with them.

That is what Laban is comparing this to. Jacob the raider, dragging women off at sword point.

Never mind that the women got on the camels themselves and said, whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do.

Then the party he says he would have thrown. Mirth is joy, celebration. Songs.

A tabret, which is a small hand drum, something like a tambourine. A harp.

Laban paints a picture of the farewell Jacob robbed him of.

And then the grandfather. *Hast not suffered me to kiss my sons and my daughters.* Suffered means allowed.

You did not let me kiss my grandchildren goodbye. *Thou hast now done foolishly.* You acted like a fool.

Now, hold that speech up against yesterday.

Rachel and Leah, in private, out in the field, said their father counted them as strangers, sold them, and ate their money.

The father says, I would have sung you down the road with drums. Both cannot be true. And I believe the daughters.

A man who sells his girls for fourteen years of labor and keeps every penny does not get to stand on a hillside and talk about the party he was planning.

This is Laban performing for the kinsmen standing behind him.

The women who lived in his house already told us who he was.

## The Mask Slips

> “It is in the power of my hand to do you hurt: but the God of your father spake unto me yesternight, saying, Take thou heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad.” (Genesis 31:29)

And here the mask slips. *It is in the power of my hand to do you hurt.* I could crush you right now. I have the men.

That is the real Laban, and he says it out loud. Then he has to admit why he will not.

*The God of your father spake unto me.* Notice he does not say my God. He says your father’s God.

Laban has his own gods, little ones, in a bag, and we are about to go looking for them.

But the God of Isaac showed up in his dream last night and told him to stand down, and Laban is enough of a realist to listen.

He does not become a believer. He just knows a bigger power when it speaks to him.

Then Laban says, fine, you had to go, you were homesick for your father’s house, *yet wherefore hast thou stolen my gods?*

And there it is, the thing he actually rode seven days for. Not the daughters. Not the grandchildren.

The images, the household idols we saw Rachel take yesterday.

To Laban those little figures meant protection, blessing, maybe the rights to his household.

To lose them was to lose his luck, and he wants them back.

## Some Gods They Are

> “And Jacob answered and said to Laban, Because I was afraid: for I said, Peradventure thou wouldest take by force thy daughters from me. With whomsoever thou findest thy gods, let him not live: before our brethren discern thou what is thine with me, and take it to thee. For Jacob knew not that Rachel had stolen them.” (Genesis 31:31–32)

Jacob answers the first charge honestly. *Because I was afraid.* I thought you would take your daughters back by force.

Laban just admitted he had the power to do it.

Then he answers the second charge with a death sentence. *With whomsoever thou findest thy gods, let him not live.*

Search the camp. Whoever has your gods dies.

Jacob says it in front of both families, with total confidence, because he knows he did not take them and cannot imagine anyone in his household would.

And the Bible tells you, in the same breath, what Jacob does not know. *Jacob knew not that Rachel had stolen them.*

He just put a curse on the wife he loves.

Feel the tension of the search. Laban goes tent by tent. Jacob’s. Leah’s. Bilhah’s and Zilpah’s. Nothing. Then Rachel’s.

And the Bible tells us she had put the images in the camel’s furniture, the saddle and bags that go on a camel’s back, and sat on them.

Her father is tearing through her tent, inches from the thing that would get her killed.

> “And she said to her father, Let it not displease my lord that I cannot rise up before thee; for the custom of women is upon me. And he searched, but found not the images.” (Genesis 31:35)

In that world a daughter stood up when her father came in. Rachel does not.

She says, forgive me, I cannot get up, *the custom of women is upon me*. It is her monthly period.

In that culture a man did not go near a woman at that time, and he certainly did not go digging under where she was sitting.

So Laban backs off, and the gods he says are so powerful end up under a woman who tells him she is unclean.

The Bible is quietly laughing at those idols. Some gods they are, if you can sit on them.

Why did she take them? I have thought about this a lot. Rachel grew up in that house.

Those figures were on the shelf her whole life, and some part of her may have believed they carried protection for a long road into a land she had never seen.

Or she may have wanted to strip her father of the thing he trusted, one last blow for the man who sold her.

Some people think the idols carried rights to the family property. It could be more than one of those at once.

What I know is that she took them and lied to keep them. I will not tell you that Jacob’s words killed her.

Rachel dies years later, in childbirth, and the Bible does not connect the two.

But a woman who grabs idols on her way out of her father’s tent has not fully left her father’s house yet.

## Twenty Years in Thy House

> “And Jacob was wroth, and chode with Laban: and Jacob answered and said to Laban, What is my trespass? what is my sin, that thou hast so hotly pursued after me?” (Genesis 31:36)

Now Jacob is angry. Wroth is furious. Chode is the old past tense of chide. He let him have it.

Twenty years of swallowed words come up at once. You searched every bag I own. What did you find?

Lay it out here in front of everybody. And when Laban has nothing to lay out, Jacob keeps going.

> “That which was torn of beasts I brought not unto thee; I bare the loss of it; of my hand didst thou require it, whether stolen by day, or stolen by night. Thus I was; in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep departed from mine eyes.” (Genesis 31:39–40)

This is the speech of a good shepherd who had a bad master. Your ewes did not miscarry on my watch.

I never ate one of your rams. When a lion took a lamb, I paid for it myself.

I stood in the heat all day and the frost all night and I did not sleep.

Every line of that is the life of a shepherd in that country, and Laban knows it is true.

> “Thus have I been twenty years in thy house; I served thee fourteen years for thy two daughters, and six years for thy cattle: and thou hast changed my wages ten times. Except the God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty. God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight.” (Genesis 31:41–42)

Here is the whole twenty years in two verses. Fourteen for the daughters. Six for the flocks. Ten changed wages.

And then the sentence I want you to carry out of today.

*Except the God of my father had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty.*

If God had not been on my side, you would have let me walk out of here with nothing.

Jacob is not bragging about his sticks anymore. He knows exactly who kept him.

And look at that name for God. *The fear of Isaac.* The God of Abraham, and the One Isaac stands in awe of.

Jacob is claiming his father’s God as his own, out loud, in front of Laban’s kinsmen. Then the closing line.

*God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight.*

That dream you had last night, Laban, that was God telling you off on my behalf.

Laban has no answer, so he does what Laban does. He claims everything.

*“These daughters are my daughters, and these children are my children, and these cattle are my cattle, and all that thou seest is mine” (Genesis 31:43)*.

He has just lost the argument and he is still saying the whole camp belongs to him. But then, *what can I do this day?*

He knows he cannot take any of it. God has tied his hands.

So he says, *“let us make a covenant, I and thou; and let it be for a witness between me and thee” (Genesis 31:44)*.

A covenant is a formal agreement with God as the witness. Not because he has grown to love Jacob.

Because it is the only move he has left.

## The LORD Watch Between Me and Thee

> “And Jacob took a stone, and set it up for a pillar. And Jacob said unto his brethren, Gather stones; and they took stones, and made an heap: and they did eat there upon the heap. And Laban called it Jegarsahadutha: but Jacob called it Galeed.” (Genesis 31:45–47)

Jacob sets up a standing stone, the way he did at Bethel.

The men pile up a heap of stones next to it, and they eat on it, a shared meal, which in that world sealed a deal.

Then the two men name the heap, each in his own language. Jegarsahadutha is Aramaic, Laban’s tongue.

Galeed is Hebrew, Jacob’s. Both mean the same thing, heap of witness. One pile of rocks, two languages.

These two families are splitting into two peoples, and from here on they will not even share a word for the same stones.

> “And Laban said, This heap is a witness between me and thee this day. Therefore was the name of it called Galeed; And Mizpah; for he said, The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another.” (Genesis 31:48–49)

Mizpah means watchtower.

You may have seen that verse on a necklace, *the LORD watch between me and thee*, and it sounds like a tender goodbye between friends.

It is not. Hear it the way Laban meant it. I cannot see you anymore after today, so God had better keep an eye on you.

It is a warning, not a blessing.

These men do not trust each other, and they are asking God to stand guard on the line between them.

Laban adds his conditions. Do not afflict my daughters. Do not take other wives beside them.

Nobody from my family will be watching, so God is the witness. I will say this for him.

Underneath the performance, there may be a father who really does not want his girls hurt in a far country.

It is the one decent thing he asks for.

## Jacob Has Picked a Side

> “This heap be witness, and this pillar be witness, that I will not pass over this heap to thee, and that thou shalt not pass over this heap and this pillar unto me, for harm. The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge betwixt us. And Jacob sware by the fear of his father Isaac.” (Genesis 31:52–53)

This is the heart of the covenant. A boundary. I will not cross this heap to hurt you. You will not cross it to hurt me.

The stones are a border, and both men swear to stay behind it.

Laban calls on the God of Abraham and the God of Nahor, Abraham’s brother, the ancestor of Laban’s side.

He is hedging, naming every family god he can. Jacob swears by one. *The fear of his father Isaac.*

Jacob has picked a side.

> “And early in the morning Laban rose up, and kissed his sons and his daughters, and blessed them: and Laban departed, and returned unto his place.” (Genesis 31:55)

Jacob offers a sacrifice on the mountain, the families eat together one more time and sleep on the hill.

And in the morning, Laban gets the thing he said he was robbed of. He kisses his grandsons and his daughters.

He blesses them. And he goes home. That is the last we see of him.

Twenty years of Laban, and he walks off the page with a kiss and a heap of stones between him and the family he could not hold.

So step back and look at the whole day.

A man with armed kinsmen and every reason to use them caught up with Jacob and could not touch him, because God got to Laban first.

Laban told a story about what kind of father he was, and the stones remember a different one.

Here is the lesson I want you to carry. Jacob said it best.

*Except the God of my father had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty.*

That is the truth about every Laban in your life.

If you walked out with anything at all, it is because God was with you in that house. He saw your affliction.

He saw the labour of your hands. And when it was time, He rebuked the one who had you, and He let you go.

Laban rides north. Jacob turns south, toward the Jordan and the land God promised him.

And the moment Laban is gone, a different fear comes up over the hills. Esau is down there.

Twenty years ago he swore to kill his brother, and tomorrow Jacob has to face him. But today is not finished yet.

Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 31

This is a long passage and most of it is two men talking. Read it like a courtroom scene.

Laban makes his case first, then Jacob answers, then they settle.

Keep yesterday’s verses 14 to 16 in your head. Rachel and Leah told you what kind of father Laban was.

Now listen to how Laban describes himself.

Watch Rachel closely in verses 33 to 35. She is in real danger and she knows it.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🌙 What God tells Laban in the dream in verse 24
* 🎶 How Laban says he would have sent Jacob off in verse 27
* ⚖️ What Jacob swears about the thief in verse 32, not knowing who it is
* 🪨 The two names for the heap of stones, and what Mizpah means

And think about this question: when two people cannot trust each other, what does it take to let each other go in peace?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Laban said, I would have sent you off with drums and harps. His daughters said, he sold us. Who in your life tells a story about themselves that the people closest to them would not recognize?** Be honest if the answer is you. Ask God to show you the gap between your speech and your house.
2. **Rachel left her father’s tent with his idols hidden in her saddle. What have you carried out of your old life that you should have left behind?** A habit, a fear, a way of thinking you grew up with. Name it. You cannot sit on it forever.
3. **Jacob said, if God had not been with me, you would have sent me away empty. Looking back at a hard season, where can you see now that God was with you the whole time?** Write down what you walked out with. Then write who gave it to you.

## Today's One Thing

**Build the Heap**

Jacob and Laban could not trust each other, so they built a boundary with God as the witness and walked away in peace.

Do the same with one relationship today.

1. Name one person you keep going back and forth with, where every conversation turns into a search of the tents.
2. Write one line that is your heap. I will not cross this to hurt you. Then write the one thing you need them not to cross.
3. Pray it with God as the witness. Then, if it is safe and right, say your line to them. Short, plain, no drums and harps.

One person, one boundary, one prayer with God watching the line. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You went to Laban in the dark before he ever reached Jacob’s tents. You get there first.

Before the people who could hurt me ever arrive, You have already spoken to them.

Thank You that You saw Jacob’s affliction and the labour of his hands.

Twenty years of heat and frost and changed wages, and You did not miss a single night of it. You see mine too.

If You had not been with me, I would have walked out of that house empty. You were with me.

Show me what I am still carrying out of my father’s tent.

The idols in the saddle, the old fears, the things I grabbed on the way out because I did not trust You to provide.

Help me set them down.

And where I cannot trust someone, teach me to build the heap.

A clear line, with You as the witness, and the grace to walk away in peace instead of in war.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
