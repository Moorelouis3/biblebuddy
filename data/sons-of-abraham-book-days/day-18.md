Twenty years ago a young man ran away from home with nothing but a staff in his hand and a death threat behind him.

His brother had said it out loud. When our father is dead, I will kill Jacob.

Yesterday Jacob finally shook loose from Laban.

The covenant heap is built, the father in law has gone home, and Jacob turns his face toward Canaan with two wives, eleven sons, servants, and herds that stretch across the hills.

But Canaan is where Esau is. And the last time Jacob saw Esau, Esau wanted him dead.

Today Jacob sends word ahead. The word that comes back is four hundred men.

Four hundred men coming his way, led by the brother he cheated.

And Jacob, the schemer, the grabber of heels, does three things in a row that tell you how much twenty years have changed him.

He splits his camp. He prays. And he gives away a fortune.

Then the brothers meet. One of them runs. One of them bows seven times. And both of them cry.

Today a feared enemy turns out to be a brother.

# I Come in Peace

> “Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau: for I fear him.” (Genesis 32:11)

Yesterday ended with Laban kissing his daughters goodbye and heading back north. Jacob is free. Here is the next verse.

> “And Jacob went on his way, and the angels of God met him. And when Jacob saw them, he said, This is God’s host: and he called the name of that place Mahanaim.” (Genesis 32:1–2)

Think about where you have seen angels in Jacob’s life before.

Back on Day 10, a scared young man slept with his head on a rock and saw a ladder to heaven with angels on it.

That was the night he left the land. Now, twenty years later, on the way back in, angels meet him again.

They are bookends on the whole journey.

And notice what the angels do not do. They do not say anything.

When God sends Gabriel to Zacharias and to Mary in Luke chapter 1, there are speeches and instructions.

Here there is nothing. Jacob just sees them, and that is the message.

I read it as God’s quiet nod after Laban and before Esau. I told you at Bethel I would keep you and bring you back.

I told you in Haran I would be with you. Look up. I am still here.

*Host* here means an army, a company. Mahanaim means two camps. His camp and God’s camp on the same hillside.

Hold on to that word, because a few verses from now Jacob is going to split his own people into two camps.

## My Lord Esau

> “And Jacob sent messengers before him to Esau his brother unto the land of Seir, the country of Edom. And he commanded them, saying, Thus shall ye speak unto my lord Esau; Thy servant Jacob saith thus, I have sojourned with Laban, and stayed there until now: And I have oxen, and asses, flocks, and menservants, and womenservants: and I have sent to tell my lord, that I may find grace in thy sight.” (Genesis 32:3–5)

Seir is the mountain country south and east of the Dead Sea, and Edom is the name of Esau’s people.

We will spend a whole day on it before this book is finished.

For now just notice that Jacob does not have to pass through Seir to get home. Canaan is straight ahead.

He is choosing to send word to his brother. Nobody is making him. That is the first sign that this is a different Jacob.

Now listen to the words he picks. *My lord Esau. Thy servant Jacob.*

Go back to Day 7 and hear what Isaac said over Jacob’s head when he thought he was blessing Esau.

*“Be lord over thy brethren, and let thy mother’s sons bow down to thee.” (Genesis 27:29)* Jacob stole the blessing that made him lord.

And now, with the blessing in his pocket, he calls Esau lord and himself servant.

The man who grabbed for the top spot is putting himself at the bottom.

Then he lists what he owns. Oxen, donkeys, flocks, servants. Why tell your angry brother you are rich?

I think the inventory is a message. I have my own. I did not come back to take yours.

All I want is to find grace in your sight. In plain words, I come in peace.

Think about who left and who is coming back. The Jacob who ran was a mama’s boy.

He stayed in the tents while Esau hunted, and his mother dressed him in his brother’s clothes.

The Jacob coming home has been cheated on his wedding night, had his wages changed ten times, stood in the frost watching another man’s sheep, and has a whole camp depending on him.

Getting used by Laban taught him what it feels like to be on the wrong end of a trick. I think it taught him humility.

Give him credit. He is not the same man.

## Greatly Afraid and Distressed

> “And the messengers returned to Jacob, saying, We came to thy brother Esau, and also he cometh to meet thee, and four hundred men with him.” (Genesis 32:6)

Four hundred men. Let me give you a sense of scale.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you remember the night four kings carried Lot away and Abraham went after them.

*“He armed his trained servants, born in his own house, three hundred and eighteen.” (Genesis 14:14)* Three hundred and eighteen men was enough to chase down an army of kings and win.

Esau is coming with four hundred. That is not a welcome party. That is more than the force that beat four kings.

Maybe it was an honor guard. Maybe that is just how a chief in Seir travels. But put yourself where Jacob is standing.

The last thing you heard about your brother was that he planned to kill you.

You send a humble message, and the answer is four hundred men on the road toward your wives and children.

What would you think?

> “Then Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed: and he divided the people that was with him, and the flocks, and herds, and the camels, into two bands; And said, If Esau come to the one company, and smite it, then the other company which is left shall escape.” (Genesis 32:7–8)

Greatly afraid and distressed. The Bible does not clean him up.

His first move is the move of a man who expects to lose half of everything. Two camps. If Esau hits one, the other runs.

That is what fear does. It plans for the worst. But look at what he does next.

## Prayer and Preparation

> “And Jacob said, O God of my father Abraham, and God of my father Isaac, the LORD which saidst unto me, Return unto thy country, and to thy kindred, and I will deal well with thee: I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies, and of all the truth, which thou hast shewed unto thy servant; for with my staff I passed over this Jordan; and now I am become two bands. Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau: for I fear him, lest he will come and smite me, and the mother with the children. And thou saidst, I will surely do thee good, and make thy seed as the sand of the sea, which cannot be numbered for multitude.” (Genesis 32:9–12)

Read that prayer out loud if you can.

It is the first long personal prayer we have from Jacob, and look how he builds it. He starts with who God is.

Then he reminds God of God’s own words. You told me to go home. You said You would deal well with me.

Then he gets small. I crossed this river with a stick and nothing else, and now I am two whole camps.

Then he says the thing out loud. Deliver me. I am afraid he will kill me and the mothers and the children.

And he ends by handing God His own promise. You said my children would be like sand on the shore.

Here is what struck me. Up to now in Genesis, when people get scared, they act. Abraham ran to Egypt in the famine.

Isaac lied about his wife in Gerar.

Jacob slipped away from Laban in the night, and even though God had told him to go, it was fear that made him sneak.

We do not read a prayer for protection before any of those moves.

But here, with four hundred men coming, Jacob stops and talks to God. You told me to come back. You promised.

Now I am scared. Help me.

That is a man bringing a real danger and a real fear to the God who made him a promise, and I think it is one of the most honest prayers in the book.

Notice too that he did not stop preparing just because he prayed. He split the camp, then prayed, then kept working.

Prayer and preparation are not enemies. Jacob did both.

## A Wave at a Time

> “And he lodged there that same night; and took of that which came to his hand a present for Esau his brother; Two hundred she goats, and twenty he goats, two hundred ewes, and twenty rams, Thirty milch camels with their colts, forty kine, and ten bulls, twenty she asses, and ten foals.” (Genesis 32:13–15)

Add it up. Two hundred and twenty goats. Two hundred and twenty sheep. Thirty mother camels with their babies.

Fifty cattle. Thirty donkeys. Five hundred and fifty animals, plus the camel young.

And *of that which came to his hand* means he took this out of what he had on hand. Not everything he owns. A slice.

But what a slice. Females and males of every kind.

Milch camels are camels giving milk, which means they have young and will have more. Kine are cows.

This is a breeding herd. Jacob is not giving Esau a nice dinner.

He is handing him a whole working livestock business that will multiply for years.

It is the difference between giving your brother a phone and giving him the store.

> “And he delivered them into the hand of his servants, every drove by themselves; and said unto his servants, Pass over before me, and put a space betwixt drove and drove.” (Genesis 32:16)

A drove is a herd being driven along the road. And here is the clever part.

Jacob does not send one giant wall of animals. He splits the gift into groups with a gap between each one.

Esau meets the goats, and the servant says, a gift from your servant Jacob. He rides on. Here come the sheep.

Same message. Camels. Cattle. Donkeys.

Five times in a row Esau runs into his brother’s generosity before he ever sees his brother’s face.

One big herd would be a blur. Five herds, one after another, let the size of it sink in.

Jacob is softening his brother a wave at a time.

> “And say ye moreover, Behold, thy servant Jacob is behind us. For he said, I will appease him with the present that goeth before me, and afterward I will see his face; peradventure he will accept of me.” (Genesis 32:20)

There it is in Jacob’s own words. Appease means to calm someone down, to turn away their anger.

Peradventure means maybe. He coaches every servant to say the same two things. This belongs to your servant Jacob.

And Jacob himself is behind us. Lord and servant, carried to Esau by every herd on the road.

## The Ford Jabbok

> “And he rose up that night, and took his two wives, and his two womenservants, and his eleven sons, and passed over the ford Jabbok. And he took them, and sent them over the brook, and sent over that he had.” (Genesis 32:22–23)

The Jabbok is a stream that runs down into the Jordan from the east, and a ford is a shallow place where you can walk across.

In the middle of the night Jacob gets his whole family up and moves them over the water, and then he sends everything he owns across after them.

One small thing I noticed. Two wives, two womenservants, eleven sons. Where is Dinah?

She was born back on Day 14, and she matters a great deal in the next book.

The list only counts the boys, because that is how lists were written in that world. It does not mean she was not there.

But the Bible is already pointing you at the sons, because the whole next volume is about them.

Now I have to be straight with you about how I built this book.

The very next verse, Genesis 32:24, says Jacob was left alone, and a man wrestled with him until morning.

That night, the night before he meets Esau, is the night Jacob becomes Israel.

I did not want to squeeze one of the most important nights in the Bible into the corner of a day about Esau.

So we step over it today and give it the whole of tomorrow.

Just know that between verse 23 and chapter 33, a whole night passed, and Jacob came out of it with a new name and a limp.

Skip ahead with me to the morning.

## Esau Ran to Meet Him

> “And Jacob lifted up his eyes, and looked, and, behold, Esau came, and with him four hundred men. And he divided the children unto Leah, and unto Rachel, and unto the two handmaids. And he put the handmaids and their children foremost, and Leah and her children after, and Rachel and Joseph hindermost.” (Genesis 33:1–2)

Four hundred men, exactly as feared. And Jacob lines up his family in an order that tells you his heart.

The servant women and their boys in front. Leah and her children next.

Rachel and Joseph at the very back, farthest from danger. It is not fair, and I will not pretend it is.

But it is honest.

> “And he passed over before them, and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother.” (Genesis 33:3)

Then Jacob does the thing that gets me every time. He walks out in front of all of them. Not hiding in the second camp.

In front. Alone. And he bows to the ground seven times, getting closer with each one.

That is how you approached a king in that world. Put that next to *let thy mother’s sons bow down to thee*.

The blessing said Esau would bow to Jacob. Jacob is bowing to Esau.

He is giving back with his body the thing he stole with his words.

> “And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept.” (Genesis 33:4)

Esau runs. He does not wait for the seventh bow. He does not send the four hundred men forward.

He throws his arms around his brother, falls on his neck, kisses him, and the two of them stand there and cry.

Twenty years of hate and fear and distance, and it ends in a hug on the road.

Go back to the prayer. *Deliver me from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau.* This is the answer.

God did not take the four hundred men away. The thing Jacob feared still came walking up the road.

But the hand he was afraid of grabbed him, and it was a hug. God did not remove the meeting.

He changed what happened in it.

## I Have Enough

> “And he lifted up his eyes, and saw the women and the children; and said, Who are those with thee? And he said, The children which God hath graciously given thy servant.” (Genesis 33:5)

Esau looks past Jacob and sees a crowd. Who are all these? Listen to the answer. The children God has graciously given.

Not look how well I have done. God gets the credit.

Then the women and children come forward in their groups and bow, and Esau stands there meeting a family he did not know existed.

> “And he said, What meanest thou by all this drove which I met? And he said, These are to find grace in the sight of my lord. And Esau said, I have enough, my brother; keep that thou hast unto thyself.” (Genesis 33:8–9)

What was all that livestock on the road about? To find grace with you, my lord.

And Esau says one of the best lines in Genesis. *I have enough, my brother.* Notice the word. Not servant. Brother.

Esau is not keeping score. He has done well in Seir, with his own herds, his own men, his own country.

Both brothers are rich now. Nobody is coming to take anything from anybody.

> “And Jacob said, Nay, I pray thee, if now I have found grace in thy sight, then receive my present at my hand: for therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me. Take, I pray thee, my blessing that is brought to thee; because God hath dealt graciously with me, and because I have enough. And he urged him, and he took it.” (Genesis 33:10–11)

Two things here, and both are loaded. First, Jacob says seeing Esau’s face is like seeing the face of God.

Tomorrow you will find out that Jacob saw God’s face the night before, and you will hear this line very differently.

Second, look at what Jacob calls the gift. *My blessing.* The man who stole a blessing is handing one over.

He cannot give back Isaac’s words, but he can give his brother five hundred and fifty animals and call it what it is.

And Esau takes it. In that world, taking the gift meant the quarrel was over.

Esau offers to ride along with him. Jacob says no, gently.

The children are *tender*, the flocks have young, and if they are pushed hard for one day they will die.

You go ahead, my lord, and I will lead on softly.

Esau offers to leave some men as an escort, and Jacob turns that down too. He still says my lord. He is still careful.

Twenty years with Laban taught him to keep things polite and keep his distance, and I do not blame him.

> “So Esau returned that day on his way unto Seir.” (Genesis 33:16)

And that is where we stop. Esau goes home to the south. Jacob turns toward Canaan.

The Bible does not record another visit between them until they stand at their father’s grave, and we will not invent one.

So here is what I want you to take from today. Jacob was afraid, and the Bible says so.

He split his camp like a man who expected the worst, and then he knelt down and told God exactly what he was scared of, holding God to God’s own promise.

Then he got back up and kept working.

He sent the gift, coached the servants, moved the family, walked out front and bowed. Prayer and preparation, both.

And when the four hundred men came over the hill, God had already been at work in Esau’s heart.

The feared enemy ran to him as a brother.

That is what I come in peace looks like. Not weakness.

A rich man with a big camp choosing to call his brother lord, giving away a herd, and bowing in the dirt, because peace with his family was worth more to him than being right.

And God honored it.

Tomorrow we go back to the river, to the night we skipped.

Jacob is alone in the dark, and a man steps out and takes hold of him.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 32

Read Genesis 32:1 to 23 first, then skip ahead and read Genesis 33:1 to 16.

We are reading both passages today, and we are deliberately stepping over the verses in between.

Genesis 32:24 to 32, the night Jacob wrestles with God, happens in the middle of today’s story.

We are saving it for tomorrow so it can have a whole day to itself.

Watch how Jacob talks about Esau in chapter 32.

Listen for the words my lord and thy servant, and remember who was supposed to be lord over whom.

Count the animals in verses 14 and 15. Then in chapter 33, watch who runs and who bows.

**As you read, notice:**

* 👼 Who meets Jacob in the very first verse of chapter 32
* ⚔️ The number the messengers bring back in verse 6
* 🙏 The three things Jacob does with his fear in verses 7 to 16
* 🤝 What Esau says in Genesis 33:9

And think about this question: what do you do with fear when the thing you are afraid of is already on its way?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Jacob told God exactly what he was afraid of and reminded Him of His promise. What fear have you been carrying that you have never actually said out loud to God?** Write it in a sentence. Then write the promise of God you want to hold Him to.
2. **Jacob called Esau my lord and bowed seven times, even though the blessing said it should be the other way around. Is there someone you need to lower yourself in front of to make peace?** Being right did not matter to Jacob as much as his brother did. What is it costing you to stay on top?
3. **God did not take the four hundred men away. He changed what happened when they arrived. Where are you asking God to remove something when He may be planning to meet you inside it?** Think of a hard meeting or a hard season ahead. Ask God to be in it, not just to cancel it.

## Today's One Thing

**Send the Gift First**

Jacob did not wait for Esau to make the first move. He sent word, sent gifts, and walked out front.

Do the same with one broken relationship today.

1. Name the person. Someone you have been avoiding because you are not sure how they will react. Write their name down.
2. Pray Jacob’s prayer over it in your own words. Tell God the fear, and remind Him of what He has promised you.
3. Send something ahead. A message, a call, a small gift, an apology. Make it humble. Let it say I come in peace before you ever see their face.

One name, one honest prayer, one gift sent ahead. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You met Jacob on the road with angels before he ever asked.

You were there when he was scared, and You are here when I am. Thank You for the quiet reminders that You have not left.

I want to pray the way Jacob prayed. You are the God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, and You are my God.

You have made me promises. And I am afraid.

I am saying it out loud. Deliver me from the thing I see coming over the hill.

Teach me to pray and prepare, to kneel and then get up and do the work.

Give me Jacob’s humility. Help me call my brother lord when every part of me wants to be right.

Help me send the gift first and bow before I am asked.

And when the four hundred men arrive, be in the meeting. Change what happens there.

Turn the hand I fear into an embrace, the way You did for Jacob on the road.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
