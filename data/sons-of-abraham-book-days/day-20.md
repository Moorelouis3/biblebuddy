Yesterday Jacob limped away from the river with a new name. The day before, Esau rode home to Seir.

Today we follow Esau.

I know what you are thinking when you open Genesis 36. Forty three verses, and almost every one of them is a name.

Dukes and sons and wives and kings. It is the chapter people skip. I used to skip it too.

But hidden inside this list is a story better than anything you will find on a screen tonight.

Two brothers who got so rich they could not live in the same country.

A man who married his own cousin, the daughter of Ishmael.

A grandson named Amalek whose children would become Israel’s worst enemy.

A whole people called the Horites who were living in those mountains first, and what happened to them.

And eight kings sitting on thrones in Edom before Israel had a king at all.

Back on Day 3 God told a pregnant woman that two nations were in her womb. Today we watch the second one get born.

Today a man named Red becomes a country called Edom.

# Two Nations

> “Thus dwelt Esau in mount Seir: Esau is Edom.” (Genesis 36:8)

Two days ago Esau turned south and rode home. Now the Bible stops following Jacob for a whole chapter and goes with him.

Here is how it opens.

> “Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom.” (Genesis 36:1)

Esau, who is Edom.

The Bible says it three times in this chapter, in verse 1, verse 8 and verse 19, because it wants you to connect the man with the nation.

Edom means red. Go back to Day 3. Esau came out of the womb *red, all over like an hairy garment*.

Then he came in from the field starving and begged his brother, *Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage*, and the Bible says, *therefore was his name called Edom*.

A nickname from a bowl of stew. And now that nickname is the name of a country.

Put that next to yesterday. Jacob got his new name from God, at the river, after a night of holding on.

Esau got his from a bad trade over a bowl of soup. Both names became nations. Israel and Edom.

Two brothers, two names, two peoples, exactly as God told their mother before they were born.

*Two nations are in thy womb.*

## Three Wives

> “Esau took his wives of the daughters of Canaan; Adah the daughter of Elon the Hittite, and Aholibamah the daughter of Anah the daughter of Zibeon the Hivite; And Bashemath Ishmael’s daughter, sister of Nebajoth.” (Genesis 36:2–3)

Three wives.

The Hittites were a Canaanite people, the sons of Heth, the same people Abraham bought the cave of Machpelah from.

The Hivites were another Canaanite people living in the hill country.

So two of Esau’s wives were local Canaanite girls, which is exactly what grieved his parents back on Day 8.

Now, if you have a good memory, something is bothering you.

On Day 8 the wives were named Judith, daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Bashemath, daughter of Elon the Hittite.

On Day 10 Esau went to Ishmael and married Mahalath, Ishmael’s daughter, the sister of Nebajoth.

Here the names are Adah, Aholibamah and Bashemath, and Bashemath is now Ishmael’s daughter.

The lists do not match, and the Bible never stops to line them up for us. Here is what I see.

The daughter of Elon the Hittite is in both lists. The daughter of Ishmael, sister of Nebajoth, is in both lists.

The same women are clearly in view.

People in that world often carried more than one name, and this chapter was written down using the names Edom remembered them by.

I am comfortable with that, and I do not want you stuck on it. The family is the same family.

What I do want you to stop on is the third wife. Bashemath, Ishmael’s daughter. Think about who that is.

Ishmael is Isaac’s half brother, the son Abraham sent into the desert.

That makes Ishmael Esau’s uncle, and it makes Bashemath Esau’s cousin.

Esau married into Abraham’s family through the other son.

So Esau’s children through her are Abraham’s great grandchildren on both sides, through Isaac and through Ishmael.

The sons of Abraham keep marrying each other.

> “And Adah bare to Esau Eliphaz; and Bashemath bare Reuel; And Aholibamah bare Jeush, and Jaalam, and Korah: these are the sons of Esau, which were born unto him in the land of Canaan.” (Genesis 36:4–5)

Five sons. Eliphaz from Adah. Reuel from Bashemath. Jeush, Jaalam and Korah from Aholibamah.

Say them once, because two of them matter. Eliphaz is the firstborn, and we are coming back to him.

All five were born in Canaan, before the move. Now here is the move.

## Esau in Mount Seir

> “And Esau took his wives, and his sons, and his daughters, and all the persons of his house, and his cattle, and all his beasts, and all his substance, which he had got in the land of Canaan; and went into the country from the face of his brother Jacob. For their riches were more than that they might dwell together; and the land wherein they were strangers could not bear them because of their cattle. Thus dwelt Esau in mount Seir: Esau is Edom.” (Genesis 36:6–8)

Read that reason again. *Their riches were more than that they might dwell together.*

Esau did not leave because he was pushed out, or poor, or beaten.

He left because both brothers were so rich that one piece of land could not hold them.

Look at what he is bringing with him.

Wives, sons, daughters, every person in his house, cattle, every animal, everything he had gotten in Canaan.

That is a wealthy man moving a wealthy household.

And where does he go? Mount Seir, the rough mountain country south and east of the Dead Sea.

From Hebron, where Isaac was living, that is roughly a hundred and forty kilometers in a straight line, and much farther with herds on a winding road.

Far enough that the two brothers would not be sharing a well again. The Bible never tells us when Esau died.

It just tells us that he settled in Seir and became Edom.

## Two Things That Hit Me

Now I want to stop here and share two things that hit me when I studied these three verses.

Both of them matter beyond this chapter.

The first one is about money.

I think a lot of Christians grow up believing that money is evil, or that God does not want His people to be wealthy.

But when I read the Bible, I see something different. Look at Abraham and Lot in Genesis 13.

They had so much livestock and silver and gold that the land could not support both households living together.

Think about that. They were so wealthy that they literally had to separate. Now look at Jacob and Esau.

Years later, Jacob comes home to Canaan, and the same thing happens again.

Both brothers have become so wealthy, with so many animals and possessions, that the land cannot hold them together.

So Esau moves to Mount Seir. That is two generations of the same family experiencing incredible wealth.

So where did we get the idea that God does not want His people to prosper?

The Bible does not teach that money itself is evil. It warns us about the love of money.

*“For the love of money is the root of all evil.” (1 Timothy 6:10)* There is a difference between having wealth and letting wealth control your heart.

Being rich does not automatically mean someone has God’s approval, and being poor does not mean someone lacks His blessing.

But these stories make one thing clear. God is not against His people having abundance.

The question is not simply how much we have. It is what place those possessions hold in our hearts.

The second thing is about the Bible itself. Does this story sound familiar? Go back to Genesis 13.

Abraham and Lot were traveling together, but their households had grown so large that the land could not support them both.

Their herdsmen started arguing, and Abraham suggested they separate.

Now here we are in Genesis 36, watching something remarkably similar happen with Jacob and Esau.

There is even a striking similarity in the wording.

*“And the land was not able to bear them, that they might dwell together.” (Genesis 13:6)* And here, *“For their riches were more than that they might dwell together.” (Genesis 36:7)* Two different generations.

Two different sets of relatives. Almost the exact same situation.

But notice the difference. Abraham and Lot separated after a fight between their herdsmen.

With Jacob and Esau, Genesis points only at how much wealth and livestock they had. No fight is recorded.

I love finding connections like this, because they remind us that the Bible is not a collection of unrelated stories.

We are following one family across generations, watching its members grow, make decisions, and become nations.

History repeats itself in this family, and when you read Genesis straight through, you get to watch it happen.

## Israel Was Fighting Family

> “And the sons of Eliphaz were Teman, Omar, Zepho, and Gatam, and Kenaz. And Timna was concubine to Eliphaz Esau’s son; and she bare to Eliphaz Amalek: these were the sons of Adah Esau’s wife.” (Genesis 36:11–12)

Now the grandsons. Eliphaz, the firstborn, has five sons by his wife and one more by a concubine named Timna.

We explained that word on Day 1.

A concubine was a real partner in the household, a wife of a second rank, and her children were real children.

Timna’s boy is named Amalek.

Remember that name. The Amalekites come from him, and they are the first enemy Israel fights after leaving Egypt.

In Exodus 17, Israel has barely crossed the Red Sea when *then came Amalek, and fought with Israel in Rephidim*.

Moses stands on a hill with his hands raised while Joshua fights below, and afterward God says He will have war with Amalek from generation to generation.

Centuries later King Saul loses his throne over the Amalekites. And they come from here. From Esau’s grandson.

Abraham’s great great grandson, by a concubine, in the mountains of Seir.

When Israel fought Amalek, Israel was fighting family.

You may remember that way back in Genesis 14, in Abraham’s day, the four kings smote *all the country of the Amalekites*.

That was before Amalek was born.

The Bible is doing there what it did with Beersheba in the last book, using the name people knew later to tell you where a place was.

Amalek’s people settled that southern desert country, and that is what it was called by the time Genesis was written down.

> “These were dukes of the sons of Esau: the sons of Eliphaz the firstborn son of Esau; duke Teman, duke Omar, duke Zepho, duke Kenaz, Duke Korah, duke Gatam, and duke Amalek: these are the dukes that came of Eliphaz in the land of Edom.” (Genesis 36:15–16)

Now the word that throws everyone. Duke. Forget castles and England.

A duke here is a clan chief, the head of a family group, the man the tents gathered around.

What you are watching in verses 15 to 19 is Esau’s grandsons growing up into leaders of their own clans.

Fourteen duke names are listed, and Korah shows up twice, once under Eliphaz and once under Aholibamah, so do not try to turn this into an exact count of tribes.

The point is bigger than the count. A single man’s household has turned into an organized people with chiefs.

## Seir the Horite

> “These are the sons of Seir the Horite, who inhabited the land; Lotan, and Shobal, and Zibeon, and Anah, And Dishon, and Ezer, and Dishan: these are the dukes of the Horites, the children of Seir in the land of Edom.” (Genesis 36:20–21)

Here is where the chapter changes families. Seir the Horite.

The Horites were the people already living in those mountains before Esau ever arrived.

They were there in Abraham’s day.

Genesis 14 lists *the Horites in their mount Seir* among the peoples the four kings attacked.

Seir has seven sons, and verses 22 to 30 give you their children and their own dukes.

This was not a few people in caves.

They had families, chiefs, and a way of life, and the mountain was named after their father.

And look closely, because the two families are tangled together.

Esau’s wife Aholibamah is the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon.

Zibeon and Anah are right here in the Horite list, verses 24 and 25.

And Timna, the concubine who bore Amalek, shows up in verse 22 as *Lotan’s sister*, a Horite woman.

Esau and his sons married into the people of the land. Amalek had a Horite mother.

So what happened to the Horites?

For that you have to jump ahead to Deuteronomy 2, where Moses is explaining Israel’s neighbors to a new generation.

> “The Horims also dwelt in Seir beforetime; but the children of Esau succeeded them, when they had destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their stead.” (Deuteronomy 2:12)

Horims is just another spelling of Horites. And destroyed means what it says.

The Hebrew word is *shamad*, and it means wiped out, killed off, brought to nothing.

It is the same word used in Joshua 11:20 for what Israel did to the Canaanite armies.

Esau’s descendants did not politely ask the Horites to move over. They fought them, destroyed them, and took their land.

The Bible does not tell us how that war started. But think about Esau’s father.

On Day 5 we read Abimelech telling Isaac, *“Go from us; for thou art much mightier than we.” (Genesis 26:16)* Isaac had grown so big that the people around him wanted him gone.

It makes sense to me that the same thing happened a generation later in Seir.

Esau arrives with a household the size of a town, and it keeps growing, and it marries into the local families, and at some point the people who were there first look at this family and get afraid.

That is the kind of fear that turns into war. And Esau’s side won.

## I Have Given Mount Seir to Esau

Now here is the part that really caught my attention. Listen to what God says about it.

> “Meddle not with them; for I will not give you of their land, no, not so much as a foot breadth; because I have given mount Seir unto Esau for a possession.” (Deuteronomy 2:5)

I have given Mount Seir to Esau. God says it. Not Esau took it. God gave it.

Deuteronomy 2:22 says it again, that God destroyed the Horims before the children of Esau so they could live there.

God Himself gave Esau a land of his own and helped his descendants win it, and then He told Israel to keep their hands off it.

Think about that. Esau is not the covenant son. The promise does not run through him. And God still gave him a country.

That is why this chapter is in your Bible. God told Rebekah two nations. He told Abraham *I will make nations of thee*.

Here is a nation. Jacob’s family becomes Israel. Esau’s family becomes Edom.

And the God who was keeping His promise to Jacob was keeping His word to Esau at the same time.

## Kings Shall Come Out of Thee

> “And these are the kings that reigned in the land of Edom, before there reigned any king over the children of Israel.” (Genesis 36:31)

Now kings. Eight of them, verses 32 to 39. Bela, Jobab, Husham, Hadad, Samlah, Saul, Baalhanan, Hadar.

Notice two things. First, none of them is the son of the one before. Each comes from a different city.

This is not a royal family passing down a throne. It is a people choosing a strong man from one place, then another.

Second, catch the little story in verse 35. Hadad *smote Midian in the field of Moab*. Midian.

The sons of Keturah from Day 1.

Abraham’s descendants through Esau fighting Abraham’s descendants through Keturah, out in Moab.

The family is already at war with itself, and Israel is not even in the picture yet.

And look at verse 31 again. *Before there reigned any king over the children of Israel.*

Edom had kings while Israel was still in Egypt, or wandering, or ruled by judges.

Back in Genesis 17, God told Abraham *kings shall come out of thee*.

The first kings to come out of Abraham were not David’s line. They were Esau’s.

The chapter ends with one more list of dukes, verses 40 to 43, but this one is different.

These are sorted *after their places*, by where they lived. The first list was clans by family.

This one is a map of Edom by region. Timnah, Alvah, Jetheth, and so on, each name a district in the mountains.

And then the last line. *He is Esau the father of the Edomites.*

So here is what I want you to carry out of the chapter everybody skips.

A man who sold his birthright for stew, who lost the blessing, who was not the covenant son, still became the father of a nation with chiefs and cities and kings, in a land God Himself gave him.

God is bigger than the one line we follow. He keeps track of the brother who went south.

He keeps His word to the ones outside the covenant too.

And the sons of Abraham, every branch of them, are going to keep bumping into each other for the rest of the Bible.

Edom and Israel. Amalek and Israel. Edom and Midian. One family, spread across a map.

Tomorrow is our last day together.

We are going to step all the way back and look at that whole map at once, every son of Abraham and every people who came from him, and find out just how big God’s promise really was.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 36

This is a long list, so read it the way you would read a family tree, not a story. Do not try to hold every name.

Watch for the ones that repeat, and watch for the little sentences tucked in between the names. Those are the story.

Verses 6 to 8 explain why Esau left Canaan. Read those slowly.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, they will sound familiar.

Notice that the chapter is not only about Esau’s family. Verse 20 starts a different family altogether.

**As you read, notice:**

* 👪 Whose daughter Bashemath is in verse 3
* 💰 The reason Esau moves in verse 7
* 🛡️ The name of Eliphaz’s son in verse 12
* 👑 What verse 31 says about kings

And think about this question: why would God put a whole chapter about Esau’s family in a book that is following Jacob’s?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Esau and Jacob both got rich enough to need their own countries. Where did you pick up the idea that God does not want His people to have abundance, and does Genesis agree with it?** Then ask the harder question. What place do your possessions hold in your heart?
2. **God gave Mount Seir to Esau, the brother outside the covenant. Who in your life have you quietly decided God is finished with?** Write their name. God gave Esau a nation. Do not close an account God is still keeping.
3. **The same story happened to Abraham and Lot, then to Jacob and Esau. What pattern keeps repeating in your own family?** Name it honestly. The Bible shows you patterns so you can see your own.
