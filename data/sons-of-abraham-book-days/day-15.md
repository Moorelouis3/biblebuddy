Yesterday ended with a baby in Rachel’s arms. Joseph. The son she had begged for, cried for, bargained for.

God remembered her, and the camp now has eleven boys and a girl.

And the moment that baby is born, something shifts in Jacob.

Fourteen years ago he walked into Laban’s camp with nothing but a staff and a promise from God at Bethel.

He worked seven years for a wife and got the wrong one. He worked seven more for the right one.

He has built his father in law’s herds from a handful into a multitude, and he does not own a single animal.

So today Jacob walks up to Laban and says, send me away.

And Laban, who has never once let anything valuable walk out of his camp, does what Laban always does. He makes a deal.

Then he changes it before the ink is dry.

What follows is one of the strangest passages in Genesis. Striped sticks in watering troughs.

Speckled goats and brown sheep. Two clever men trying to outsmart each other over a herd.

And underneath all the cleverness, a God who has already decided how this ends.

Today Jacob finally starts building his own house.

# Wages You Can See

> “And the man increased exceedingly, and had much cattle, and maidservants, and menservants, and camels, and asses.” (Genesis 30:43)

Today we are reading Genesis chapter 30, verses 25 to 43. We pick up the very moment yesterday ended.

> “And it came to pass, when Rachel had born Joseph, that Jacob said unto Laban, Send me away, that I may go unto mine own place, and to my country. Give me my wives and my children, for whom I have served thee, and let me go: for thou knowest my service which I have done thee.” (Genesis 30:25–26)

Look at the timing. *When Rachel had born Joseph.*

The second seven years are up, and the wife he loves finally has a son of her own. Something in Jacob says, now.

And listen to how he says it. *Send me away. Give me my wives and my children. Let me go.* Three times in two verses.

He is asking to be released. After fourteen years, Jacob still cannot just pack up and leave.

Everything he has, including the women he married, came through a deal with this man.

His wives and children are his, but he has to say *give me* anyway. That tells you how tight Laban’s grip is.

Remember how he got here.

On Day 8 Rebekah told him, *“tarry with him a few days, until thy brother’s fury turn away” (Genesis 27:44)*.

A few days. That was fourteen years ago. Nobody ever sent for him.

And now the man he was supposed to stay with for a few days is about to ask him to tarry some more.

## One Long Tarry

> “And Laban said unto him, I pray thee, if I have found favour in thine eyes, tarry: for I have learned by experience that the LORD hath blessed me for thy sake. And he said, Appoint me thy wages, and I will give it.” (Genesis 30:27–28)

There is that word again. *Tarry.* Stay. Rebekah said it, and now Laban says it.

Jacob’s whole life in Haran has been one long tarry.

But notice how Laban talks. *If I have found favour in thine eyes.*

That is the language of someone asking for a kindness.

Laban, the man who switched brides on his own son in law, is now the one asking. Why? Because he has done the math.

*I have learned by experience that the LORD hath blessed me for thy sake.*

Laban has watched his herds grow for fourteen years, and he knows exactly why. Not because Laban is wise.

Because Jacob is here, and God is with Jacob.

This is a pattern in Abraham’s family.

God told Abraham, *“I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and thou shalt be a blessing” (Genesis 12:2)* .

The people around this family get blessed just by standing near it.

Years from now, Joseph will be a slave in Egypt, and the Bible will say *“the LORD blessed the Egyptian’s house for Joseph’s sake” (Genesis 39:5)*.

Potiphar got rich because Joseph was in his house. Laban got rich because Jacob was in his.

The blessing spills over onto whoever is holding this family.

And here is what strikes me about Laban. He sees it. He says it out loud. And his response is not to seek the LORD.

It is to keep Jacob. He wants the blessing without the God who gives it. So he says, name your price.

*Appoint me thy wages.*

## Mine Own House

> “And he said unto him, Thou knowest how I have served thee, and how thy cattle was with me. For it was little which thou hadst before I came, and it is now increased unto a multitude; and the LORD hath blessed thee since my coming: and now when shall I provide for mine own house also?” (Genesis 30:29–30)

Jacob lays it out. You know how I have worked. You know what your herds were like when I showed up. Little.

And now, a multitude. He is stating the record, and Laban cannot argue with any of it.

Then the question that has been building for fourteen years. *When shall I provide for mine own house also?*

Jacob has spent fourteen years building another man’s house.

His sweat went into Laban’s herds, Laban’s wealth, Laban’s name.

Fourteen years, twelve children, and not one goat to call his own. When do I get to build something that is mine?

Here is how I think about it. Jacob helped grow this business from almost nothing.

He is not asking to own part of Laban’s herd.

He is asking to share in what it produces from here on, like a partner who finally gets a cut of what he helped build.

## The Audit Is Built In

> “And he said, What shall I give thee? And Jacob said, Thou shalt not give me any thing: if thou wilt do this thing for me, I will again feed and keep thy flock: I will pass through all thy flock to day, removing from thence all the speckled and spotted cattle, and all the brown cattle among the sheep, and the spotted and speckled among the goats: and of such shall be my hire.” (Genesis 30:31–32)

Now the deal.

First, a word. Cattle in the King James does not mean cows. It means livestock, here sheep and goats.

In that part of the world, sheep were normally white, and goats were normally dark all over.

An animal with the wrong colouring stood out. A brown sheep. A goat with white spots or speckles.

Not sick, not runts, just unusual. And Jacob says, those are my wages.

Every oddly marked animal from now on belongs to me. The normal ones stay yours.

Why would he ask for that? Because you can see it. Listen to his reason.

> “So shall my righteousness answer for me in time to come, when it shall come for my hire before thy face: every one that is not speckled and spotted among the goats, and brown among the sheep, that shall be counted stolen with me.” (Genesis 30:33)

Righteousness here means honesty. My honesty will speak for itself.

Jacob has worked for this man long enough to know he cannot be trusted with a vague arrangement.

So he builds the proof into the animals. Anyone can walk through Jacob’s flock and check.

Spotted, speckled, brown, it is Jacob’s. A plain white sheep in Jacob’s pen, that one was stolen.

You cannot argue with a goat’s coat. The audit is built in.

And understand what animals meant. A flock is not money sitting still. Sheep give wool, milk, meat, and lambs.

Goats give milk, hair, meat, and kids. A flock makes more flock.

If Jacob gets even a small number of marked animals, and they breed, he has a growing business without selling a single one.

## Yes With His Mouth

> “And Laban said, Behold, I would it might be according to thy word.” (Genesis 30:34)

Laban says yes. Of course he does. The odd animals are the minority.

Most of the flock is white sheep and dark goats, and those stay his.

From where Laban sits, Jacob just asked for the scraps. He thinks he is getting a bargain.

And then he does something that tells you everything about him.

> “And he removed that day the he goats that were ringstraked and spotted, and all the she goats that were speckled and spotted, and every one that had some white in it, and all the brown among the sheep, and gave them into the hand of his sons. And he set three days’ journey betwixt himself and Jacob: and Jacob fed the rest of Laban’s flocks.” (Genesis 30:35–36)

Read that again slowly. *He removed that day.*

Jacob said he would walk through the flock and pull out the marked animals. Laban beats him to it.

He pulls out every spotted goat, every speckled one, every goat with any white on it at all, ringstraked meaning striped, and every brown sheep, and he hands them to his own sons.

Then he marches them three days away. Three days.

Far enough that none of them will ever breed with the flock Jacob is tending.

Let me make this simple with made up numbers.

Say Laban has eighty white sheep and ten brown ones, eighty plain goats and ten spotted ones.

Jacob’s wages were supposed to start with those twenty marked animals.

Laban removes all twenty and leaves Jacob a hundred and sixty plain ones.

Jacob owns nothing, again, and has to wait for plain parents to produce marked babies, which does not happen often.

Laban agreed to the deal and gutted it in the same afternoon.

That is Laban. He says yes with his mouth and no with his hands. He did it with the wedding, and he does it again here.

And I think I see why, even though it is wrong. Laban is scared.

He just admitted that God has blessed him because of Jacob.

If Jacob gets wealthy enough to leave, the blessing leaves with him.

So Laban makes sure the pay is as small as possible, so that Jacob never has enough to walk away.

That is a man living from fear.

He has the man God is blessing right there in his camp, and instead of trusting the God, he tries to trap the man.

## Rods of Green Poplar

Now watch what Jacob does.

> “And Jacob took him rods of green poplar, and of the hazel and chesnut tree; and pilled white strakes in them, and made the white appear which was in the rods. And he set the rods which he had pilled before the flocks in the gutters in the watering troughs when the flocks came to drink, that they should conceive when they came to drink.” (Genesis 30:37–38)

This is the part everyone remembers and nobody understands, so let me walk through it.

Jacob cuts fresh branches, green meaning still full of sap. Pilled means peeled.

He strips off bark in stripes so that the pale wood underneath shows through, and now he has a handful of striped sticks.

He stands them up in the gutters, the channels that carry water into the troughs where the animals drink.

And animals in heat mate where they gather, which is at the water.

What is he doing?

In the ancient world people believed that what a mother saw at the moment she conceived could mark the baby.

Striped sticks in front of mating goats, and you get striped kids. It is not how breeding works.

A goat’s coat comes from what its parents carry inside them, not from what it looked at.

But Jacob believed in the sticks, the way Rachel believed in the mandrakes yesterday, and he did what a man in his world would do.

And here is a small thing I noticed. Watering troughs.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you remember the girl who ran back and forth filling the trough for a stranger’s camels.

That girl was Rebekah, Jacob’s mother, and Laban’s sister. The trough was where this family was found.

Now her son is standing at a trough, trying to get free of her brother.

> “And the flocks conceived before the rods, and brought forth cattle ringstraked, speckled, and spotted. And Jacob did separate the lambs, and set the faces of the flocks toward the ringstraked, and all the brown in the flock of Laban; and he put his own flocks by themselves, and put them not unto Laban’s cattle.” (Genesis 30:39–40)

It works. The animals start producing marked young. Not because of the sticks.

Jump ahead one chapter and Jacob himself will explain it.

God showed him in a dream that the rams mounting the flock were striped and speckled, and the angel said, *“I have seen all that Laban doeth unto thee” (Genesis 31:12)*.

God saw Laban’s trick. God was running the breeding. The sticks were Jacob’s idea. The results were God’s.

And Jacob does what a smart herdsman does. He separates.

The marked lambs go into their own flock, away from Laban’s animals, so there is never a question of whose is whose.

The business has started.

## Jacob Is Still Jacob

> “And it came to pass, whensoever the stronger cattle did conceive, that Jacob laid the rods before the eyes of the cattle in the gutters, that they might conceive among the rods. But when the cattle were feeble, he put them not in: so the feebler were Laban’s, and the stronger Jacob’s.” (Genesis 30:41–42)

Here is Jacob’s own trick. When the strong animals are mating, out come the sticks.

When the weak ones are mating, he leaves the sticks in his tent.

So the strong animals produce marked young, which are his, and the weak ones produce plain young, which are Laban’s.

Over a few seasons, Jacob’s flock is the healthy one and Laban’s is the feeble one.

Laban tried to pull a fast one by removing all the marked animals.

Jacob answers by making sure every marked animal from now on comes from the best stock. Laban wanted Jacob poor.

Jacob makes himself strong. And I will say it plainly: Jacob is still Jacob.

The man who bought a birthright with stew and stole a blessing in a goatskin is still working the angles.

But this time he is working them against a man who cheated him first, and God is on his side of the trough.

## Two Camps Coming Back

> “And the man increased exceedingly, and had much cattle, and maidservants, and menservants, and camels, and asses.” (Genesis 30:43)

Look at that list. Flocks. Women servants. Men servants. Camels. Donkeys. That is not a herdsman with a few goats.

That is a household that can move across a desert and feed itself on the way.

Fourteen years with nothing, then six years with the marked animals, and he walks out of Haran rich.

Put it next to the rest of the family. *“And Abram was very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold.” (Genesis 13:2)* Isaac sowed in Gerar and *“the man waxed great, and went forward, and grew until he became very great” (Genesis 26:13)*.

Now Jacob, who came with a staff.

Three generations, each ending up with more than they started with, under a God who said He would bless them.

Jacob will say it himself later, *“for with my staff I passed over this Jordan; and now I am become two bands” (Genesis 32:10)*.

One stick going out. Two camps coming back.

So step back and look at the whole day. Jacob asked to go home and got told to tarry.

He made a fair deal with visible wages, and Laban gutted it the same afternoon.

Jacob answered with sticks and separation and strong stock, and somehow, against a man who held every card, he came out rich.

Here is the lesson I want you to carry. The sticks did not make Jacob rich. Laban could not make Jacob poor.

God had said at Bethel, *I am with thee, and will keep thee*, and twenty years of a cheating father in law could not undo one word of it.

You may be working for a Laban right now.

Somebody who takes what you build and moves the goalposts every time you get close. Do your honest work. Be smart.

But know this. Your wages are not in Laban’s hands. They were never in Laban’s hands.

Tomorrow, Laban’s sons start grumbling, Laban’s face changes, and God says the word Jacob has waited twenty years to hear.

Return. But today is not finished yet.

Take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 30

This passage is a business negotiation. Read it like one.

Who asks for what, who agrees, and who quietly changes the terms.

The word cattle in the King James does not mean cows. It means livestock in general, sheep and goats here.

Keep that in mind or the whole chapter gets confusing.

The sticks in the troughs will seem odd. Do not get stuck on them.

Watch what Jacob is trying to do and what actually happens.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🙏 What Laban admits in verse 27 about why he has prospered
* 🐐 The exact animals Jacob asks for in verse 32
* 🚶 What Laban does the same day in verse 35, and how far away he goes
* 🐪 The list of what Jacob owns by verse 43

And think about this question: how do you build something of your own when you work for a man who keeps moving the goalposts?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Laban knew God was blessing him because of Jacob, and his answer was to hold on to Jacob instead of seeking God. Where are you clinging to a person or a job instead of the God behind the blessing?** Name the Jacob in your life. Then ask who actually sent the blessing.
2. **Jacob worked fourteen years building another man’s house before he asked, when shall I provide for mine own house also? Is there something of your own you have been putting off building?** Write it down. Then write the first small step, the way Jacob started with a few spotted goats.
3. **Laban agreed to the deal and gutted it the same day. Have you ever said yes with your mouth and no with your hands?** A promise you quietly worked around. Be honest about it, and about what fear was behind it.

## Today's One Thing

**Count the Flock**

Jacob walked into Haran with a staff and walked out with camels.

God did that over twenty years, a few animals at a time. Today, count what He has done for you.

1. Write down what you had five years ago. Be specific. Job, home, people, skills, money, faith.
2. Next to it, write what you have now. Do not skip the small things. A spotted goat was a small thing.
3. Circle everything on the second list that you could not have made happen by yourself. Then thank God for each one out loud.

Two lists, and a circle around what only God could do. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You told Jacob at Bethel that You would be with him and keep him, and then You let him work twenty years for a man who cheated him.

And You kept him anyway. Through every changed wage and every moved goalpost, You kept him.

Thank You that my wages are not in anybody else’s hands.

Not my boss, not my family, not the person who takes what I build. You see what Laban does. You have always seen it.

Teach me to work honestly and trust You with the results.

Forgive me for the times I have been Laban. For saying yes and then holding back.

For clinging to the people You bless instead of seeking You. Break that fear in me.

And when I get tired of building someone else’s house, remind me that You are the one who increases a man exceedingly.

A staff going out. Two camps coming home. Do that in me, in Your time.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
