Yesterday Jacob walked out of his father’s tent with the blessing.

Five lies, a plate of goat, his brother’s clothes, and the biggest words a father in this family can say, all spoken over the wrong son.

Isaac leaned back, full and satisfied, believing he had just settled the future with Esau.

Esau is about to walk in.

He has been out in the field all this time doing exactly what his father asked. He found the animal. He killed it.

He carried it home and cooked it the way his father loves.

He is coming through the tent flap with a hot dish and a glad heart, and he has no idea what happened while he was gone.

Today is the hardest day in this part of the book, and I want to warn you about that. An old man is going to shake.

A grown man is going to cry out loud.

Nobody in this scene is going to get what they wanted, not even Jacob, who is already gone.

But before we follow Esau into the tent, we need to go back a few verses and pick up two lines the Bible told us earlier about him, about who he married and what it did to his parents.

I saved them for today on purpose. They explain some of the weight Esau is already carrying when he walks in.

Then we go in with him.

# A Bitter Cry

> “Hast thou but one blessing, my father? bless me, even me also, O my father.” (Genesis 27:38)

Before Esau walks in, let me take you back.

This is a flashback, two verses from the end of chapter 26 that I held back on Day 5 so we could read them here.

> “And Esau was forty years old when he took to wife Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Bashemath the daughter of Elon the Hittite: Which were a grief of mind unto Isaac and to Rebekah.” (Genesis 26:34–35)

Esau married at forty. Two wives, Judith and Bashemath, both Hittite women.

The Hittites here are a people living in Canaan, the same people the Bible also calls the children of Heth.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you have met them.

Ephron the Hittite is the man who sold Abraham the cave of Machpelah to bury Sarah. These were the neighbors.

Esau married two of their daughters.

And it was *a grief of mind* to his parents. That is strong language. Not a little disappointment.

A heaviness, a bitterness of spirit that stayed with Isaac and Rebekah. Why?

Because of something Abraham had done a generation earlier.

When it was time for Isaac to marry, Abraham made his servant swear an oath that he would not take a wife for Isaac *of the daughters of the Canaanites*, but would go back to Abraham’s own family.

The reason was not that Canaanite people were worthless. The reason was the promise.

This family was carrying something from God, and Abraham wanted the next generation raised in a home that knew it.

Isaac got Rebekah that way. Esau went another way, twice, and his parents felt it every day for thirty seven years.

I am not telling you this to make Esau the bad guy.

I am telling you so you know what he is already carrying when he walks through that tent flap.

He sold his birthright on Day 3. His marriages have been a sore spot in the house for decades.

He knows his father loves him anyway. And today he has done everything his father asked.

Hold all of that, and now watch him come home.

## Scarce Gone Out

> “And it came to pass, as soon as Isaac had made an end of blessing Jacob, and Jacob was yet scarce gone out from the presence of Isaac his father, that Esau his brother came in from his hunting.” (Genesis 27:30)

Look at the timing. *Scarce gone out.* Jacob has barely left. The blessing is still hanging in the air of the tent.

If Esau had come back an hour sooner, there would be no story. The Bible wants you to feel how close it was.

> “And he also had made savoury meat, and brought it unto his father, and said unto his father, Let my father arise, and eat of his son’s venison, that thy soul may bless me. And Isaac his father said unto him, Who art thou? And he said, I am thy son, thy firstborn Esau.” (Genesis 27:31–32)

Hear it. Those are almost exactly the words Jacob said yesterday. *Eat of my venison, that thy soul may bless me. I am thy firstborn.*

Yesterday they were lies. Today every word of them is true. This really is the firstborn. This really is venison.

He really did what his father told him. And the old man says, *Who art thou?*

The same question he asked yesterday, and this time the honest answer is the one that breaks everything open.

Esau does not know yet. Put yourself in his place. You have been out hunting for hours.

You are tired, you are pleased with yourself, you cooked the meal just right.

You are expecting your father to sit up and smile. And instead he asks who you are, like he does not know you.

## Trembled Very Exceedingly

> “And Isaac trembled very exceedingly, and said, Who? where is he that hath taken venison, and brought it me, and I have eaten of all before thou camest, and have blessed him? yea, and he shall be blessed.” (Genesis 27:33)

*Trembled very exceedingly.* In Hebrew the words pile up on each other, a trembling, a very great trembling.

This is not an old man’s hands shaking a little. This is a whole body shudder.

Isaac is sitting there blind, and in one second the last hour rearranges itself in his head.

The voice that sounded like Jacob. The strange timing. The hands that felt right. The kiss.

He put the pieces together, and the picture is his own son.

Listen to his questions. *Who? Where is he?* He is not asking for information the way a judge does.

He is a father saying, I cannot believe he did this. He took advantage of me. I could not see, and he used it.

I trusted him, and he used that too. That is what the trembling is.

It is a man realizing he has been betrayed by someone who leaned in and kissed him.

We do not know yet whether he knows Rebekah was behind it. The scene does not say.

But you know, and that makes it heavier.

And then, still shaking, he says the thing that settles the day. *Yea, and he shall be blessed.*

Yes. The blessing stands. Isaac does not say, I take it back, let’s start over. He cannot.

Remember what this blessing was on Day 6. Not a wish.

A father, with God’s ear, naming the next leader of the family and praying God’s provision and authority over him, carrying Abraham’s promise forward.

It was spoken in God’s presence, and it was spoken over Jacob. Isaac wanted Esau. God had said Jacob.

And when Isaac realizes what has happened, he does not fight it. He is shaking, he is heartbroken, and he lets it stand.

## Bless Me, Even Me Also

> “And when Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry, and said unto his father, Bless me, even me also, O my father.” (Genesis 27:34)

I want you to stop and let this be as loud as it is. *A great and exceeding bitter cry.*

A grown man, a hunter, seventy seven years old, crying out loud in his father’s tent. Not sulking. Not storming off.

Crying. And then the plea. *Bless me, even me also, O my father.* Me too. Have you got something for me?

I think Esau deserves a minute here, and I do not want to rush past him to get to the point.

Yes, he sold his birthright for stew, and he was careless about it. Yes, his marriages hurt his parents.

Those were his choices. But what just happened to him was not his choice. He did the right thing today.

He went and hunted and cooked and came home, and while he was gone his brother wore his clothes and took the one thing he still had coming.

Both things are true. He made his own mistakes, and he was also genuinely robbed. Do not let one truth erase the other.

> “And he said, Thy brother came with subtilty, and hath taken away thy blessing.” (Genesis 27:35)

This is Isaac talking to Esau. Subtilty means deceit, trickery. Isaac is not saying Jacob tiptoed in quietly.

He is saying Jacob lied. The false name, the goat, the skins, the clothes, God’s name on the whole thing.

Isaac names it exactly for what it was.

## Rightly Named Jacob

> “And he said, Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times: he took away my birthright; and, behold, now he hath taken away my blessing. And he said, Hast thou not reserved a blessing for me?” (Genesis 27:36)

Now Esau. And he goes straight to his brother’s name.

On Day 3 we saw the twins born, and the second one came out with *his hand took hold on Esau’s heel*, so they called him Jacob, which sounds like the Hebrew word for heel.

Esau takes that name and turns it into a verdict. *Is not he rightly named?*

He grabbed my heel at birth and he has been grabbing ever since.

Supplanted means pushed out of my place, took my spot by getting around me. Esau is saying, he has done it to me twice.

He took the birthright, and now he has taken the blessing.

Now, be fair to the text. The first time, Esau sold the birthright. He swore an oath and ate the stew and walked away.

That one he gave up. The second time is different. Nobody asked Esau anything.

His father was lied to while he was out of the house.

So when he says *he took away my birthright*, that is grief talking, and grief does not always keep good records.

But when he says *now he hath taken away my blessing*, he is exactly right.

And then the question I think every reader feels in their chest. *Hast thou not reserved a blessing for me?*

You did not save anything for me? Not even a little? It sounds like a child, and in a way it is.

It is a son asking his father if he still counts.

## Hast Thou But One Blessing

> “And Isaac answered and said unto Esau, Behold, I have made him thy lord, and all his brethren have I given to him for servants; and with corn and wine have I sustained him: and what shall I do now unto thee, my son?” (Genesis 27:37)

Here is why Isaac cannot just say the same words again. Go back to what he spoke yesterday.

*Be lord over thy brethren, and let thy mother’s sons bow down to thee.* He made Jacob the head.

He gave him the brothers as servants, which in a family of two sons means Esau.

He prayed grain and wine over him, the provision and the prosperity.

All of that has already been said, and you cannot make two men lord over each other.

If Jacob is the head of this family, Esau is not. Isaac is not out of love. He is out of room.

*What shall I do now unto thee, my son?* He is as stuck as Esau is.

And notice what has quietly happened. God told Rebekah before the boys were born, *the elder shall serve the younger*.

Isaac just said it with his own mouth, to the elder’s face. *I have made him thy lord.*

Not because God told Isaac to say it. Isaac was trying to say the opposite.

But the story has come out exactly where God said it would.

> “And Esau said unto his father, Hast thou but one blessing, my father? bless me, even me also, O my father. And Esau lifted up his voice, and wept.” (Genesis 27:38)

Your key verse. He asks again, the same words, and this time he breaks. *Esau lifted up his voice, and wept.*

I do not think he is being stubborn. I think he cannot take in that it is really gone.

Some losses you have to ask about three times before they become real.

He is a man standing in the wreckage of a day that started so well, asking his blind father if there is anything left.

And there is. Not the same thing. But something.

## By Thy Sword Shalt Thou Live

> “And Isaac his father answered and said unto him, Behold, thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven from above; And by thy sword shalt thou live, and shalt serve thy brother; and it shall come to pass when thou shalt have the dominion, that thou shalt break his yoke from off thy neck.” (Genesis 27:39–40)

Let me be honest with you about verse 39, because if you compare Bibles you will notice something.

The King James reads it the way we just did.

Esau’s dwelling will be *of the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven*, which sounds like the same good land Jacob got.

But the little Hebrew word behind *of* can mean from, as in coming from, or it can mean away from.

Several newer translations read it the second way, that Esau’s home will be away from the rich earth and away from the dew.

Esau’s people did end up in rough, dry hill country south and east of the land, so both readings have something to them.

I am not going to pretend the King James settles it.

What I will say is that verse 40 settles the important part, and nobody argues about verse 40.

*By thy sword shalt thou live.* Esau’s life, and his family’s life, will be a fighting life.

Not a settled farm with a full table. A life you hold with a blade.

*And shalt serve thy brother.* There it is again, the painful family order. Jacob leads. Esau follows.

Said now for the second time in one day, and this time to Esau directly.

*And it shall come to pass when thou shalt have the dominion, that thou shalt break his yoke from off thy neck.*

A yoke is the wooden bar a farmer lays across the necks of two oxen to make them pull together, and in the Bible it is a picture of being under somebody’s control.

Isaac is telling Esau, you will be under your brother, but not forever.

There will come a day when you get restless, when you get strong, and you throw it off.

In plain words, at some point you get out from under his thumb.

## Two Brothers, Two Nations

So lay the two blessings side by side, because this is where the whole day has been going.

Jacob got the dew and the fat land, grain and wine, nations bowing, lordship over his brothers, and the promise of Abraham, God’s blessing on whoever blesses him.

Esau got a sword, a place under his brother, and a promise that one day he would break loose.

One blessing builds a nation that leads. The other builds a nation that fights and eventually tears itself free.

Nations. That is the word to hold. Because none of this was ever just about two old brothers in a tent.

Go back to what God told Rebekah when the boys were still inside her.

*“Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger.” (Genesis 25:23)* Two nations.

Two peoples. God was talking about descendants before the boys had names.

So let me give you the names.

Jacob is going to be renamed Israel, and his descendants are the Israelites, the people the rest of the Bible is about.

Esau is also called Edom, which means red, after the red stew he sold his birthright for, and his descendants are the Edomites.

Genesis 36 says it plainly, Esau is Edom.

Two brothers, two nations, living next to each other for centuries, and carrying this day’s words between them the whole time.

And you can watch Isaac’s words come true if you keep reading your Bible.

Hundreds of years later, King David conquered Edom and put soldiers in their land, and the Bible says *all they of Edom became David’s servants*.

Esau’s people serving Jacob’s people, just like the old man said.

Then, a few generations after that, under a king of Judah named Jehoram, Edom *revolted from under the hand of Judah, and made a king over themselves*.

The yoke came off the neck. That is the dominion Isaac was talking about.

It took centuries, but every word spoken in that tent played out between two nations.

That is what I want you to take from today.

The words a father speaks over his sons in a tent in Beersheba reach all the way down to armies and kings.

God said it to Rebekah before they were born. Isaac said it, twice, without meaning to.

And it came true across generations, for both brothers, the one who led and the one who broke free.

Remember what we said on Day 1. In the Bible, a son can mean a whole people.

When you read about Edom from now on, you will know.

That is Esau, still working out the blessing his father gave him through his tears.

Esau is still standing in the tent when we leave him today. He has his answer.

He has a sword and a future and a brother he has to serve. What he does with all of that is tomorrow.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 27

Start with a flashback.

Before you read today’s verses, turn back to Genesis 26:34–35, two short verses about who Esau married and how his parents felt about it.

We skipped them on Day 5 so we could read them here, where they matter most.

Then read Genesis 27:30–40. Notice how little time passes between Jacob leaving and Esau arriving.

Isaac speaks in verses 33, 35, 37, 39 and 40. Esau speaks in 31, 32, 34, 36 and 38. Keep track of who is talking.

It is easy to lose.

**As you read, notice:**

* 💬 The words Esau uses to identify himself in verse 32, and where you heard them yesterday
* 😨 What Isaac’s body does in verse 33 before he says anything
* 👣 The two things Esau says Jacob took in verse 36
* 🔗 The three parts of Esau’s future in verse 40

And think about this question: who pays for a lie after the liar has left the room?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Esau did exactly what his father asked and still came home to nothing. When have you done the right thing and lost anyway?** Write down what you did with the grief. Esau cried out loud. Did you?
2. **Isaac trembled when he realized his own son had used his blindness and his trust. Who trusts you in a way you could take advantage of?** A parent, a spouse, a child, someone who cannot check up on you. Name them, and name the line you will not cross.
3. **The words spoken in that tent played out between nations for centuries. What words have been spoken over your life that you are still living out?** Some were good and some were not. Write one of each, and ask God which ones He actually said.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You told Rebekah two nations were in her womb, and You were right down to the last king.

Nothing that happened in that tent surprised You. Not the lie, not the trembling, not the tears.

Your word stood through all of it.

Thank You that You let Esau cry. You did not cut his grief out of the story.

You wrote it down, loud and bitter, so that people like me who have done the right thing and still lost would know we are not the first.

When I lose something I cannot get back, let me bring the cry to You instead of burying it.

Keep me from ever using someone’s blindness, or their trust, or their love for me, to get what I want.

Let me be the son who comes home with the real thing, even if it costs me.

And remind me that Your words over my life are bigger than one bad day. You are speaking over generations.

Help me believe that what You have said about me will come true, with or without my scheming, and help me wait for it like Esau could not.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
