Twenty one days ago I asked you how many sons Abraham had, and I told you the answer was eight. Then we started walking.

Six boys sent east with gifts. Twelve princes in the desert. Twins fighting in the womb. A bowl of red stew.

Wells dug and stolen and dug again. A blind father and a goatskin on a smooth man’s arms. A ladder. Two sisters.

Speckled sheep. Stolen idols and a heap of stones. Four hundred men. A night at the river.

And yesterday, a nation called Edom.

Today we stop walking and climb a hill.

I want you to see the whole thing at once.

Every son, every grandson, every people that came from one old man who had no children at seventy five.

Because there is a promise behind all of it, and I think most of us have been reading that promise too small.

God did not tell Abraham he would be the father of a nation. He said nations. Many.

And when we count, we usually count one.

Today we count all of them.

# Count the Nations

> “As for me, behold, my covenant is with thee, and thou shalt be a father of many nations.” (Genesis 17:4)

Yesterday we left Esau’s family in the mountains of Seir with chiefs and kings.

Today we go all the way back, before Esau and Jacob were born, before Isaac was born, to a night when Abraham was ninety nine years old and still had only one son, by a servant girl.

God spoke to him, and here is what He said.

> “As for me, behold, my covenant is with thee, and thou shalt be a father of many nations. Neither shall thy name any more be called Abram, but thy name shall be Abraham; for a father of many nations have I made thee. And I will make thee exceeding fruitful, and I will make nations of thee, and kings shall come out of thee.” (Genesis 17:4–6)

Read the words that repeat. Many nations. Many nations. Nations.

Three times in three verses, and every time it is plural. God changed the man’s name to say it.

Abram meant exalted father. Abraham means father of a multitude.

God wrote the promise into what people called him every day for the rest of his life.

And remember what we learned on Day 1. In the Bible, father can mean ancestor.

When God speaks to Jacob at Bethel He says *I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father*, and Abraham was Jacob’s grandfather.

So when God says father of many nations, He means that whole peoples are going to trace their line back to this one man.

## God Did Not Say One Nation

Now here is what I have noticed, and it is the reason I wrote this book.

When Christians talk about God keeping this promise, we almost always count one nation. Israel. And we should.

Israel is the covenant people, the line that runs to David and to Jesus. God made that clear in the very same chapter.

*“But my covenant will I establish with Isaac.” (Genesis 17:21)* Nothing I say today moves that.

But God did not say one nation. He said many.

And if we only count Israel, we are giving God credit for a fraction of what He actually did.

So let’s count. Get your map.

Abraham had three women in his life and eight sons. Hagar gave him Ishmael. Sarah gave him Isaac.

Keturah gave him Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak and Shuah.

Isaac had two sons, Jacob and Esau, and Esau had a grandson named Amalek.

From those names come five peoples that your Bible keeps running into.

Let me walk you through each one, who they were, where they lived, and where you will meet them again.

## The Sons of Hagar

Start with Hagar. Her son was Ishmael, and God made him a promise in this same chapter.

*“Twelve princes shall he beget, and I will make him a great nation.” (Genesis 17:20)* On Day 2 we read those twelve names, *twelve princes according to their nations*, each with his own towns and camps.

The Ishmaelites lived in the desert country between Canaan and Egypt and out toward Arabia, *from Havilah unto Shur*.

They became the traders of the desert roads. When Joseph’s brothers sit down to eat with him in a pit, what do they see?

*A company of Ishmeelites came from Gilead with their camels bearing spicery and balm and myrrh, going to carry it down to Egypt.*

The same passage calls the traders Midianites too, and that is not a mistake.

These were cousin peoples working the same roads, so close that the book of Judges can call the Midianites Ishmaelites in the same breath.

Hundreds of years later, Ezekiel lists *the princes of Kedar*, one of Ishmael’s twelve, as merchants in lambs and rams and goats.

The caravans of the Old Testament are, by and large, the sons of Hagar.

## The Covenant Son

Now Sarah. Her son was Isaac, and Isaac is the covenant son.

From Isaac came the twins, and the younger twin became Israel at the river on Day 19.

His twelve sons became the twelve tribes.

The Israelites lived in Canaan, went down to Egypt as seventy people, and came out as a nation.

*The children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty.*

They got the land, the law, the kings, the prophets, and the Messiah.

This is the line God chose to carry the covenant, and the rest of the Bible is mostly their story.

Hold them at the center of your map, because that is where God put them.

But Isaac had another son. Esau.

And from him came the Edomites, the nation we studied yesterday, living in Mount Seir, southeast of the Dead Sea, in land God Himself gave them.

Where do you meet them again?

When Moses leads Israel out of the wilderness, he sends messengers to the king of Edom and calls Israel *thy brother*, and asks to pass through.

Edom answers, *Thou shalt not pass by me, lest I come out against thee with the sword.*

The brothers who hugged on the road became nations that did not. David put soldiers in Edom and ruled it.

A few generations later Edom broke away and crowned its own king. Jacob and Esau, still at it, centuries later.

Then from Esau, through Eliphaz and Timna, came Amalek.

The Amalekites lived in the southern wilderness, and they are the first enemy Israel fights after the Red Sea.

*Then came Amalek, and fought with Israel in Rephidim.*

God told Moses to write it down, that He would have war with Amalek from generation to generation.

King Saul’s throne was taken from him over the Amalekites.

Of all the sons of Abraham, this branch became the bitterest enemy of the covenant line.

## The Priest of Midian

And finally Keturah. Six sons, sent east with gifts on Day 1. The one who matters most is Midian.

The Midianites lived in the desert east and south of Canaan, down toward the Red Sea.

When Moses kills an Egyptian and runs, he runs to Midian, sits down by a well, and marries a daughter of the priest of Midian.

*Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses’ father in law* is the man who teaches Moses how to lead.

Then in Judges the Midianites come up with their camels *as grasshoppers for multitude* and strip Israel’s fields for seven years until God raises up Gideon.

And notice who is camped with them in that valley. *The Midianites and the Amalekites and all the children of the east.*

Two branches of Abraham’s family, camped together against a third.

The other sons of Keturah are quieter, but they are there. Jokshan’s sons Sheba and Dedan became trading peoples.

Shuah is most likely the father of the Shuhites, and Bildad the Shuhite sat in the ashes with Job.

Even Job’s friends may be family.

## Count Them With Me

So count them with me. Ishmaelites. Israelites. Edomites. Amalekites. Midianites.

Five peoples with names, from one man who had no son at seventy five.

Plus the traders of Sheba and Dedan, the Shuhites, the twelve settlements of Ishmael, the clans and kings of Edom.

When God said many nations, this is what He meant.

And every time one of those names shows up in your Bible, from Joseph’s pit to Gideon’s valley to Saul’s throne, you are watching Genesis 17 come true.

Not once. Over and over, for a thousand years.

And look at the last line of the promise. *Kings shall come out of thee.*

We found out yesterday that the first kings to come out of Abraham sat on thrones in Edom, before Israel ever had one.

God was keeping that word through Esau while Jacob’s family was still in Egypt.

## His Promises Are Not Fragile

Here is the thing I most want you to see. Being in the wider family did not mean being on Israel’s side.

The Ishmaelites carried Joseph to Egypt. Amalek attacked. Edom drew the sword. Midian raided the harvest.

Over and over, the sons of Abraham were the enemies of the sons of Abraham.

Family was a refuge, like Jethro, and family was a threat, like Amalek, sometimes in the same book.

And God kept His word to all of them anyway. He gave Ishmael twelve princes. He gave Esau a mountain.

He let Midian grow until it covered a valley. He did not take His promise back when the family turned on itself.

That tells you something about God. His promises are not fragile.

He said many nations to a childless old man, and He did not need every one of those nations to behave in order to keep it.

He kept it through the covenant son and through the sons who were sent away. Not the covenant, but still the blessing.

Still the family. Still counted.

And there is one more line in the promise to Abraham that we have not counted yet.

Back at the very beginning, in Genesis 12, God said *in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed*. All families.

Not five. All. That is the line that runs through Isaac and Jacob and Judah and David, all the way to Jesus.

And when Paul writes to the Galatians he says, *“And if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise.” (Galatians 3:29)* If you belong to Jesus, you are on this map too.

Not as an Israelite. Not as an Edomite. As a child of the promise, by faith.

The family we have spent twenty one days counting is still growing, and you are in it.

## The Story Is Not Over

So step back one more time and look at the whole road, from Keturah’s tent to Esau’s mountain.

This book is called The Sons of Abraham, and I hope you see now why I did not call it The Son of Abraham.

We watched six boys walk east with gifts and become peoples.

We watched a servant girl’s son die under a bush and get up and father twelve princes.

We watched twins fight in the womb and at a stew pot and in a tent and on a road, and we watched both of them become nations.

We watched Jacob leave with a stick and come home with two camps and a limp.

We watched a chapter full of names turn into a country.

And through all of it, under all of it, God was doing exactly what He said He would do on the night He renamed Abram.

Making nations.

Here is what I want you to take out of this book. When you measure God’s faithfulness, count everything.

Not just the part of the story you were taught to follow. Not just the branch you are on.

God’s promises are bigger than the one answer you were waiting for, and He is keeping them in places you are not looking.

He kept track of Zimran. He kept track of Amalek.

He will keep track of the parts of your family, and your life, that nobody else is counting.

And now I have to tell you what comes next, because the story is not over.

We stopped reading at Genesis 33:16, when Esau rode home, and I skipped ahead to chapter 36. That was on purpose.

Genesis 33:17 to 20, chapter 34 and chapter 35 are waiting for you in the next book, and they belong there, because they are not about Abraham’s sons anymore.

They are about Jacob’s.

The next book is called The Sons of Jacob, and it opens at Genesis 34 with the hardest chapter in this family’s story.

Jacob’s daughter Dinah, the girl missing from the list at the river, is taken by a prince named Shechem, and two of her brothers, Simeon and Levi, do something about it that Jacob never forgets.

Then chapter 35 takes Jacob back to Bethel, gives him a twelfth son, costs him Rachel, and buries Isaac with both of his boys standing at the grave, just like their father before them.

And then Genesis 37 to 50. Joseph and the coat. Judah and Tamar. The pit, the prison, the palace.

Brothers who sold a brother bowing to him in Egypt.

Twelve sons who become twelve tribes, and the whole family going down into Egypt so that God can bring them out.

The sons of Abraham became nations. The sons of Jacob are about to become Israel.

I hope you will walk that road with me too.

For now, finish this one well. You have done twenty one days.

Take the reflection questions, do today’s action, and end your day, and this study, in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 17

Only three verses today, from long before this book began. Read them twice. Count how many times God says nations.

Then take ten minutes and skim back over Genesis 25 to 36 with your family map open beside you. Do not reread it all.

Just let your eye run down the chapters and notice how many names on your map you now recognize.

Keep the map out. We are going to fill in the last of it today.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🌍 The word God uses in verse 4 and again in verse 5
* 🌱 What God says He will make in verse 6
* 👑 Who is promised to come out of Abraham at the end of verse 6
* 🗺️ How many of the peoples on your map you can name from memory

And think about this question: when you say God kept His promise to Abraham, who are you counting?

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You told a ninety nine year old man with one son that he would be the father of many nations, and You did it.

Ishmael and Isaac and Keturah’s six. Jacob and Esau. Israel and Edom and Amalek and Midian.

You kept every word, to every branch, even the ones that turned against each other.

Forgive me for counting small.

For measuring Your faithfulness by the one answer I was waiting for and missing everything else You were doing.

Open my eyes to the nations.

Teach me to count everything You have done, not just the part I was watching.

Thank You that Your promises are not fragile.

You did not need Ishmael to be good, or Esau, or Midian, to keep what You said to Abraham.

You do not need my family to be perfect to keep what You said to me.

And thank You that the blessing kept going.

Through Isaac, through Jacob, through Judah, all the way to Jesus, all the way to me. I am Abraham’s seed by faith.

Put my name on the map, and keep the family growing through me to whoever comes next.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
