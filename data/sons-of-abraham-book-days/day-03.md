Yesterday we counted Ishmael’s twelve princes and watched God keep a promise He made to a servant girl in the desert.

Today the family map turns back to the covenant line. Back to Isaac.

And the first thing we learn about Isaac’s marriage is that it looked a lot like his parents’ marriage.

A beautiful wife, chosen by God, brought from far away. And no baby.

Twenty years of no baby.

Then, when the baby finally comes, it is two babies, and they are fighting before they are born.

Their mother is in so much pain she goes to God and asks Him what is going on, and God tells her something no mother wants to hear.

Your sons are going to be two nations, and they are going to be at odds.

By the end of today’s reading the boys are grown men, their parents have picked favorites, and the older brother has traded the biggest inheritance in the world for a bowl of stew.

All of that in sixteen verses.

This is where the trouble in Abraham’s family starts. Let’s go find out why.

# A Bowl of Stew

> “Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels.” (Genesis 25:23)

The map unrolls again, and this time it is the branch we have been waiting for.

> “And these are the generations of Isaac, Abraham’s son: Abraham begat Isaac: And Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah to wife, the daughter of Bethuel the Syrian of Padanaram, the sister to Laban the Syrian.” (Genesis 25:19–20)

Same words as yesterday. *These are the generations of*.

But where Ishmael got seven verses, Isaac’s family is going to get the rest of this book.

So let me make sure you know who everybody is, because this one verse packs in a lot of relatives.

Abraham had a brother named Nahor who stayed behind up north when Abraham walked away.

Nahor married Milcah and they had a son named Bethuel, Abraham’s nephew. Bethuel had two children we care about.

A daughter, Rebekah, and a son, Laban. So Rebekah is Abraham’s brother’s granddaughter. Family.

That was the whole point.

In The Obedience of Abraham, Abraham sent his oldest servant north to find Isaac a wife from his own people, and the servant came home with Rebekah, the girl at the well who watered ten camels for a stranger and then said, *I will go*.

And Laban, her brother, is the man who counted the gold on her wrists. Remember him. He matters when Jacob is grown.

Two more words.

Syrian here means Aramean, a person from the land of Aram, the country up north around the top of the big rivers.

And Padanaram is the region around Haran, where Abraham’s father stopped and died. Not Ur. Haran.

That is where the family stayed when Abraham moved on.

## Isaac Intreated the LORD

> “And Isaac intreated the LORD for his wife, because she was barren: and the LORD was intreated of him, and Rebekah his wife conceived.” (Genesis 25:21)

Read that fast and it sounds like a wedding, a prayer and a baby in the same week. It was not.

Intreated means begged. Pleaded. Isaac did not say a quick prayer and move on.

He went to God and asked, hard, for his wife. Barren means she could not conceive.

And *the LORD was intreated of him* does not mean God prayed to Isaac.

It means God let Himself be moved by Isaac’s pleading. He heard it, and He answered.

Now do the math I asked you to do. Verse 20 says Isaac was forty when he married.

Verse 26 is going to say he was threescore, a score is twenty, so sixty, when the twins were born. Forty to sixty.

Twenty years. Twenty years of a tent with no baby in it.

Feel that. Isaac’s parents waited twenty five years for him. Sarah was ninety when he was born.

Isaac grew up as the miracle baby of the whole camp, the boy the entire promise rested on.

God had said to Abraham, *in Isaac shall thy seed be called*.

So Isaac marries Rebekah, the wife God picked, and then nothing happens. One year. Five years. Ten. Fifteen.

The promise of a people like the stars of heaven is sitting in a tent with a childless couple in their fifties.

And why did it matter so much?

In that world, children were your heirs, the hands that would run the herds when you got old, the reason the household kept going after you were gone.

For Isaac it was heavier still. The promise ran through him. No child, no promise.

Here is what I want you to see. The same test came back around.

Abraham and Sarah faced a barren wife and a long wait, and they took a shortcut named Hagar.

Now Isaac faces the exact same test, and there is no Hagar in this tent. Isaac prays.

I think Isaac carried something the rest of us can only imagine. He had been on the mountain.

He had been tied to the wood and watched his father lift a knife, and he had heard the voice stop it.

He knew in his bones that God provides. So when the test came to his own tent, he did not go find a servant girl.

He intreated the LORD.

That does not make prayer a formula that hands everyone a baby.

It means this generation had to learn to trust God just like the last one did.

Isaac waited twenty years and kept asking. God answered.

## Two Nations Are in Thy Womb

> “And the children struggled together within her; and she said, If it be so, why am I thus? And she went to enquire of the LORD.” (Genesis 25:22)

And then the answer to twenty years of prayer hurts. *The children struggled together*. Not kicked. Struggled. Fought.

Rebekah can feel two babies wrestling inside her, and it is bad enough that she says, *If it be so, why am I thus?*

I hear it as, if this is really Your doing, God, if this baby is Your answer, then why is it like this?

That is an honest question from a woman in pain, and I love what she does with it. She does not complain to Isaac.

She goes and asks God directly.

> “And the LORD said unto her, Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger.” (Genesis 25:23)

God answers her, and He answers big. She asked why it hurts. He tells her the next thousand years.

*Two nations are in thy womb*. Not two boys. Two nations. Bowels here just means her insides, her womb.

Two different peoples are going to come out of this one pregnancy and go two different directions.

And we know who they are.

The younger twin is Jacob, whose name God later changes to Israel, and his children are the Israelites.

The older twin is Esau, who is also called Edom, and his children are the Edomites, a people south and east of Israel who show up all through the rest of the Bible, usually not as friends.

Then the line that turns the whole family upside down. *The elder shall serve the younger*.

In that world the firstborn came first in everything.

He led the family, he got the biggest share, he stood where his father stood.

God is telling Rebekah that the normal order is reversed in this house. The older will end up under the younger.

And that happened in two sizes. In the family, Jacob ends up with the birthright and the blessing.

In history, Israel ends up the stronger nation, and centuries later King David conquers Edom and the Bible says the Edomites became his servants.

Hold on to one thing about this verse. God said it before either boy had done a single thing.

Before the stew, before the stolen blessing, before any of it. The choice was God’s, made in the womb.

That matters later, because Jacob is going to do some ugly things to get what God already said was his, and those tricks are not righteous just because they moved toward the result God announced.

## The Heel Grabber

> “And when her days to be delivered were fulfilled, behold, there were twins in her womb. And the first came out red, all over like an hairy garment; and they called his name Esau. And after that came his brother out, and his hand took hold on Esau’s heel; and his name was called Jacob: and Isaac was threescore years old when she bare them.” (Genesis 25:24–26)

Picture the birth.

The first boy comes out and everybody stares, because he is red and covered in hair, like somebody wrapped a newborn in a shaggy coat.

So they name him Esau, a name tied in the story to that hairy look. Remember the hair.

In Genesis 27 the difference between a hairy brother and a smooth one is going to decide who gets a blessing.

Then the second boy comes out, and his tiny hand is wrapped around his brother’s heel.

So they name him Jacob, which plays on the Hebrew word for heel. The heel grabber.

Later in the story that name picks up a second meaning, someone who trips you up and takes your place, and Esau is going to spit that meaning out with tears in his eyes.

But do not put that on the baby. Right now he is a newborn holding on to his brother’s foot.

## Each Parent Picked a Son

> “And the boys grew: and Esau was a cunning hunter, a man of the field; and Jacob was a plain man, dwelling in tents. And Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob.” (Genesis 25:27–28)

Twenty years of waiting took one verse. Now the boys growing up takes one verse.

Slow down and let the two pictures form.

Esau is a cunning hunter. Cunning in old English means skilled, not sneaky.

He is good with a bow, good at reading the ground, the kind of man who comes back with meat.

A *man of the field* means a man of the open country, the hills and scrub and dry valleys outside the camp.

Picture him gone for days, sunburned, with a carcass over his shoulder.

Jacob is a plain man. That word has nothing to do with his looks or his brains.

The Hebrew word is tam, and it means quiet, steady, even tempered. Settled.

*Dwelling in tents* puts him at the camp, among the herds and the servants and the household, the man who keeps things running while his brother is out hunting.

Two very different sons from one womb, just like God said.

And then the verse I think Rebekah and Isaac would both wish was not in the Bible.

*Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob*.

Venison here means any hunted meat, not just deer. Isaac liked what Esau brought home. Rebekah loved the boy at home.

So it was already favorites. Each parent picked a son.

This does not mean Isaac had no love for Jacob or Rebekah none for Esau.

But a house with two sons and two camps is a house that is going to crack.

A father leaning toward one boy and a mother leaning toward the other will end up on opposite sides of something, and in Genesis 27 they do.

The scheme in that chapter did not come out of nowhere. It grew in a tent where everybody already knew who loved whom.

## That Same Red Pottage

> “And Jacob sod pottage: and Esau came from the field, and he was faint: And Esau said to Jacob, Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage; for I am faint: therefore was his name called Edom.” (Genesis 25:29–30)

Now the scene. Jacob is at the camp with a pot on the fire. Sod is not sowed.

It is an old past tense of seethe, so it means boiled. Pottage is stew.

Jacob is cooking a pot of lentil stew, and it is red.

Esau comes in from the open country and he is faint, worn out, famished, shaking with hunger.

And he says, feed me that red stuff. The King James gives you his words. *That same red pottage*.

He does not even call it by name. He is pointing at the pot.

And right there the Bible tells you where a second name came from. Edom means red.

Red stew, a red and hairy baby, and a nickname that stuck. Esau does not stop being Esau.

People go on calling him Esau for the rest of Genesis.

But Genesis 36 opens with *Esau, who is Edom*, and his descendants are the Edomites, the nation of Red.

One of the two nations God told Rebekah about just got its name from a bowl of lentils.

## Sell Me This Day Thy Birthright

> “And Jacob said, Sell me this day thy birthright.” (Genesis 25:31)

Look at Jacob. His exhausted brother asks for food, and the quiet one at the camp sees an opening and names a price.

And the price is not small.

The birthright was the set of rights that belonged to the firstborn son. First place in the family.

Leadership of the household when the father died. And the biggest share of everything.

Later, in the law God gave Israel, it was written down plainly: the firstborn gets a double portion.

Picture it with two sons. Split the estate into three parts, and the firstborn takes two.

That law came later, but it shows the kind of privilege a firstborn had. Now remember whose estate this is.

Isaac got *all that he had* from Abraham. The herds, the silver, the servants, the field with the cave.

This is one of the richest households in the land, and Esau is standing in line for the double share of it.

That is what Jacob is asking for in exchange for lunch.

One careful line here, because it matters later.

The birthright is not the same as the blessing Isaac gives in Genesis 27, and it is not the same as God’s covenant choice.

Jacob cannot buy God’s promise with a bowl of stew. God already made that choice in the womb.

What Jacob is buying is the family position, the firstborn’s place and share.

Esau himself keeps the two things separate later when he cries that Jacob took his birthright and now has taken his blessing too.

Two different things, both lost.

> “And Esau said, Behold, I am at the point to die: and what profit shall this birthright do to me?” (Genesis 25:32)

Was he really about to die? *Faint* does not mean dying. I think it was desperate exhaustion with some drama on top.

But do not wave off the hunger. He felt like he was dying, and when you feel like that, your judgment goes.

Listen to his reasoning. *What profit shall this birthright do to me?* What good is that to me right now?

That is the sentence the whole day turns on.

He is weighing a future worth more than he can count against a bowl in front of him, and the bowl wins.

> “And Jacob said, Swear to me this day; and he sware unto him: and he sold his birthright unto Jacob.” (Genesis 25:33)

Jacob wants it locked in. Swear means take a solemn oath, a binding promise spoken out loud before God. Not cursing.

Think of it as signing a contract with your voice. Jacob is not going to feed his brother on a casual yeah, whatever.

Esau swears, and the sale is done before the first spoonful.

Look at both of them honestly.

Jacob sees his brother at his weakest and makes feeding him conditional on the biggest concession of his life.

That is sharp dealing, and the fact that God had already chosen Jacob does not make it kind.

And Esau swears away his place in the family with his eyes open, for one meal. Nobody in this scene comes out clean.

## Thus Esau Despised His Birthright

> “Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way: thus Esau despised his birthright.” (Genesis 25:34)

Count the verbs. Ate. Drank. Rose up. Went his way. Four quick movements and he is gone.

The Bible makes the meal as fast and ordinary as it felt to Esau.

Bread, a bowl of lentils, which are small flat seeds cooked soft into a thick stew, and out the door.

And then the narrator stops and gives his verdict on the whole thing. *Thus Esau despised his birthright*.

I want to slow all the way down on that word, because the first time I read it I heard it wrong.

I heard despised the way we use it now, as hate.

As if Esau walked away hating the trade, angry that Jacob now had what was his. That is not what this verse is saying.

The Hebrew word is bazah, and it means to treat something as worth little. To regard it as insignificant.

Despised here is not about his feelings afterward. It is about how he treated the birthright in the moment he traded it.

He handled it as if it did not matter.

If you could have asked him at the pot, his answer would have been something like, that thing?

What is it going to do for me? It does not matter.

That is contempt, not because he hated it, but because he treated something precious as expendable the second his stomach got loud.

He handled it recklessly.

And here is why that is wrong, not just unlucky. The birthright was not Esau’s to burn.

It was a place in Abraham’s family that reached forward to his own children and grandchildren.

Everything his grandfather walked away from Ur to receive, everything his father carried down off the mountain, was sitting in that firstborn’s share.

He traded it for relief. Not even for something big.

For a meal he would have gotten anyway in an hour if he had walked into any other tent in the camp.

That is the one thing I want you to carry out of today. Appetite has a way of shrinking the future.

When you are hungry enough, or lonely enough, or tired enough, the thing in front of you looks huge and the thing that lasts looks like nothing.

*What profit shall this do to me?* Esau said it about his inheritance.

People say it about their marriage, their integrity, their savings, their calling, their walk with God, right before they trade it for something red in a bowl.

The stew is always gone in an hour. The birthright is gone for good.

Yesterday God kept His word to Ishmael out in the desert.

Today, inside the covenant family, the firstborn treats the inheritance like nothing.

And God’s word to Rebekah still stands. God is not thrown off by any of it.

But the family is going to pay for this scene for a long time.

Tomorrow a famine hits the land, Isaac moves his whole household to a town called Gerar, and God speaks to him directly for the first time.

Then Isaac makes a choice you have seen before, because his father made it twice.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 25

Do the math as you go. Verse 20 gives you Isaac’s age when he married.

Verse 26 gives you his age when the twins were born. Subtract.

Watch what God says to Rebekah in verse 23 before either boy has taken a breath.

Everything after it happens in the shadow of that sentence.

The last six verses move fast. Count the verbs in verse 34. That speed is the point.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🙏 What Isaac does about his wife being barren in verse 21
* 👶 How each twin looks and what each one is named
* ❤️ Which parent loves which son, and why
* 🍲 The one word the Bible uses to sum up Esau in verse 34

And think about this question: what would you trade for relief if you were hungry enough?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Isaac faced the same test as his father, a barren wife and a long wait, and he prayed instead of taking a shortcut. Where are you tempted to build your own Hagar instead of waiting on God?** Name the shortcut you have been eyeing. Then name what twenty years of asking would look like instead.
2. **Isaac loved Esau and Rebekah loved Jacob, and the house was already divided. Is there a line like that in your family or your home?** Favorites are rarely announced. They are felt. Ask God to show you where someone is feeling it.
3. **Esau asked, what good is this birthright going to do for me? What lasting thing in your life have you started talking about that way?** Be specific. The stew is always something real. So is what it costs.

## Today's One Thing

**Name Your Stew**

Esau did not lose his birthright to an enemy. He traded it for relief. Find your trade before you make it.

1. Write down one thing of lasting value that God has given you. Your marriage, your kids, your health, your reputation, your calling, your walk with Him. Pick one.
2. Next to it, write the stew. The quick relief that tempts you to treat it as nothing when you are tired, hungry, lonely or angry. Be honest. Nobody else reads this page.
3. Do one concrete thing today that puts distance between you and the pot. Delete the app. Make the call. Move the money. Tell one person.

Then add Rebekah, Esau and Jacob to your family map, with Edom written next to Esau. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer
