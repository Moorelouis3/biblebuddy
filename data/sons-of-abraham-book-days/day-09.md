Yesterday we left Esau standing in his father’s tent with tears on his face and a second blessing in his ears. A sword.

A life under his brother. A promise that one day he would break loose.

We also looked ahead and saw where it all went, two nations, Israel and Edom, living out those words for centuries.

Today we come back to the tent, because Esau has not finished reacting. The tears dry.

Something harder takes their place.

And once again, when the trouble comes, watch who steps in to handle it. Rebekah.

The woman who built the scheme on Day 6 and said, let the curse fall on me.

She is about to find out that the curse has come, and it is not falling on her. It is falling on the son she did it for.

So she makes another plan.

That is really what today is about.

Three people in this family have a serious problem, and every one of them reaches for a plan instead of reaching for God.

Rebekah arranges. Isaac sends. Esau, at the very end, does the strangest thing of all.

He tries to fix his parents’ grief by adding a third wife.

By the time we are done, Jacob is on the road alone, walking away from everything he just schemed to get. Let’s go.

# One More Plan

> “And give thee the blessing of Abraham, to thee, and to thy seed with thee.” (Genesis 28:4)

We left Esau weeping in his father’s tent. Here is what the weeping turned into.

> “And Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing wherewith his father blessed him: and Esau said in his heart, The days of mourning for my father are at hand; then will I slay my brother Jacob.” (Genesis 27:41)

The word hated here is a word for holding a grudge, carrying hostility around with you and feeding it.

It is not a flash of temper. It is the kind of anger you lie awake with. And Esau has fed it until it has a shape.

*Then will I slay my brother Jacob.* Slay means kill. He is not thinking about a fight. He is planning a killing.

Notice the timing he has picked. *The days of mourning for my father are at hand.*

Esau thinks Isaac is going to die soon.

Everybody in the family thinks that, which is why the whole blessing business happened in the first place.

So Esau decides, I will wait. I will not do it while the old man is alive.

I will bury my father, and then I will kill my brother. That is cold. That is a man who has thought it through.

The Bible says he *said in his heart*. It was private. But somehow it got out.

## Purposing to Kill Thee

> “And these words of Esau her elder son were told to Rebekah: and she sent and called Jacob her younger son, and said unto him, Behold, thy brother Esau, as touching thee, doth comfort himself, purposing to kill thee.” (Genesis 27:42)

Who told her? The Bible does not say.

In a camp of hundreds of people, a man like Esau does not have to say something very loud for it to travel.

What matters is that Rebekah hears it, and she believes it.

*As touching thee* just means concerning you. *Purposing* means planning.

And then this phrase: Esau *doth comfort himself* with the idea of killing you. That is the unsettling part.

The thought of Jacob dead is what makes Esau feel better. It is his consolation.

When a man starts comforting himself with revenge, you take it seriously, and Rebekah does.

Now stop with me for a second, because I cannot read this verse without hearing Day 6.

Do you remember what Rebekah said when Jacob was afraid of getting caught? *Upon me be thy curse, my son.*

Let it fall on me. Well, here is the curse. A brother who wants Jacob dead. And it is not falling on Rebekah.

It is falling on the son she said she would cover. She could not catch it. Nobody can.

And I want to ask the question that I think the Bible is inviting us to ask.

Where is the scene where Rebekah goes to Esau and says, it was my idea, I pushed him into it, be angry with me?

We do not get that scene. Maybe she said something we are not shown.

But what we are shown is her doing what she did last time. She calls Jacob, and she makes a plan.

## Flee Thou to Laban

> “Now therefore, my son, obey my voice; and arise, flee thou to Laban my brother to Haran; And tarry with him a few days, until thy brother’s fury turn away;” (Genesis 27:43–44)

*Obey my voice.* The exact words from Day 6. Twice she said it then, to send him into the tent with the goat skins.

Now she says it a third time, to send him out of the country because of what happened in the tent.

The same voice that got him into this is now getting him out of it. I am not saying she is wrong to get him away.

Esau means it. Running from a man who plans to kill you is not a sin. But notice, once again, what is missing.

There is no prayer in this scene. No, let us ask the God who told me about these boys before they were born.

There is a plan, and there is a mother managing it.

Flee means exactly what it sounds like. Run. Get out, now, to save your life. And where? *To Laban my brother to Haran.*

Let me reintroduce Laban, because it has been a long time.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, you remember the day Abraham’s servant arrived at a well in the north, and a girl named Rebekah watered his camels, and her brother Laban came running out when he saw the gold.

Laban is Rebekah’s brother, Jacob’s uncle. And Haran is not a person here.

It is the city in the far north, up in what is now southeastern Turkey, where Abraham’s family settled when they left Ur.

Here is what I want you to grasp about the time. Rebekah has not seen that place in nearly a century.

She left it when Isaac was forty. The twins came twenty years later, and the twins are now about seventy seven.

It is close to a hundred years since a young woman climbed on a camel and rode south to marry a man she had never met.

Rebekah is an old woman sending a grown son to a brother she last saw as a young man.

And look at how she describes the trip. *Tarry with him a few days.* Tarry means stay.

Haran is around five hundred miles from Beersheba.

That is weeks of walking, sleeping on the ground, finding water, watching for robbers.

And at the other end there is a brother whose fury has to cool.

This is not a few days, and I think part of Rebekah knows it.

Either she is playing it down so Jacob will go, or she honestly thinks Esau will get over it fast.

What actually happens is that Jacob stays with Laban twenty years.

## What You Did to Him

> “Until thy brother’s anger turn away from thee, and he forget that which thou hast done to him: then I will send, and fetch thee from thence: why should I be deprived also of you both in one day?” (Genesis 27:45)

Read that middle part again. *That which thou hast done to him.* What YOU did to him. Hold on. Whose plan was this?

Who cooked the goat, who pulled out Esau’s clothes, who tied the skins on?

Rebekah talks about this as something Jacob did, and her own part just quietly disappears from the sentence.

I am not going to claim I know everything in her heart. But I know what she said, and what she said leaves herself out.

Then the mother comes through for a moment. *Why should I be deprived also of you both in one day?*

Deprived means bereaved, robbed of her children. And *both* means Jacob and Esau.

If Esau kills Jacob, she loses Jacob to the grave, and then Esau too, because a man who kills his brother does not get to stay home and carry on.

One terrible day, both sons gone. That is the fear underneath all her managing. And she makes a promise.

*Then I will send, and fetch thee from thence.*

Keep that promise in mind as we go on, and see whether the Bible ever shows us that message arriving.

## Two Conversations, Two Reasons, One Trip

> “And Rebekah said to Isaac, I am weary of my life because of the daughters of Heth: if Jacob take a wife of the daughters of Heth, such as these which are of the daughters of the land, what good shall my life do me?” (Genesis 27:46)

Now watch what she does, because it is clever. She has already told Jacob to run for his life.

Now she goes to Isaac, and she does not mention Esau’s threat at all. She talks about marriage.

*The daughters of Heth* are the Hittite women, the people Esau married into.

Remember from yesterday, those marriages were a grief of mind to both parents for nearly forty years.

So Rebekah is not making this up. *I am weary of my life* means I am sick to death of this. And then the hook.

If Jacob does the same thing, what is the point of my living?

Do you see it? Two conversations, two reasons, one trip. To Jacob she says, run, your brother wants you dead.

To Isaac she says, send him north, he needs a proper wife. Both are true.

But the second one gets Isaac to send Jacob away himself, with his blessing, without Rebekah ever having to say, by the way, I set up the thing that made Esau want to kill him.

The Bible does not tell us whether Isaac ever learned about the threat, or about her part.

It just shows us a woman who is very good at getting the outcome she wants.

## The Blessing of Abraham

> “And Isaac called Jacob, and blessed him, and charged him, and said unto him, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan. Arise, go to Padanaram, to the house of Bethuel thy mother’s father; and take thee a wife from thence of the daughters of Laban thy mother’s brother.” (Genesis 28:1–2)

It works. Isaac calls Jacob in, and this time there is no disguise. He knows exactly which son this is.

He charges him, and he blesses him. The charge is about marriage. Not *the daughters of Canaan*.

Rebekah said Heth, Isaac says Canaan, and that is not a contradiction.

Genesis 10 lists Heth as one of the sons of Canaan, so the Hittites were one of the Canaanite peoples.

Go north, to Padanaram, the region around Haran, to Bethuel’s house, and marry one of Laban’s daughters.

Find a wife in the family that knows this God. Exactly what Abraham did for Isaac, Isaac now does for Jacob.

And then the blessing. Listen to it, because it is not the same one as Day 7.

> “And God Almighty bless thee, and make thee fruitful, and multiply thee, that thou mayest be a multitude of people; And give thee the blessing of Abraham, to thee, and to thy seed with thee; that thou mayest inherit the land wherein thou art a stranger, which God gave unto Abraham.” (Genesis 28:3–4)

Your key verse today. *Give thee the blessing of Abraham.* Isaac says it out loud.

On Day 7 he prayed dew and grain and lordship over a son he thought was Esau, with words that echoed Abraham.

Now he looks at Jacob, knowing it is Jacob, and names it. Many descendants, a multitude of people, and the land.

The whole covenant, handed on by name, in the open.

So is this a second blessing? Yes. There was never a rule that a father gets one blessing per son and then runs out.

Count them. Jacob got the leading blessing in Esau’s clothes. Esau got the sword and the yoke.

And now Jacob gets this, the covenant blessing, as himself.

I like to call it his rightful blessing, not because the first one was fake, but because this one he received with his own face.

Nobody was tricked. That is how it should have happened in the first place.

And remember, Isaac does not create this promise by saying it.

God made it to Abraham, God confirmed it to Isaac, and tomorrow God is going to speak it to Jacob directly.

The father is passing on what God already said.

> “And Isaac sent away Jacob: and he went to Padanaram unto Laban, son of Bethuel the Syrian, the brother of Rebekah, Jacob’s and Esau’s mother.” (Genesis 28:5)

And he goes. One man, on foot, with a staff.

Years later Jacob will look back on this day and say, *with my staff I passed over this Jordan*.

No camels, no servants, no herds.

The son who schemed his way into the headship of a great household leaves it with what he can carry.

## Then Went Esau Unto Ishmael

Now the Bible turns one more time to Esau, and this is the part I want to end on.

> “When Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob, and sent him away to Padanaram, to take him a wife from thence; and that as he blessed him he gave him a charge, saying, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan; And that Jacob obeyed his father and his mother, and was gone to Padanaram; And Esau seeing that the daughters of Canaan pleased not Isaac his father; Then went Esau unto Ishmael, and took unto the wives which he had Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael Abraham’s son, the sister of Nebajoth, to be his wife.” (Genesis 28:6–9)

Esau has been watching.

He sees Jacob blessed and sent to find a wife in the family, and he hears the charge, not a Canaanite woman.

And it dawns on him. *The daughters of Canaan pleased not Isaac his father.* My wives are the problem.

So he does something about it. He goes to Ishmael’s family. Let me connect the names, because this is our book.

Ishmael is Abraham’s son by Hagar, which makes him Isaac’s half brother and Esau’s uncle.

On Day 2 we met his twelve sons, and the first one was Nebajoth.

Mahalath is Nebajoth’s sister, Ishmael’s daughter, Esau’s cousin.

By the common timeline Ishmael himself has already died by now, so going *unto Ishmael* means going to Ishmael’s people.

Esau marries into the other branch of Abraham’s family.

The family that was split in the desert on the day Hagar was sent away comes back together at a wedding.

And here is the line I keep reading. He *took unto the wives which he had* Mahalath. Unto the wives he had.

He did not send Judith and Bashemath away. He added a third.

Esau looked at a problem his parents had carried for nearly forty years and said, I know, I will add a wife from the right family.

That will fix it. The Bible does not tell us what Isaac and Rebekah thought about it. It does not need to.

You can feel it.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying Esau should have thrown out his first two wives.

The point is smaller and sharper than that. Esau treated a deep problem like a math problem.

Something is wrong, so add something. And adding something does not fix what is already there.

## Something to Stop

Now step back and look at the whole day, because everybody in it is doing a version of the same thing.

Rebekah knew what God had said. *The elder shall serve the younger.*

And when the moment came, she acted like God’s promise needed her goat skins to come true.

Then, when the goat skins blew up in her face, she did not go back to God. She made another plan. Run to Laban.

Talk to Isaac about wives. Manage it.

She trusts her own hands more than she trusts what God already said, and we never see her put her hands down.

Esau has a sore spot in the family that has been there for decades, and his answer is one more wedding.

And in the middle of all these plans, nobody has asked God anything.

That changes tomorrow, on the road, when Jacob lies down with a stone for a pillow and God finally speaks.

But notice how long the family went without asking.

So here is the question I am leaving with you. When something in your life is broken, what is your first move?

Most of us do what Rebekah and Esau did. We add. A new plan, a new purchase, a new promise, another layer on top.

And very often the real answer is not something to add. It is something to stop. A habit to put down.

A shortcut to give up. An excuse to stop making. A situation we keep managing that we need to hand to God.

Rebekah needed to stop arranging.

Esau needed to deal with what was already in his tent before he brought anything else into it.

Ask yourself tonight, honestly. Am I adding another plan so I do not have to face the thing I actually need to stop?

Then take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

Tomorrow Jacob is alone in the dark on the road to Haran, and God finally speaks.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 27-28

Today crosses a chapter break. Keep reading straight from the end of 27 into the start of 28 and stop at verse 9.

The dream Jacob has on the road is tomorrow.

Rebekah has two conversations today, one with Jacob and one with Isaac.

Notice that she gives each of them a different reason for the same trip.

Isaac blesses Jacob again in 28:3 and 4. Compare it with what he said on Day 7.

This time he knows exactly who is standing in front of him.

**As you read, notice:**

* ⏳ What Esau is waiting for in verse 41 before he acts
* 👂 The words obey my voice in verse 43, and where you heard them on Day 6
* 👉 Whose fault Rebekah says the whole thing is in verse 45
* 💍 Whose family Esau marries into in 28:9

And think about this question: when something is broken, is your first move to add something or to stop something?

## Reflection Questions

1. **Rebekah knew God’s promise and still acted as if the future depended on her. Where are you managing an outcome God has already spoken about?** Name it. Then ask whether your hands are helping or just in the way.
2. **What thou hast done to him. Rebekah told the story with her own part left out. Where are you telling a story that leaves out what you did?** Try telling it again, out loud, with your part in it. Notice how different it sounds.
3. **Esau tried to fix a forty year problem by adding a wife. What have you added to your life recently to fix something that actually needed to be removed?** A purchase, a plan, a promise, a person. Write down what the real fix would have been.

## Today's One Thing

**Subtract One Thing**

Everybody in today’s reading added a plan. You are going to take one away.

1. Write down one problem you keep trying to fix by adding something. Another plan, another purchase, another promise to do better.
2. Under it, write the thing you actually need to stop. A habit, a shortcut, an excuse, a situation you keep managing instead of handing to God.
3. Stop it today. Not next week. Delete it, cancel it, put it down, or say the words. Then tell one person what you stopped, so it stays stopped.

One problem named, one thing stopped, one person told. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You had already spoken over this family before anyone in it made a plan. The elder would serve the younger.

The blessing of Abraham would go to Jacob.

You did not need Rebekah’s management or Esau’s third wedding, and You do not need mine.

Forgive me for the plans I make instead of praying.

For the way I reach for one more thing to add when You are asking me to put something down.

For telling my story with my own part left out.

Show me the one thing I need to stop, and give me the courage to stop it today.

When the consequences of my own choices come back around, keep me from scrambling to manage them.

Let me come to You first, before I call anyone else or arrange anything else.

And thank You that You met Jacob on the road after all of this.

He walked out with nothing but a staff and a stolen blessing, and You were waiting for him.

Be waiting for me too, Lord, at the end of every plan that did not work.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
