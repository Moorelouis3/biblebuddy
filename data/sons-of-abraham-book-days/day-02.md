Yesterday we watched six sons ride east. Abraham handed each of them a gift, pointed toward the desert, and let them go.

Isaac kept the tents, the herds and the promise.

But there was one more son, and he had been gone a lot longer than the other six.

Picture him the last time we really saw him. A teenage boy lying under a desert shrub with no water left in the skin.

His mother sitting a bowshot away so she would not have to watch him die.

If you walked with us through The Obedience of Abraham, that scene is still in your chest. And then a voice from heaven.

God heard the boy. A well. A drink.

And a promise, said straight to a servant girl in the dirt: I will make him a great nation.

That was Ishmael. Hagar’s boy. Abraham’s first son.

Today the Bible goes back and checks on him. Not with a story this time. With a list.

Twelve names, a region, and a number of years. It looks like the kind of paragraph you skim. Do not.

This is God showing you a receipt. He made a promise in the desert, and here is what it turned into.

Let’s count the princes.

# A Promise Kept

> “These are the sons of Ishmael, and these are their names, by their towns, and by their castles; twelve princes according to their nations.” (Genesis 25:16)

Here is how the Bible brings Ishmael back into the story.

> “Now these are the generations of Ishmael, Abraham’s son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah’s handmaid, bare unto Abraham.” (Genesis 25:12)

Yesterday I told you a genealogy is a family map.

*These are the generations of* is the Bible’s way of unrolling a new piece of that map.

You will see the same words again tomorrow for Isaac. And look at how carefully this one verse tells you who Ishmael is.

*Abraham’s son*. Not a servant, not a stranger. A son. Then *Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah’s handmaid*.

A handmaid is a female servant, a woman who belonged to the household and worked for the lady of the tent.

The Bible wants you to remember that Ishmael’s mother was Sarah’s Egyptian servant girl, because the whole weight of today comes from that.

## God Hears

So let me put the story back together for you in a few lines, in case you did not read The Obedience of Abraham.

Sarah could not have children. She gave her servant Hagar to Abraham so that Hagar could have a baby for her.

It worked, and it wrecked the house.

Hagar ran away pregnant, and God met her by a spring on the way to a place called Shur and told her to go back.

The boy was born and named Ishmael, which means God hears.

Thirteen years later Isaac came, and when Isaac was a little boy Sarah had Hagar and Ishmael sent away.

They walked into the wilderness with a skin of water. The water ran out. The boy collapsed under a bush.

And God heard him, opened Hagar’s eyes to a well, and said, *I will make him a great nation*.

Genesis 21 ends with Ishmael grown up in the wilderness of Paran, an archer, married to an Egyptian woman his mother picked for him.

Then, years later, one more glimpse. Genesis 25:9 says *his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him*.

The son who was sent away came back to put his father in the ground.

Sending him into the desert did not erase him from the family.

He was still Abraham’s son, and he stood at the grave like one.

## Twelve Princes

Now, the promise.

Before any of that happened, back when Abraham was ninety nine and God was giving him his new name, Abraham begged God for his boy.

And God answered.

> “And as for Ishmael, I have heard thee: Behold, I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful, and will multiply him exceedingly; twelve princes shall he beget, and I will make him a great nation.” (Genesis 17:20)

Twelve princes. God gave a number. That was decades before the water ran out under that bush. Now watch the Bible count.

> “And these are the names of the sons of Ishmael, by their names, according to their generations: the firstborn of Ishmael, Nebajoth; and Kedar, and Adbeel, and Mibsam, And Mishma, and Dumah, and Massa, Hadar, and Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah.” (Genesis 25:13–15)

Count them. Nebajoth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, Kedemah. Twelve.

Exactly what God said.

The boy whose mother could not bear to watch him die has twelve sons with names, and every one of them is written down.

> “These are the sons of Ishmael, and these are their names, by their towns, and by their castles; twelve princes according to their nations.” (Genesis 25:16)

## Towns and Castles

I want to slow down on three words in that verse, because they can give you the wrong picture.

First, prince. When I hear prince I think of a palace and a crown. That is not this.

A prince here is a chief, the head of a family group, the man a whole clan looks to.

Think of a large tent in the middle of a camp, with the herds and the families of everyone who belongs to him spread around it.

Twelve princes means twelve clan leaders, and the people under them took their names.

Just like Midian became the Midianites, Kedar became the people of Kedar.

The name of the son becomes the name of the branch.

Second, towns and castles. Were there twelve cities? That is what I wanted to know the first time I read this.

The answer is no, and the Bible is not saying that.

A town here is a settlement, a place with some houses where people stayed put.

A castle is not a stone fortress with a moat.

The old word means a camp, an encampment, a ring of tents with the animals penned inside, maybe with a rough wall of brush or stone around it.

So the verse is telling you that Ishmael’s family lived in both kinds of places. Some settled down in villages.

Some stayed in camps and moved. It does not hand each son his own city.

It describes a people with settlements and camps spread across a region.

## From Havilah Unto Shur

And where was that region?

> “And they dwelt from Havilah unto Shur, that is before Egypt, as thou goest toward Assyria: and he died in the presence of all his brethren.” (Genesis 25:18)

Notice the first word is *they*. Not Ishmael in one city. His descendants, spread out. Shur is the easy one.

It is the desert on the eastern edge of Egypt, the dry country between Egypt and Canaan, heading down toward Sinai.

That is the very road Hagar was on when the angel first found her, *by the fountain in the way to Shur*.

Her grandchildren ended up living in that same borderland. Havilah is harder.

It is somewhere out in Arabia, and most people who study this put it in the northern part.

And *as thou goest toward Assyria* is giving you a direction, up and east, not telling you Ishmael’s sons owned the Assyrian empire.

Put it together and you get a wide band of desert country south and east of Canaan, from the edge of Egypt out toward Arabia.

Let me paint that country for you, because I do not want you picturing endless sand dunes like a movie.

Most of it is bare rock and gravel, brown hills, dry valleys that only run with water a few days a year.

After the rains there are patches of thin grass, and then they burn off. Water is everything.

A spring, a well, an oasis with a few palms, that is where the camps gather, and when the grass is gone the whole camp moves to the next water.

That is what nomadic means. Not wandering for fun.

Moving your whole household and all your animals because the animals have to eat and drink.

So how did they live? Herds, mostly. Sheep and goats and camels.

And trade, because the roads between Egypt and Arabia and the north ran right through their country.

Later in the Bible you can see it. Ezekiel 27 talks about the princes of Kedar trading lambs and rams and goats.

Jeremiah 49 describes Kedar’s tents and flocks and camels.

And in Genesis 37, when Joseph is in the pit, a caravan of camels loaded with spices comes down the road toward Egypt.

The King James spells them *Ishmeelites*.

Those are Ishmael’s people, running a trade route with camels, two generations after today’s passage.

And remember what Ishmael himself was. An archer. Isaiah 21 still talks about the archers of Kedar centuries later.

Herders, traders, bowmen. That is the family.

## One Family, Many Branches

Now I do not want to give all twelve sons the same size biography, because the Bible does not.

Most of them are a name on the map and nothing more.

But a few of these names keep walking back into Scripture, and you should know them.

* 🐏 Nebajoth is the firstborn, and his family shows up in this very book. In Genesis 28, Esau goes to Ishmael and marries his daughter Mahalath, *the sister of Nebajoth*. So keep Nebajoth in your head. His sister is about to marry Abraham’s grandson. Isaiah 60 later talks about *the rams of Nebaioth* and *the flocks of Kedar* being brought as gifts to God. Even their sheep get mentioned.
* 🏹 Kedar is the one the prophets talk about most. Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel all know Kedar as a desert people with tents, flocks, camels and archers. When a prophet a thousand years later says Kedar, he means the children of this second son of Ishmael.
* 🌴 Tema became an oasis town on the caravan roads. Job 6 talks about *the troops of Tema* looking for water, and Isaiah 21 pictures the people of Tema bringing bread and water to people fleeing through the desert. Tema was a place you could get a drink.
* ⚔️ Jetur and Naphish show up together in 1 Chronicles 5, where the tribes of Israel living east of the Jordan go to war with them and win. Think about that. Reuben and Gad fighting Jetur and Naphish is Isaac’s great grandchildren fighting Ishmael’s. Family against family, just like we saw with Midian yesterday.

That last one is worth a moment. Ishmael’s sons and Isaac’s sons did not live in separate worlds.

They lived next to each other, traded with each other, married into each other and sometimes fought each other.

The Bible even mixes the names.

In the Joseph story, the same caravan is called Ishmeelites in one verse and Midianites in another.

Both are Abraham’s family, both are desert traders on the same road, and the writer does not seem worried about which cousin is driving which camel.

One family, many branches, all tangled up together. That is the picture.

## Gathered Unto His People

Now the man himself.

> “And these are the years of the life of Ishmael, an hundred and thirty and seven years: and he gave up the ghost and died; and was gathered unto his people.” (Genesis 25:17)

A hundred and thirty seven years.

Abraham lived to a hundred and seventy five, so Ishmael did not quite match his father, but that is a long, full life for a boy who nearly died at sixteen.

*Gave up the ghost* is old English for breathed his last. Ghost just means breath, spirit. He let go of his last breath.

And *gathered unto his people*. We talked about that phrase when Abraham died. It is more than a burial.

It means he joined the ones who went before him. Here is what I notice.

The Bible uses the exact same words for Ishmael’s death that it used for Abraham’s. Same honor. Same quiet dignity.

The boy under the bush got the same sentence as the father of the faithful.

Then the odd ending of verse 18. *He died in the presence of all his brethren*.

Read it the way the King James puts it and you picture a deathbed with his brothers around it.

Isaac on one side, Keturah’s boys on the other. That is how I read it the first time too.

But verse 17 already told us he died, and this verse is about where the family lived.

The word the King James turns into *died* is literally *fell* in Hebrew, and *brethren* can mean the whole wider family, cousins and clans, not just the seven brothers.

Most other Bibles translate it as he settled, or lived, facing all his relatives, or even in hostility toward them.

And here is why that matters.

Go back to the very first promise about Ishmael, before he was born, when the angel spoke to Hagar by the spring.

He said the boy would be a wild man, and he ended with these words: *he shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren*.

Same phrase. Genesis 16:12 said it would happen. Genesis 25:18 says it did.

So this is not a bedside scene. It is the Bible closing a loop.

Ishmael’s people ended up living right alongside the rest of Abraham’s family, face to face, close enough to trade and marry and fight.

Not inside the covenant, but never far from it.

Everything the angel told a pregnant servant girl on the road to Shur came true, down to the last line.

## The Hagar of Your Family

And that is the thing I want you to carry out of today.

God keeps promises to people who are not the main character. Think about who Hagar was. A foreign servant.

Used by her mistress, resented by her mistress, sent away with bread and a skin of water.

Nobody in that camp was building their hopes on Hagar’s son.

The covenant went through Isaac, and God said so plainly in the very next verse after the promise about Ishmael: *But my covenant will I establish with Isaac*.

Ishmael was not the line that leads to Jesus. But God heard him. God made him twelve princes and a great nation.

God gave him a hundred and thirty seven years and wrote his death down with the same words He used for Abraham.

The promise to the servant girl was kept as carefully as the promise to the father of the faithful.

Remember the big idea of this book. God said Abraham would be *a father of many nations*.

Yesterday we counted six sons who became peoples in the east.

Today we counted twelve princes who became peoples in the south.

The family is already far bigger than the one branch we usually follow. And every branch has God’s fingerprints on it.

He did not bless Isaac and forget the rest. He kept track of all of them, by name, by town, by camp.

If you have ever felt like the Hagar of your family, the one on the outside, the one whose story nobody is watching, read verse 16 again.

Twelve princes. God hears. He heard a boy under a bush, and He kept hearing his family for a thousand years.

Tomorrow the map turns back to the covenant line.

Verse 19 says *these are the generations of Isaac*, and the first thing we learn about Isaac’s marriage is that for twenty years there was no baby.

The family that waited twenty five years for Isaac is about to wait again.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 25

Seven verses, and most of them are names again. Before you start, go back and read Genesis 17:20.

That is God’s promise about Ishmael. Keep it open next to today’s passage and see if the numbers match.

Notice how the Bible introduces Ishmael in verse 12. It tells you who his mother was and whose servant she was.

That is on purpose.

The last verse has a strange ending. Read it twice. We will spend some time on it.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🔢 How many sons are named in verses 13 to 15
* ⛺ The two words in verse 16 that tell you where they lived
* ⏳ How old Ishmael is when he dies
* 🏜️ The region described in verse 18

And think about this question: what does it look like when God keeps a promise to someone outside the main story?

## Reflection Questions

1. **God gave Hagar a number, twelve princes, and then the Bible counted them out loud. What promise from God are you still waiting to see counted?** Write it down the way God said it. Then write today’s date next to it. His receipts come later than we want, but they come.
2. **Ishmael was outside the covenant and still inside God’s care. Who in your life have you quietly decided is outside God’s attention?** A relative, an ex, a coworker, yourself. God heard the boy under the bush. He hears that person too.
3. **Ishmael’s family lived face to face with Isaac’s family for centuries, sometimes trading, sometimes fighting. Who do you live face to face with that you have never made peace with?** Name them. Then ask God what one step toward peace would look like this week.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You heard a boy dying under a bush, and You did not just give him a drink.

You gave him twelve sons, a country, a hundred and thirty seven years, and a place in Your book.

You said twelve princes, and there were twelve princes.

Thank You for keeping promises to the people nobody else is watching. To the servant girl. To the son who was sent away.

To the branches of the family that never carried the covenant but always carried Your blessing.

Teach me that You hear me where I am, even when I feel outside the main story.

Forgive me for the people I have written off. For deciding who You care about and who You have forgotten.

You kept track of Kedar and Tema and Naphish by name. Nobody is off Your map.

And give me patience to wait for the count. You said it in the desert and You proved it a generation later.

Help me trust the number You gave me, even while the water skin feels empty.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
