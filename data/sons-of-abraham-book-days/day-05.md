Yesterday we left Isaac in Gerar with his lie exposed and a king’s order hanging over the town.

Touch this man or his wife and you die. God said He would be with Isaac, and He was, through a window and a Philistine.

Now watch what God does next with a man who just failed Him.

Isaac plants a field in the middle of a famine and reaps a hundred times what he sowed. The herds multiply.

The servants multiply.

The whole household gets so big that the people of Gerar stop being curious and start being afraid.

And when people get afraid of you, they do not come and talk. They go find your wells and fill them with dirt.

Then the king who protected him tells him to leave.

Today is about what happens when the people around you turn, when the agreement your father made stops meaning anything, when you have to pack up and move and dig and move again.

Isaac is going to name three wells after what happened at each one, and the names tell the whole story. Trouble. Hatred.

Room.

And by the end, the same men who sent him away are standing in his camp asking for peace. Let’s dig.

# Man’s Word, God’s Word

> “For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land.” (Genesis 26:22)

Today we are reading Genesis chapter 26, verses 12 to 33, and it starts with the question I asked you to hold overnight.

> “Then Isaac sowed in that land, and received in the same year an hundredfold: and the LORD blessed him.” (Genesis 26:12)

Let me tell you exactly what multiplied.

Isaac planted seed, and in that same year, a famine year, the harvest came back a hundred times what he put in the ground.

One sack of seed, a hundred sacks of grain. That is what hundredfold means.

Not his whole inheritance a hundred times bigger. One field, in a dry year, coming up like nothing anyone had seen.

And the Bible tells you why. *The LORD blessed him*.

Now the question. He just lied about his wife. Why is God blessing him?

Look at the order. God promised to bless Isaac in verse 3, before the lie. The lie was exposed and rebuked.

And the thing God actually asked, stay in the land, Isaac did. So this harvest is not God paying Isaac for lying.

It is God keeping the word He gave before the lie, in spite of it. Abraham came out of Egypt rich after his lie too.

Do not read success as God’s stamp of approval. A full barn is not a clean conscience.

> “And the man waxed great, and went forward, and grew until he became very great: For he had possession of flocks, and possession of herds, and great store of servants: and the Philistines envied him.” (Genesis 26:13–14)

Waxed great means he got rich and powerful. *Went forward* means he kept going, kept growing, year after year.

Flocks of sheep and goats, herds of cattle, and *great store of servants*, a whole workforce. Picture the size of this.

Not a little cluster of tents.

A town’s worth of people, with shepherds and herdsmen spread across the grazing land and watering places for miles, and Isaac’s main camp somewhere in the middle.

That is the household Abraham built, now growing in a famine.

And the Philistines envied him.

They watched the foreigner get richer while their own fields stayed brown, and it ate at them.

I used to read this as Isaac getting too big for his britches, but the Bible never says Isaac got proud.

It says the locals got jealous.

## Water Is Life

> “For all the wells which his father’s servants had digged in the days of Abraham his father, the Philistines had stopped them, and filled them with earth.” (Genesis 26:15)

Now feel this verse, because if you have running water in your house you will skip right over it.

In that country, water is life. Your family drinks from the well. Your flocks drink from the well.

And a well is not a hole somebody dug one afternoon.

It is weeks of men digging through rock and gravel until they hit water.

Abraham’s servants had dug wells all across that region, and those wells were the reason his family could stay in the south at all.

Filling one with dirt is not a prank.

It is a neighbor saying, we want you gone, and we are going to take away the one thing you cannot live without.

> “And Abimelech said unto Isaac, Go from us; for thou art much mightier than we.” (Genesis 26:16)

Then the king says it out loud. Leave. And look at his reason. *Thou art much mightier than we*. Not, you lied to us.

You are stronger than we are, and we are afraid of what that means.

A household that size is power sitting right outside your town. I do not think Isaac was planning to take Gerar.

But the king looked at the numbers and decided he did not want to find out.

## Man’s Word Wavers

And here is the thing this whole day is about.

Go back to Genesis 21.

The old Abimelech came to Abraham with his army commander, Phichol, and asked for a sworn agreement.

The Bible calls it a covenant. It was supposed to reach down the generations.

No dealing falsely with my son or my son’s son. Kindness for kindness.

Abraham swore it, gave him seven ewe lambs to seal it, and named the place Beersheba because of the oath.

That was man’s word. One generation later, here is what it is worth. Envy. Wells full of dirt. Get out.

Now look at God’s word. God made a covenant with Abraham too, sworn on His own name.

One generation later, here is what it is worth. *I will be with thee, and will bless thee*.

A hundredfold harvest in a famine. Flocks, herds, servants, growing while the neighbors seethe.

Same generation. Two promises made to the same man. One fell apart the moment fear and envy got involved.

The other is still standing, still growing.

To be fair, nobody formally tore up the old treaty, and Isaac’s own lie in Gerar did not honor it either.

Not every human promise is worthless. But human promises bend when the pressure rises. God’s do not.

His faithfulness to Isaac did not depend on anyone in Gerar continuing to like him. Man’s word wavers. God’s word holds.

Watch how the rest of the chapter proves it.

## His Father’s Wells

> “And Isaac departed thence, and pitched his tent in the valley of Gerar, and dwelt there.” (Genesis 26:17)

Let me clear up the geography. If Isaac left, why are the same people still fighting with him?

Because Gerar is both a town and the country around it. Isaac had been camped near the town.

Now he moves out into the valley of Gerar, the countryside that belongs to it.

A big pastoral household moving its camp from near the walls out into the open range.

And that valley is a wadi, a dry streambed that only runs after rain, with water sitting under the gravel, which is why men dig there.

Isaac is still in the region, still among the same herdsmen, still in the land God told him to stay in.

Put this next to Abraham in Genesis 12. Famine came, and Abraham went to Egypt.

Famine came to Isaac, God said do not go, and he did not go.

Now he is being pushed around inside the land, and he still does not go.

He moves, but within the direction God gave him. That is obedience under pressure.

Staying, and shifting your tent when you have to.

> “And Isaac digged again the wells of water, which they had digged in the days of Abraham his father; for the Philistines had stopped them after the death of Abraham: and he called their names after the names by which his father had called them.” (Genesis 26:18)

First thing he does is go back to his father’s wells and dig the dirt out. Notice when they were filled in.

*After the death of Abraham*. As long as the old man was alive, nobody touched them.

The day he was gone, the shovels came out. The peace was built on respect for one man, not on the agreement.

And Isaac gives the wells back their old names, the names Abraham used.

These were my father’s, and they will be called what he called them.

The son restoring what the father built, one well at a time.

## The LORD Hath Made Room for Us

> “And Isaac’s servants digged in the valley, and found there a well of springing water. And the herdmen of Gerar did strive with Isaac’s herdmen, saying, The water is ours: and he called the name of the well Esek; because they strove with him. And they digged another well, and strove for that also: and he called the name of it Sitnah.” (Genesis 26:19–21)

Then his men dig a new one, and they hit springing water. Living water, the Hebrew says.

Not a pit that collects rain, but a spring flowing up from underneath, the best kind of well there is.

And the moment they find it, the local herdsmen show up. *The water is ours*. Strive means they quarreled over it.

Not a battle with swords. A fight over a hole in the ground that could water a thousand animals.

So Isaac names the well Esek, which means strife. Trouble. Contention.

Then they dig another one, and the same thing happens, and he names that one Sitnah, which means hatred, hostility, the kind of thing an enemy does to you.

He is naming the wells after what he went through at each one.

This should remind you of Genesis 13, when Abraham’s herdsmen and Lot’s herdsmen fought over grazing and Abraham gave Lot first choice rather than fight.

Isaac does the same thing his father did. He gives ground. He could have taken the well by force.

The king just said he was mightier than the whole town. Instead he walks away and digs again.

That is not a rule that you must never defend what is yours.

It is a man deciding that a fight over every water hole is not worth it, and trusting God for water somewhere else.

> “And he removed from thence, and digged another well; and for that they strove not: and he called the name of it Rehoboth; and he said, For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land.” (Genesis 26:22)

Third well. No fight. And Isaac names it Rehoboth, which means room. Wide open spaces. Listen to what he says.

*The LORD hath made room for us*. Not, we finally outlasted them. The LORD made room.

After two wells taken from him, Isaac looks at the one that was not and gives God the credit.

*We shall be fruitful in the land*. We can grow here.

Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth. Strife, hatred, room.

Say those three in order and you have the shape of a lot of seasons in a believer’s life.

## The Well of the Oath

> “And he went up from thence to Beersheba. And the LORD appeared unto him the same night, and said, I am the God of Abraham thy father: fear not, for I am with thee, and will bless thee, and multiply thy seed for my servant Abraham’s sake.” (Genesis 26:23–24)

Beersheba. Yes, you know that name. Hagar wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba with her water skin.

Abraham swore his oath to the old Abimelech there and named the place for it, the well of the oath.

He planted a grove there and called on the name of the LORD. This is family ground.

Why leave Rehoboth if he had finally found water with no fight? A household like his was never nailed to one spot.

Rehoboth was room in the land, not a promise never to move the main camp again, and Beersheba was a place the family had used for decades.

And the very first night at Beersheba, God shows up.

Every word is aimed at a man who has been shoved around for a chapter. *I am the God of Abraham thy father*.

The same God who kept His word to your father. *Fear not*. You have been afraid since Gerar. Stop. *I am with thee*.

I was with you at Esek and at Sitnah and at Rehoboth. I have not gone anywhere.

*And will bless thee, and multiply thy seed*. The promise is still the promise. For my servant Abraham’s sake.

The treaty failed. The wells got filled. The king said leave. And God’s promise did not move an inch.

He waits until Isaac is standing on his father’s ground, and then He says it again, out loud, so Isaac can hear it over the noise.

> “And he builded an altar there, and called upon the name of the LORD, and pitched his tent there: and there Isaac’s servants digged a well.” (Genesis 26:25)

Look at the order. Altar first. This is the first altar we ever see Isaac build.

His father built them everywhere he camped, and now the son does it, right where Abraham did.

Then he pitches his tent, and then the servants start digging. Worship, settle, work.

That is how a man responds when God says fear not. He does not just feel better.

He builds something, sets up camp, and puts shovels in the ground.

## Here Comes the King

> “Then Abimelech went to him from Gerar, and Ahuzzath one of his friends, and Phichol the chief captain of his army. And Isaac said unto them, Wherefore come ye to me, seeing ye hate me, and have sent me away from you?” (Genesis 26:26–27)

And now, of all people, here comes the king.

He brings Ahuzzath, a friend, meaning a trusted adviser, and Phichol, the commander of his army.

That is the same name as the commander beside the old Abimelech in Genesis 21, a generation later, probably successors carrying the old names.

This is an official delegation, walking out to Isaac’s camp to talk.

And Isaac does not roll out a welcome. *Wherefore come ye to me, seeing ye hate me, and have sent me away from you?*

Why are you here? You hate me. You threw me out. I love that he says it.

He names the hostility to their faces before he hears a word.

> “And they said, We saw certainly that the LORD was with thee: and we said, Let there be now an oath betwixt us, even betwixt us and thee, and let us make a covenant with thee; That thou wilt do us no hurt, as we have not touched thee, and as we have done unto thee nothing but good, and have sent thee away in peace: thou art now the blessed of the LORD.” (Genesis 26:28–29)

Here is their answer. *We saw certainly that the LORD was with thee*.

They watched him lose his wells and keep growing, get pushed from place to place and come out blessed every time.

It finally sank in. God is with this man.

And if God is with this man, we would rather have him swear not to hurt us than find out what he can do.

Then they say something that made me stop. *We have done unto thee nothing but good, and have sent thee away in peace*.

Nothing but good? Filled wells and get out of town? I do not buy it. This is a king talking the way kings talk.

What he means is, we never laid a hand on you. We let you leave without a war.

True as far as it goes, but a long way from an apology, and no apology comes.

> “And he made them a feast, and they did eat and drink. And they rose up betimes in the morning, and sware one to another: and Isaac sent them away, and they departed from him in peace.” (Genesis 26:30–31)

And Isaac makes peace anyway. He does not wait for the apology.

He throws a feast, they eat and drink together, and betimes in the morning, that means early, they swear oaths to each other and go home.

No more hostility between Isaac’s household and Gerar.

A household that size needs safe grazing, open roads, undisputed water and no raids, so this matters.

And it tells you something I do not want you to miss. Man’s word is fragile, but it is not worthless.

Broken relationships can be repaired. God’s word is the thing you build on.

Man’s word is still worth making, and worth keeping.

## We Have Found Water

> “And it came to pass the same day, that Isaac’s servants came, and told him concerning the well which they had digged, and said unto him, We have found water. And he called it Shebah: therefore the name of the city is Beersheba unto this day.” (Genesis 26:32–33)

The same day. The delegation has barely left, and the servants come running from the well they started in verse 25.

*We have found water*. Peace in the morning, water in the afternoon.

Isaac names the well Shebah, which plays on the Hebrew words for oath and for seven, and that is why the city is called Beersheba, the well of the oath.

Abraham had already named the place that, after his oath and his seven lambs.

Now Isaac has his own oath and his own well in the same spot. Father and son, same ground, same God, same water.

So step back. Isaac was blessed, envied, cut off from water, told to leave, fought over twice, and finally given room.

Then God met him at his father’s well and said fear not. Then his enemies came asking for peace. Then the water came in.

That is the one thing to take out of today. Opposition changed Isaac’s address. It never changed God’s presence.

The people who should have kept their fathers’ agreement did not. God kept His.

I am not telling you that if you trust God you will always get rich and every enemy will come to dinner.

Isaac lost two wells for good.

I am telling you the opposition did not cancel God’s purpose, and it will not cancel His purpose in your life either.

Keep digging. Move when you have to. Give ground rather than make war over every hole.

And when you get to the family ground, build the altar before you pitch the tent.

Isaac is at peace now. Wells full, a treaty with the neighbors, God’s promise in his ears.

But the trouble in this family was never going to come from the Philistines. It was going to come from inside the tent.

Tomorrow Isaac is an old man with failing eyes, he calls for his favorite son and asks for one last meal of venison, and his wife is listening at the door.

For now, take the reflection questions first, then today’s action, and end your day in prayer.

## What To Watch For In Genesis 26

This is a longer passage, and it is really one story: blessing, envy, being pushed out, digging for water, and finally peace.

Read it in one sitting so you feel the whole arc.

Keep a finger in Genesis 21:22 to 32.

The names Abimelech, Phichol and Beersheba all come from that scene, and today the next generation walks right back into it.

Count the wells. Count how many times Isaac moves instead of fights.

**As you read, notice:**

* 🌾 What exactly multiplied a hundredfold in verse 12
* 🪨 What the Philistines do to the wells, and when they did it
* 🏷️ The three names Isaac gives his wells in verses 20 to 22
* 🔥 What God says to Isaac at Beersheba, and what Isaac does first

And think about this question: whose word do you build your life on, man’s or God’s?

## Reflection Questions

1. **The treaty Abraham made with Gerar fell apart in one generation, and God’s promise did not. Where in your life has a person’s word failed you while God’s word quietly held?** Write both down side by side. Then ask yourself which one you have been building on.
2. **Isaac walked away from Esek and Sitnah rather than fight for them. What well are you still fighting over that God may be asking you to leave behind?** Name it. Then ask whether the fight is really about the water or about not wanting to lose.
3. **When God said fear not, Isaac built an altar before he pitched his tent. Where have you been pushed to recently, and what would it look like to worship there before you settle in?** A new job, a new town, a new season you did not choose. Build the altar first.

## Today's One Thing

**Name Your Wells**

Isaac named his wells after what happened at each one. Do the same with the seasons you are standing in.

1. Write three words on a page: Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth. Next to Esek, write one fight you are in right now. Next to Sitnah, write one place where someone is hostile to you. Next to Rehoboth, write one place where God has clearly made room for you.
2. Pick one old well. Something good your parents, grandparents or a mentor built in your life that has gotten filled in with dirt. A habit, a prayer, a relationship. Dig it out today. Call the person, restart the habit, say the prayer.
3. Thank God out loud for the Rehoboth on your list. Say the words: the LORD hath made room for us.

Three wells named, one old well reopened, one thank you said out loud. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

Father,

You blessed Isaac in a famine, and when the people around him turned on him for it, You did not turn with them.

Every well they filled, You were still there. Every time he moved, You moved with him.

Your word did not bend when theirs did.

Thank You that Your promises to me do not depend on people approving of me.

Thank You for the room You have made that I did not notice because I was busy counting the wells I lost.

Teach me to build on Your word and not on man’s, and still to make peace with men.

Give me Isaac’s patience to walk away from Esek and Sitnah instead of fighting over every hole in the ground.

And give me his faith to keep digging, because the next one might be Rehoboth.

When I get to the ground You have brought me to, let me build the altar before I pitch the tent.

Say fear not to me the way You said it to him, and let me hear it over the noise.

In Jesus’ name, amen.
