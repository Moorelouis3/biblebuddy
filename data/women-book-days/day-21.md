Two sisters loved Jesus with everything they had. They just did not love Him the same way. One ran to the kitchen the moment He walked through the door. The other sat down on the floor and listened.

Then their brother got sick. Then he died. And both sisters stood at that grave asking Jesus the exact same question: where were You when we needed You?

Maybe you already know which sister you are. Maybe you are the one who cooks, plans, and serves, and quietly wonders if anyone notices how tired you are. Or maybe you are the one who would rather sit still and listen, and you feel guilty for not doing more.

Both sisters wrestled, without ever saying it out loud, with the same fear: is the way I love God the wrong way?

On this last day of our study, we meet two women who answer that question together, and a Savior who loved them both, exactly as they were.

**Today we close with the sisters of Bethany, and the Jesus who wept before He worked a miracle.**

## One Sister Serves, One Sister

## Sits

Martha and Mary were sisters in the village of Bethany, about two miles from Jerusalem, and they had a brother named Lazarus. Jesus loved this family and stayed in their home more than once. The first time we meet them, Jesus arrives, and Martha opens her home to Him, no small act of work and hospitality for a traveling teacher and His followers. And while Martha works, Mary does something unusual.

*“And she had a sister called Mary, which also sat at Jesus’ feet, and heard his word.” (Luke 10:39)*

Sitting at a rabbi's feet as a student was, in that culture, almost always reserved for men. Mary sat there anyway, and Jesus let her stay. Meanwhile the work piled up on Martha until she broke.

*“Lord, dost thou not care that my sister hath left me to serve alone? bid her therefore that she help me.” (Luke 10:40)*

Hear what she actually says. Not just, tell her to help. *Dost thou not care.* That is the sound of a tired heart, not just tired hands. Martha was not lazy or petty. She was exhausted and felt alone in it, and she said so out loud to Jesus, who did not scold her for saying it. His answer was tender and honest at once.

*“Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part.” (Luke 10:41–42)*

Saying her name twice is tenderness, not anger. And notice what He does not say. He never calls her serving wrong. He says she is *careful and troubled*, anxious and pulled in every direction, when only one thing was needed in that moment. Busy hands and a distant heart can happen even while doing good, useful things. This was never a contest between a good sister and a bad one. It was a loving correction about where Martha's heart had drifted while her hands stayed busy.

## A Brother Dies, and Jesus

## Waits

Some time later, Lazarus fell gravely ill, and the sisters sent for Jesus with a message full of confidence: *Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick*. And then Scripture tells you something crucial right before the hardest part.

*“Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus. When he had heard therefore that he was sick, he abode two days still in the same place where he was.” (John 11:5–6)*

Read those two verses together, because they are meant to be read together. He loved them, **and** He stayed put anyway. Two more days passed while Lazarus worsened and died. This is one of the hardest truths in the Gospels: Jesus' love for this family did not mean an instant rescue. His delay was not neglect. It was aimed at something they could not yet see. If you have ever wondered why God lets suffering run its course instead of stopping it right away, this family lived that question before you did.

By the time Jesus arrived, Lazarus had been in the grave four days. Many believed a person's spirit lingered near the body three days; by the fourth, all hope was gone. And the sisters respond true to character. Martha, the one who moves, went out to meet Jesus on the road. Mary, the one who sits, stayed in the house. And Martha said one of the most honest sentences in the Bible.

*“Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died. But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee.” (John 11:21–22)*

That is grief and faith in a single breath. A little blame, spoken straight to His face, and then, without pausing, I still know God will give You whatever You ask. Jesus told her, *thy brother shall rise again*, and Martha answered with correct theology and a broken heart: I know he will rise at the last day. And Jesus lifted her eyes from someday to right now.

*“I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live... Believest thou this?” (John 11:25–26)*

And Martha, the woman we usually remember only for being *cumbered about much serving*, gave one of the clearest confessions of faith in the entire Bible: *Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God.* She said it before Lazarus walked out of the grave. She believed first, and saw second. Do not reduce Martha to the kitchen. In the worst week of her life, she spoke a creed the whole church still stands on.

## Jesus Wept

Then Jesus called for Mary, and she came and fell at His feet, the same place we first found her, and said the identical words her sister had said: *Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.* Same grief, same ache, said on her knees. And Jesus did not answer Mary with a theology lesson. He answered her with His whole heart.

*“Jesus wept.” (John 11:35)*

The shortest verse in the Bible, and one of the most staggering. Jesus already knew He was about to raise Lazarus. He wept anyway. God in the flesh, standing feet from a miracle He was about to perform, still let Himself cry over the grief of people He loved. Why weep if you know the ending is good? Because sorrow is real even when hope is real. He did not need to grieve for information. He grieved because He loved them, and death is an enemy even to the God who is about to defeat it. Whatever these twenty one women have shown you about being seen by God, this verse shows you the heart behind the seeing. He does not watch your grief from a distance. He stands in it and cries.

Then He walked to the tomb and told them to take away the stone. Martha, ever practical, protested that after four days there would be a stench. Jesus reminded her: did I not tell you that if you believe, you would see the glory of God? They rolled the stone back, He prayed aloud for the crowd's sake, and then He cried with a loud voice, *Lazarus, come forth*. And a man four days dead walked out of his own grave, bound in his grave clothes. The One who wept at that tomb was the same One who had the power to empty it. Grief and power were never opposites in Jesus. They stood side by side at the same grave.

## The Perfume and the Close

There is one more scene, days before the Passover, back in Bethany. They made Jesus a supper, and *Martha served*. Still Martha. Still serving. And now nobody is arguing about it, because her serving, offered to Jesus with her heart in the right place, was always a beautiful thing. She had not changed who she was; she had learned where to put her heart while she worked. And then Mary did something extravagant.

*“Then took Mary a pound of ointment of spikenard, very costly, and anointed the feet of Jesus, and wiped his feet with her hair.” (John 12:3)*

A pound of pure spikenard was worth nearly a year's wages, perhaps her whole savings, poured out on His feet and wiped with her hair, something a respectable woman simply did not do in public. Judas objected that it should have been sold for the poor, but the text exposes him: he said it not because he cared for the poor, but because he was a thief. And Jesus defended her Himself: *let her alone: against the day of my burying hath she kept this*. Of everyone in that room, quiet Mary may have been the only one who understood what was coming. He had told the disciples plainly that He would die, and they kept missing it. The sister who listened at His feet was the one who was ready. Sometimes the person who loves quietly is paying closer attention than the ones doing all the talking.

Here is where this final day lands on you, and where our whole study comes to rest. There is more than one right way to love Jesus. Martha served and Mary sat, and He loved them both; you do not have to choose between them, only to know which one the moment in front of you calls for. Honest grief and real faith can live in the same sentence, the way they did in both sisters at that grave. Jesus grieves with you even when He is about to fix the thing, and delayed is not the same as denied. And what you pour out for Him, even when the world calls it a waste, is never wasted on Him.

For twenty one days you have walked from a garden to an empty tomb, meeting women God never once overlooked. Two of them, these sisters, loved Him in opposite ways and were both held close. That is where the study ends, and where your own story with Him begins. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today's action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

Take a few minutes and answer these honestly.

**Which sister am I, and have I been judging the other one?**

1. Martha served; Mary sat; Jesus loved both and never ranked them against each other. Which am I by nature, and have I quietly looked down on people who love God the other way?

**Can I hold grief and faith in the same breath?**

2. Both sisters said if thou hadst been here, and both still trusted Him. Martha confessed her creed through tears. What am I grieving that I could bring to God honestly, without letting go of my faith to do it?

**What have I been afraid to pour out as a waste?**

3. Mary’s perfume was worth a year’s wages. Judas called it waste. Jesus called it beautiful. What costly thing is God asking me to pour out on Him that others might not understand?

## Before You Close This Book

You made it. Twenty one days. Twenty two women.

You started in a garden with the first woman who ever lived. You stood outside an empty tomb with Mary Magdalene, the first person who ever saw the risen Jesus. And you finished in a home in Bethany, with two sisters who loved Him two different ways. From the first woman to the women at the empty tomb, the ones carrying this story were women. That is not an accident, and by now I hope you can see it.

Think back over who you just met. A mother who lost two sons in one day and trusted God for another. A slave girl who gave God a name. A wife nobody loved whose son carried the line of the Messiah. A widow who fought for her place. A harlot with a scarlet cord. A foreigner in a barley field. A mother who gave her only son back to God. A queen who said if I perish, I perish.

And yes, a queen who pulled a nation away from God, and a woman who sold out the strongest man alive for silver. The Bible kept them in the story too, because some lives are warnings, and a warning written down in love is still love.

Here is the thread that ran through all of it.

## People Overlooked These Women.

## God Never Did.

The LORD saw Leah. The LORD heard Hannah. God remembered Rachel. The God who sees found Hagar in the wilderness, twice. Jesus sat down at a well at noon for one woman the whole town avoided. And when He rose from the dead, He said one word, *Mary*, and sent a woman to carry the greatest news ever told.

So the next time somebody tells you the Bible has no place for women, you will know better. Women are not background characters in God's story. They are woven into every chapter of it, from the mother of all living to the mother of the Savior, and the family line of Jesus Himself runs through Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and the woman Matthew calls the wife of Uriah.

And the next time you feel overlooked yourself, unloved, unseen, stuck waiting on a promise that has not come, you now know twenty two women who stood exactly where you are standing. Go back and read their days again. That is why this book is built the way it is. It is not meant to be read once.

Keep reading your Bible. Every one of these stories is still sitting there in Scripture, with more detail than any study can hold. You know how to walk through a chapter now. Read it slowly, watch what God does, and ask what it means for your life today.
