She was an orphan with no power and no family name that mattered. She was taken from her home and placed inside a palace she never asked to enter. And she hid the one thing that made her who she was: the fact that she was Jewish.

Then one day she had to choose between staying quiet and safe, or speaking up when speaking up could get her killed.

Maybe you know a smaller version of that fear. The moment when doing the right thing could actually cost you something real. A job. A friendship. Your place in a room full of people who do not know the real you. Most of us never have to risk our lives over it. Esther did.

An entire nation was marked for extermination, and one young queen was the only person positioned to say anything about it. Saying it might end her life on the spot.

Yesterday you watched a queen spend her power to destroy God’s people. Today you watch a queen spend hers to save them.

**Today we meet the orphan who became a queen for such a time as this.**

## An Orphan Who Learned to

## Hide

Esther's story opens in the Persian empire, during the exile, generations after Babylon carried the Jewish people from their homeland and Persia later conquered Babylon. Her birth name was Hadassah, which means myrtle tree. Esther, likely a Persian name connected to the word for star, came later. Her parents had died, and her older cousin Mordecai raised her as his own daughter.

*“And he brought up Hadassah, that is, Esther, his uncle’s daughter: for she had neither father nor mother... whom Mordecai... took for his own daughter.” (Esther 2:7)*

No father. No mother. No say in what happened to her. When King Ahasuerus, the ruler historians identify with Xerxes, removed his previous queen and his officials gathered beautiful young women from across the empire, Esther was swept up with the rest and brought into the king's house. And Mordecai gave her one instruction before she went in: do not tell them you are Jewish.

In a foreign palace, being part of a scattered minority was not a safe thing to announce, and Esther obeyed. Think about what that meant every single day. She could not fully be herself with anyone around her. She carried a secret inside the most watched building in the empire. And then the thing nobody could have planned happened.

*“And the king loved Esther above all the women... so that he set the royal crown upon her head, and made her queen instead of Vashti.” (Esther 2:17)*

An orphan girl with no family name now wore the crown of Persia. She had walked into that palace with no power at all. Now she had a crown, a husband who ruled most of the known world, and still, a secret she was not allowed to tell.

## A Plot to Erase a People

The king promoted a man named Haman above every official in the kingdom and commanded everyone to bow to him. Everyone did, except Mordecai. Scripture does not fully explain why Mordecai would not bow; as a Jew, that kind of reverence may have felt like worship owed to God alone. Whatever his reason, he held his ground day after day, and Haman's pride could not take it.

Watch how fast a wounded ego becomes mass murder. Haman learned Mordecai was a Jew, and decided that punishing one man was beneath him.

*“Wherefore Haman sought to destroy all the Jews that were throughout the whole kingdom of Ahasuerus, even the people of Mordecai.” (Esther 3:6)*

He took the plan to the king without naming the real reason, describing only a certain scattered people whose laws were different and who did not keep the king's laws, and he offered to fund the whole thing himself, ten thousand talents of silver into the royal treasury. The king agreed without even asking who the people were, handed Haman his signet ring, and letters went out across the empire, sealed with the king's seal, ordering every Jewish man, woman, and child killed on a single day. And Esther, the queen of Persia, was Jewish. She was on the death list, and almost no one in the palace knew it.

## For Such a Time As This

Mordecai tore his clothes in grief and sent word to Esther: go to the king and plead for your people. Esther sent back the hard reality. There was a law that anyone who approached the king uninvited could be put to death, unless he extended his golden scepter to spare them, and she had not been called to him in thirty days. Mordecai's answer is the heart of the whole book, and one of the most quoted passages in Scripture.

*“Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king’s house, more than all the Jews... and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?” (Esther 4:13–14)*

Being queen would not protect her. Silence would not protect her. And then the question: who knows whether your whole life, including the parts that felt like an accident, the orphaning, the taking, the crown, has been leading to this exact moment? If you have ever wondered whether the strange path your life has taken actually has a purpose, this is the verse to sit with.

Esther asked Mordecai to gather the Jews to fast for her three days while she and her maids did the same. Then she gave her answer.

*“So will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish.” (Esther 4:16)*

**If I perish, I perish.** That is not confidence that it would all work out. That is courage in spite of the fear. She fasted for three days first, which tells you she was terrified. She went in anyway. That is what real courage is: not the absence of fear, but obedience while the fear is still there. Waiting for the fear to leave before you obey is a wait that may never end.

## The Gallows He Built

On the third day, Esther put on her royal robes and walked uninvited into the inner court, and the king held out the golden scepter. Her life was spared the moment she walked in. But she did not blurt out her request. She invited the king and Haman to a private banquet, and then to a second one the next night, letting the tension build.

Haman left that first banquet glowing, until he saw Mordecai still refusing to rise for him, and his good mood curdled. His wife and friends suggested he build a gallows seventy five feet high and ask the king in the morning to hang Mordecai on it. The idea pleased Haman, and he had the gallows built. He went to bed satisfied, with no idea what the next twenty four hours held.

That same night, the king could not sleep. He called for the royal records to be read aloud, and they happened to include the time Mordecai had uncovered a plot to assassinate the king, a good deed never rewarded. The next morning the king asked Haman what should be done for the man the king delights to honor. Haman, assuming it was himself, described an elaborate royal parade, and then had to lead Mordecai through the city in it, announcing his honor. The gallows he built was still standing when he went to the second banquet, and there Esther finally made her request.

*“And Esther said, The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman. Then Haman was afraid before the king and the queen.” (Esther 7:6)*

The queen who had hidden her identity for years said it out loud, at the exact moment it mattered most, in front of the two most powerful men in the empire. Haman was hanged on the very gallows he had built for Mordecai. The original decree could not be revoked, so the king let Mordecai and Esther write a new one giving the Jews the right to defend themselves, and when the day came, they were delivered. The Jewish people turned their sorrow into the yearly feast of Purim, still kept today.

Here is the thing worth carrying out of this book. God's name never appears in it, not once in ten chapters. And yet everything lines up too perfectly to be chance. An orphan happens to become queen right before her people need her. A king happens to lose sleep on the one night it mattered. A forgotten good deed happens to be read aloud at the exact right moment. God is never named, and He is present on every single page. That is the lesson for your own life, especially on the days you cannot see Him working at all. Your position is not an accident. Courage is acting while still afraid. And the hand you cannot see may be arranging every detail of the room you are standing in.

## Reflection Questions

Take a few minutes and answer these honestly.

**What have I been placed here to do?**

1. Mordecai asked whether Esther’s whole life had led to one moment. What position, relationship, or room am I in right now that I might be in for a reason bigger than me?

**Am I waiting for fear to leave before I obey?**

2. Esther fasted three days first. She was terrified, and she went anyway. What right thing am I postponing until I feel brave? What would acting while still afraid look like?

**Can I trust God even when I cannot see Him working?**

3. His name is never in the book, and His hand is on every page. Where have I decided God is absent, simply because He is not obvious?

## Name Your Such-a-time

Esther’s turning point was recognizing her moment. Look for yours.

**1. Write down one situation where you are positioned to do a right, costly thing that few others could do.**

**2. Name the fear attached to it honestly, the job, the relationship, the comfort it might cost.**

One such-a-time, one honest fear, one step taken. Do it, and check it off.
