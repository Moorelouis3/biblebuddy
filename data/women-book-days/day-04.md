Astranger showed up at her family’s well one evening and asked for a drink of water. By the end of that conversation, Rebekah had agreed to leave her home forever and marry a man she had never met.

She said yes on the spot. Three words: *I will go*. That kind of quick, bold trust is what made Rebekah remarkable.

Years later, that same boldness is what made her dangerous.

Because God told Rebekah something enormous about her sons before they were even born. He said the older one would serve the younger one. That should have settled it. God had said it. It was going to happen.

But her husband grew old and blind, the moment came close, and it looked like everything was about to go the other way. So Rebekah did what a lot of us do when we are afraid God will not come through in time. She decided to help Him, with a lie.

**Today we meet the woman who believed God’s promise completely, and trusted His timing not at all.**

## Before You Read

In Genesis 25, Rebekah does something rare: she takes her confusion directly to the LORD and asks Him. Watch what He tells her about her sons.

Watch for one short verse about favorites. It explains almost everything that goes wrong in this family.

In Genesis 27, count the steps in Rebekah's plan. Notice how detailed it is, and notice who never once asks whether it is right.

## The Girl Who Said I Will Go

Abraham was old, and Sarah's tent stood empty. Before he died, he wanted one thing settled: a wife for Isaac, the son of the promise, and not from the local Canaanite tribes. So he sent his oldest servant back to the old country, to his own relatives, to find her.

The servant arrived at a well outside the city with ten thirsty camels, and he prayed one of the most specific prayers in the Bible. Let the right girl be the one who offers to water the camels too. Understand what he was asking for. A thirsty camel can drink an enormous amount of water. He was not praying for a polite girl. He was praying for a girl who would volunteer for hours of hard labor for a total stranger.

*“And when she had done giving him drink, she said, I will draw water for thy camels also, until they have done drinking.” (Genesis 24:19)*

Before he had even finished praying, Rebekah walked up and did the exact thing he had asked God for, word for word, without knowing anyone was watching. Back and forth to the well, jar after jar, for ten camels. One ordinary act of generosity, done when she thought nobody important was looking, set the whole direction of her life.

The servant told her family the whole story, and they agreed the thing was from the LORD. But the final question belonged to her alone. *Wilt thou go with this man?* A lifetime away from home, a husband she had never seen, all of it riding on whether she believed God was in this.

*“And they called Rebekah, and said unto her, Wilt thou go with this man? And she said, I will go.” (Genesis 24:58)*

No stalling, no ten days to think it over, which is exactly what her family suggested. Once she believed it was God's doing, she moved. And the chapter ends with a line Scripture does not hand out often: Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent, *and took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her*. Notice the order. Commitment first, love growing after. Their marriage began with obedience and became affection, and Isaac, still grieving his mother, was comforted.

## Two Nations in One Womb

Then came the familiar wait. Rebekah was barren, and the waiting stretched twenty years. Isaac did not reach for a workaround the way his parents had. He prayed for his wife, and the LORD answered. But the pregnancy itself felt like a war.

*“And the children struggled together within her; and she said, If it be so, why am I thus? And she went to enquire of the LORD.” (Genesis 25:22)*

Mark what she did, because it is the best thing Rebekah ever teaches you. When life felt unbearable and made no sense, her first move was not to panic and not to guess. **She went and asked God directly.** And God answered her with a full prophecy about her sons.

Two nations were in her womb. Two peoples would come from her. One would be stronger than the other. And *the elder shall serve the younger*. Hold on to that sentence. God told her the ending in advance, plainly, before either boy drew breath. Everything she does later comes from how she handled that promise.

The twins were born, Esau first, red and hairy, and Jacob second, gripping his brother's heel. They grew into completely different men, Esau a hunter of the open field, Jacob a quieter man of the tents. And then Genesis gives you one short verse that explains the next forty years of trouble.

*“And Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob.” (Genesis 25:28)* Each parent picked a favorite, and the Bible even tells you Isaac's reason: the food. A house split into teams, right from the start. A divided house rarely stays quiet forever, and this one did not.

## The Plan at the Bedside

Years later, Isaac was old, his eyes too dim to see, and he believed he was near death. So he called Esau in to give him the blessing, the formal spoken act that would legally set who carried the family's future and the promises God gave Abraham. Hunt me venison, he said, make the meal I love, and I will bless you before I die.

Rebekah was listening at the door. And she moved fast, the same speed that once said I will go, now pointed somewhere darker.

*“Now therefore, my son, obey my voice according to that which I command thee.” (Genesis 27:8)*

The plan was hers from first to last. Two young goats, cooked the way Isaac loved. Esau's best clothes on Jacob's back, so he would smell right. Goatskins wrapped on his hands and neck, so the blind old man would feel hair when he reached out. Every detail was aimed at deceiving her own husband on what she thought was his deathbed.

And listen to Jacob's objection, because it tells you where this family was. He did not say, Mother, this is wrong. He said, what if he feels my smooth skin, *and I shall seem to him as a deceiver*, and get a curse instead of a blessing. Not right and wrong. Getting caught. Rebekah's answer is the most chilling sentence she ever speaks.

*“And his mother said unto him, Upon me be thy curse, my son: only obey my voice, and go fetch me them.” (Genesis 27:13)* Upon me be thy curse. She was ready to carry the fallout herself, if it bought Jacob what she believed God had already promised him. And the plan worked exactly as designed. Isaac felt the hairy hands, ate the meal, smelled the clothes, and gave Jacob the blessing meant for Esau. *The voice is Jacob’s voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau.*

Here is the question this chapter forces you to sit with. God had already said the younger would come out ahead. Twenty years earlier, in plain words, to her directly. **Was any of this necessary?** God's promise did not need her goatskins. Her lie did not create God's plan. It only decided how much the road to it would cost.

## What the Lie Cost Her

The bill arrived almost immediately. Esau came home, discovered the theft, and cried out with a great and bitter cry. Then his grief hardened into something worse.

*“And Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing wherewith his father blessed him: and Esau said in his heart, The days of mourning for my father are at hand; then will I slay my brother Jacob.” (Genesis 27:41)*

Murder, scheduled for the week of the funeral. Rebekah heard of it and moved fast one last time, now to save the son she had endangered. Flee to my brother Laban in Haran, she told Jacob, and stay *a few days*, until your brother's fury cools. Then I will send and fetch thee.

It was not a few days. It was twenty years. And Genesis never records Rebekah seeing Jacob's face again. Her last recorded words in the story are the fear underneath it all: *why should I be deprived also of you both in one day?* She had schemed to secure her favorite son's future, and the scheme took him out of her life. She got the outcome God had promised anyway, minus the twenty years of her son she paid for the shortcut.

Scripture closes her story quietly. She was buried in the cave of Machpelah, beside Abraham and Sarah, where Isaac would lie, one of the four mothers of Israel. And her legacy is exactly double, which is why she matters so much. The same woman shows you the best move a waiting believer can make, going straight to God with the thing you do not understand, and the worst one, grabbing His promise with both hands and a lie. The promise came true in spite of her plan, not because of it. God's word does not need our help. It needs our trust.

Tomorrow the trick at the bedside comes back around on this family. Jacob runs to his uncle Laban, and on his own wedding night, the deceiver gets deceived. Caught in the middle of it is a bride nobody asked for, a woman the Bible introduces with one bruising comparison. Her name is Leah. But today is not finished yet. Take the reflection questions first, then today's action, and end your day in prayer.

## Reflection Questions

Take a few minutes and answer these honestly.

**Which promise am I trying to make happen with my own**

1. **hands?** Rebekah believed the promise completely. What she did not trust was the timing and the method. Where am I right about what God said and wrong about how I am going after it?

**Where has favoritism crept into my house?**

2. Isaac loved Esau, Rebekah loved Jacob, and the household split into teams. Is there a person I am quietly teaming up with, or against, in my own family or circle?

**When waiting gets scary, which Rebekah am I?**

3. The young Rebekah went and asked the LORD. The older Rebekah listened at doors and cooked up schemes. What am I doing with my fear right now, asking or scheming?

## Today's Action

## Put the Plan Down

Rebekah had a true promise and a bad plan. Lay yours on the table where you can see them both.

**1. Write down the outcome you have been trying to force. Be specific about what you have been doing to force it.**

**2. Under it, write what the forcing is costing, and who it is costing. Rebekah’s price was twenty years of her son.**

**3. Tonight, pray the young Rebekah’s move: take the confusion straight to God and ask Him, and leave the scheme on the paper.**

One outcome, one honest cost, one handed-over plan. Do it, and check it off.

## Today's Prayer

*Father,*

*You told Rebekah the ending before her sons were even born, and she still could not keep her hands off the steering wheel. I understand her better than I want to admit.*

*I believe Your promises, Lord. It is Your timing I keep trying to edit. I listen at doors. I cook up plans. I dress things up to look the way I think they are supposed to look, and I call it helping You.*

**Your promises do not need my lies. Teach me to trust Your timing the way I say I trust Your word.**

*Thank You for the first Rebekah, the one who watered a stranger’s camels when nobody was watching, who said I will go with her whole life, who took her confusion straight to You and asked. Build that first Rebekah in me.*

*And guard me from the second one. From favorites in my house. From upon me be the cost. From winning the outcome and losing the people. When I am afraid You will be late, remind me that You came through for her in spite of the scheme, and that twenty years of her son was a terrible price for something You were already going to do.*

*You keep Your word, at the set time, without my help. Help me live inside that.*

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
