The first thing the Bible ever tells you about her is the thing that hurt her most. Before her story even starts, before she says a single word, Scripture hands you one sentence: *but Sarai was barren; she had no child*.

In her world, that sentence was a verdict. Children were how a family survived, how land stayed in the family, and how a woman was cared for when she grew old. A woman without children had no safety net and no standing, and everyone around her could count the years.

Then God promised her husband that his family would become a great nation. Which means God promised a nation to a man whose wife could not have one child.

And then God let twenty five years go by.

Sarah waited through her sixties. Her seventies. Her eighties. She waited so long that when the answer finally came close, she laughed at it from behind a tent door. Not the good kind of laugh. The kind that slips out when something sounds flat out impossible.

**Today we meet the woman who laughed at God, and the God who kept His promise anyway.**

## The Woman Introduced by Her

## Wound

Sarah starts her story with a different name. She is Sarai, married to a man named Abram, part of a family living in a city called Ur and later in Haran. Genesis introduces her in one quiet verse of a family list, and then immediately tells you her wound.

*“But Sarai was barren; she had no child.” (Genesis 11:30)*

That is the very first fact Scripture records about her. Not her beauty, which was famous. Not her words. Her empty arms. The Bible never hides the hard part of anyone's story, and it does not hide hers.

Then God called her husband to leave everything and go to a land He would show him, with a promise attached: *I will make of thee a great nation*. Sarai packed up and went with him. Hold those two things together. A great nation was promised to a household whose wife could not have children. From day one, her whole life sat inside one long test. Could she keep believing God while year after year nothing happened?

The road did not make it easier. A famine drove them down into Egypt, and on the way, her husband got afraid.

*“Say, I pray thee, thou art my sister: that it may be well with me for thy sake; and my soul shall live because of thee.” (Genesis 12:13)*

It was a half truth. She was his half sister, the daughter of his father but not his mother. But the point of saying it was to hide that she was his wife, and it put her in real danger. The Egyptians saw that she was *very fair*, and Pharaoh's men **took her into the king's house**. Scripture does not record one word from Sarai in that whole scene. She had no say over what happened to her. Her husband protected his own life with her, and she paid the price for it.

Now watch what God does. He did not shrug. He struck Pharaoh's house with plagues until the king found out the truth and sent them both away untouched. Years later Abraham pulled the same move with another king, Abimelech, and God stepped in again, warning the king in a dream before anyone touched her. Mark that down, because it is this book's thread already showing. Her own husband failed her twice in the same way. **God guarded her both times.** How people treated her was not how God regarded her.

## The Plan That Made

## Everything Worse

Ten years passed in Canaan. Still no child. And Sarai got tired of waiting.

*“And Sarai said unto Abram, Behold now, the LORD hath restrained me from bearing: I pray thee, go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her. And Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai.” (Genesis 16:2)*

Her maid was an Egyptian servant named Hagar. In that culture, giving a servant to a husband so the child would legally count as the wife's own was an accepted custom. But hear this clearly. It was a custom people invented. **It was never something God commanded.** It was what waiting people reach for when they stop trusting the timeline.

The plan worked, and it broke the house. Hagar conceived, and the two women began to despise each other. Sarai *dealt hardly* with her, hard enough that a pregnant servant chose the open desert over her roof. We are not going to clean that up. Sarah caused real suffering to a woman with less power than her, and the pain from this one decision followed the family for decades. Tomorrow we will walk through this same story again, standing in Hagar's sandals, because Hagar gets her own day in this book.

For today, see what the scheme actually bought Sarai. A son was born, Ishmael, and he was not the promised one. She had tried to build God's promise with her own hands, and all she built was a house full of hurt. God's promises do not need our shortcuts. When we force them, we usually just multiply the pain while we wait.

## The Laugh Behind the Tent

## Door

Thirteen more years went by. Then God spoke again, and this time He got specific about her.

*“And God said unto Abraham, As for Sarai thy wife, thou shalt not call her name Sarai, but Sarah shall her name be. And I will bless her, and give thee a son also of her: yea, I will bless her, and she shall be a mother of nations; kings of people shall be of her.” (Genesis 17:15–16)*

Notice what God did not say. He did not say the plan with Hagar would do. He did not tell Abraham to find another workaround. He said **Sarah herself** would have a son. A new name came with the promise: mother of nations. God was not going to work around this woman. He was going to work through her.

Not long after, three visitors arrived at the tent in the heat of the day, and one of them spoke as the LORD. He asked for Sarah by name, and then He said the son would come within the year. Sarah was listening at the tent door behind Him. She was about ninety years old, and her body had long since closed the door on children. The text is plain and polite about it at the same time.

*“Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?” (Genesis 18:12)*

She laughed. Quietly, inside herself, behind a door. And be fair to her before you judge her. She had prayed for one thing her whole married life, and a voice on the other side of a tent flap just promised it at ninety. Of course it slipped out. It is the laugh that escapes when something sounds too good to be real.

God heard it anyway, and His answer is the center of her whole story: *Wherefore did Sarah laugh? Is any thing too hard for the LORD?* Sarah got scared and denied it. *I laughed not.* And God answered her the way He answers frightened people, with truth and without crushing her: *Nay; but thou didst laugh.* No lightning. No lecture. He corrected the lie, restated the promise, and kept His word anyway. **He was patient with her doubt, and He is patient with yours.**

## The Laughter God Gave Back

One year later, the impossible happened on schedule.

*“And the LORD visited Sarah as he had said, and the LORD did unto Sarah as he had spoken. For Sarah conceived, and bare Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him.” (Genesis 21:1–2)*

As he had said. As he had spoken. At the set time. Three times in two verses, the text hammers the same nail. God did exactly what He promised, exactly when He appointed. The boy was named Isaac, which means **he laughs**. Every time anyone said her son's name for the rest of his life, it echoed the promise she once thought was impossible.

*“And Sarah said, God hath made me to laugh, so that all that hear will laugh with me.” (Genesis 21:6)*

Look at what happened to her laugh. In the tent it was the laugh of disbelief. At ninety, holding her own son, it became the laugh of pure joy. God did not punish her for the first laugh. He replaced it with the second one.

Her story stays honest to the end. The old wound with Hagar tore open again, and Sarah demanded that Hagar and her son be sent away, a hard scene we will sit with properly tomorrow. And then Genesis gives her one last distinction. She lived to a hundred and twenty seven, and she is the only woman in the whole Bible whose exact age at death is recorded. Abraham wept for her, and bought the cave of Machpelah as a permanent resting place in the promised land. The first piece of that land his family ever owned was bought because of her.

She never saw the nations. She never saw the kings. She held exactly one baby of promise, and she died in faith with the rest still on the way. The New Testament settles how heaven remembers her: *through faith also Sara herself received strength to conceive seed... because she judged him faithful who had promised*. The laughing, the fear, the bad plan with Hagar, all of it included, God still calls her a woman of faith. He did not need her to feel confident. He needed her to keep walking with Him through the doubt, and she did.

Tomorrow we walk back into this same household through a different door. The servant girl Sarah handed to her husband had a name, a voice, and a God who met her in the wilderness. Her name is Hagar. ## Today's Prayer

*Father,*

*You made Sarah a promise, and then You let twenty five years go by. I want to be honest with You. I do not understand Your timing either. I have waited on things so long that the waiting turned into quiet laughter, the kind Sarah laughed behind the tent door.*

*Thank You that You heard her laugh and kept Your promise anyway. Thank You that You did not strike her down for doubting. You asked her a question instead, and You have been asking it ever since.*

**Is anything too hard for You, Lord? Help me live like the answer is no.**

*Forgive me for my own Ishmaels, the plans I built with my own hands because Your calendar felt too slow. And forgive me for the people my impatience has stepped on while I waited. Guard them from me, and soften me toward them, the way You guarded Hagar.*

*Teach me to measure Your promises by You, and not by my age, my strength, my bank account, or my history. You are the God who shows up at the set time, exactly as You said.*

*And when the answer finally comes, give me Sarah's second laugh. The one You made. The one everybody who hears it laughs along with.*

**In Jesus’ name, amen.**
