## Day 8 - The Judgment of Sodom

Genesis 18 through 19 brings us into one of the heaviest days so far in the Bible in One Year journey.

This day is not only about judgment.

It is about promise.

It is about prayer.

It is about mercy.

It is about rescue.

And it is about the warning that compromise can pull a person closer and closer to a place they were never meant to live.

Day 7 ended with covenant clarity. Abram became Abraham. Sarai became Sarah. Isaac was named before he was born. God marked the covenant and made it clear that the promise would continue by His faithfulness.

Now Day 8 opens with Abraham by his tent.

He is not in a palace. He is not standing in a temple. He is sitting near the oaks of Mamre in the heat of the day.

And the Lord appears to him.

Genesis 18 begins with a holy encounter in an ordinary place.

Abraham sees three visitors nearby, and he responds immediately. He runs to meet them. He bows. He offers water. He offers rest. He prepares bread, meat, milk, and curds.

At first, the scene looks like hospitality. And it is hospitality.

But it is more than that.

Abraham is making room for a holy moment before he fully understands what is happening.

That matters because sometimes God meets people in ordinary spaces. A tent. A meal. A conversation. A moment that looks normal on the outside, but heaven is drawing near.

Then the visitors ask a question.

Where is Sarah?

That question matters.

The promise is not only about Abraham. Sarah is part of this covenant story too.

God has already promised a son. God has already said that Sarah will bear Isaac. But now the promise comes close again. The visitor says that at the appointed time, Sarah will have a son.

Sarah hears this from the tent.

And Sarah laughs within herself.

That laugh is complicated. It is not just comedy. It is the laugh of long waiting. It is the laugh of disappointment. It is the laugh of a body that knows its age. It is the laugh of someone who has heard a promise, but everything around her still looks impossible.

Then the Lord asks the question that holds the whole scene together.

Is anything too hard for the Lord?

That is not only a question for Sarah.

It is a question for the reader.

It is a question for Abraham's household.

It is a question for every person who has looked at God's promise and then looked at their own weakness.

Is anything too hard for the Lord?

Sarah's laugh is not hidden from God. God hears the laugh she keeps inside. But He does not ask the question to crush her. He asks it to lift her eyes.

Humanly speaking, Isaac is impossible.

But the covenant promise does not depend on human possibility.

It depends on the Lord.

So Genesis 18 begins with promise. A tent becomes a place of visitation. Sarah's hidden laugh becomes the place where God speaks power. The promised son is still coming.

But then the story turns.

The visitors rise and look toward Sodom.

That turn is heavy.

In Genesis 13, Lot lifted up his eyes and chose the well-watered plain. He moved near Sodom. Later, Genesis shows him living in Sodom. Now, in Genesis 19, he is sitting in the gate of Sodom.

That means Lot is not only near the city anymore. He is connected to its public life.

The direction of Lot's life has been moving step by step toward a place that is deeply broken.

Before Genesis 19 shows us the city, Genesis 18 shows us God's justice.

The Lord says the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and their sin is very grievous.

That language matters.

God is not judging because of rumor. He is not reacting carelessly. He is not moved by impatience or ignorance.

He hears the cry.

He knows the evil.

He sees the victims.

God's justice is never blind.

That is important because judgment can be hard for us to read. Genesis does not ask us to treat judgment lightly. But it also does not present God as reckless. The Judge of all the earth knows exactly what He is doing.

Then Abraham stands before the Lord.

This is one of the great intercession scenes in Genesis.

Abraham draws near and asks, Will you destroy the righteous with the wicked?

Then he says, Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?

Notice Abraham's posture.

He is humble, but he is bold.

He knows he is dust and ashes, but he still comes near.

He does not pretend Sodom is innocent. He does not say evil does not matter. But he pleads for mercy.

What if there are fifty righteous people?

What if there are forty-five?

What if there are forty?

What if there are thirty?

What if there are twenty?

What if there are ten?

Abraham keeps asking, and the Lord keeps answering.

This teaches us something about prayer.

Prayer is not pretending evil is small.

Prayer is bringing real evil before a real God and asking for mercy without denying justice.

Abraham's closeness with God makes him serious. He is serious about righteousness. Serious about judgment. Serious about mercy. Serious about people.

That is what prayer does when it is shaped by God's heart.

Then Genesis 19 begins.

The two angels come to Sodom in the evening, and Lot is sitting in the gate.

Lot welcomes them into his house, but the city quickly reveals itself.

The men of Sodom surround the house. The scene is violent, corrupt, and terrifying. It shows why the cry against Sodom was great.

Genesis is not giving us random judgment. It is exposing a city that has become deeply wicked.

And Lot's position is tragic.

Lot tries to protect the visitors, but his own judgment has been badly shaped by the place where he lives. His words and choices in this scene are painful to read. The city has not only surrounded his house. It has shaped his thinking.

That is one of the warnings of Genesis 19.

Compromise is rarely instant.

Lot did not begin in the gate of Sodom. He moved toward it. He settled near it. He lived in it. He became known in it. And by the time judgment comes, he is tangled up in a place he cannot heal and cannot control.

The angels strike the men with blindness and tell Lot to gather his family and leave.

Lot warns his sons-in-law, but they think he is joking.

That detail is painful.

When warning finally comes, his family does not take him seriously.

Morning comes, and Lot still lingers.

This is one of the most merciful and heartbreaking moments in the passage.

Lot knows judgment is coming.

The angels have warned him.

The city is not safe.

But he lingers.

So the angels take him by the hand.

They take his wife by the hand.

They take his daughters by the hand.

And Genesis tells us why.

Because the Lord was merciful to him.

That sentence is stunning.

Lot is not rescued because he is impressive in this moment.

He is not decisive.

He is not strong.

He is not quick to obey.

He is lingering.

And mercy grabs his hand.

Sometimes the mercy of God does not only invite us.

Sometimes mercy pulls.

Sometimes mercy interrupts.

Sometimes mercy drags us away from what we are still too attached to.

Lot's rescue is not clean and heroic. It is mercy.

Then judgment falls.

Lot reaches Zoar, and the Lord rains fire and brimstone on Sodom and Gomorrah.

The cities are overthrown.

The plain is overthrown.

The wickedness is judged.

And Lot's wife looks back.

That moment becomes a warning.

She has been brought out physically, but her heart turns back toward the place being judged.

Genesis does not give us every detail of her inner life, but the warning is clear. Rescue is not only about leaving a place with your feet. The heart must leave too.

Then Genesis says something very important.

God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out of the overthrow.

That means Abraham's intercession mattered.

It also means Lot's rescue was tied to mercy bigger than Lot's own strength.

Judgment is real in Genesis 19.

But mercy is real too.

God does not ignore evil.

And God knows how to rescue.

The final section of Genesis 19 is disturbing. Lot leaves Zoar and lives in a cave with his daughters. The family story ends in fear, confusion, and deep brokenness.

This ending is meant to sober us.

Lot escaped Sodom, but Sodom's damage still cast a long shadow.

Leaving a place physically does not always mean its patterns have left the heart.

The story of Moab and Ammon begins here, and those names will matter later in the Bible.

Genesis is showing us that compromise can leave wounds beyond the moment of rescue.

So what do we carry from Day 8?

First, God's promise is stronger than impossibility.

Sarah laughs because the promise sounds impossible, but God asks, Is anything too hard for the Lord?

The answer is no.

Second, God's justice is never careless.

The Lord hears the cry of Sodom. He knows the truth. He judges evil with full knowledge.

Third, prayer matters.

Abraham stands before the Lord and pleads for mercy. His prayer does not erase justice, but it participates in the mercy of God.

Fourth, compromise is dangerous.

Lot's story warns us that moving toward what looks good by sight can slowly place us inside what damages the soul.

Fifth, mercy can pull us out.

Lot lingered, but the Lord was merciful to him.

That is hope for people who know they have hesitated too long.

Day 8 is heavy, but it is not hopeless.

The same chapters that show judgment also show promise.

The same chapters that show violence also show intercession.

The same chapters that show Lot lingering also show mercy taking him by the hand.

And the question still stands over the whole day.

Is anything too hard for the Lord?

No.

Not the birth of Isaac.

Not the rescue of Lot.

Not the preservation of the promise.

Not the mercy needed to pull someone out.

Genesis 18 and 19 teach us to take evil seriously, to take prayer seriously, to take compromise seriously, and to take God's mercy seriously.

The Lord keeps His promise.

The Lord does right.

The Lord hears.

The Lord judges.

And the Lord rescues with mercy.
